## THE THREE POSITIONS STATED PRECISELY

### Popper (Karl Popper, 1902-1994)

```
CORE CLAIM:
  Science is the pursuit of truth through
  the elimination of falsehoods.

  A theory is scientific if and only if
  it makes predictions that could, in
  principle, be proven wrong by observation.
  This is the FALSIFIABILITY criterion —
  the demarcation between science and
  non-science.

  Scientific method:
    1. Propose a bold conjecture.
    2. Derive its predictions.
    3. Test the predictions rigorously.
    4. If a prediction fails: falsified.
       Discard the theory.
    5. If predictions survive: corroborated.
       Tentatively keep the theory.
    6. Never stop trying to falsify it.

  Science progresses by eliminating what
  is false. It asymptotically approaches
  truth by narrowing the space of
  possible theories. No theory is ever
  proven true. Every theory is either
  falsified or tentatively surviving.

  The method is UNIVERSAL.
  The same logic applies in all fields,
  at all times, in all cultures.
  There is one scientific method.

  Popper's key texts:
    The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)
    Conjectures and Refutations (1963)
```

### Kuhn (Thomas Kuhn, 1922-1996)

```
CORE CLAIM:
  Science does not work the way Popper
  describes, in practice or in history.

  Science operates within PARADIGMS —
  shared frameworks of assumptions,
  methods, vocabulary, standards,
  and exemplary problems that define
  what counts as legitimate science,
  valid evidence, and acceptable answers.

  Under a paradigm, scientists do
  NORMAL SCIENCE — they solve puzzles
  that the paradigm defines as solvable.
  They do not question the paradigm.
  Anomalies are typically set aside,
  explained away, or attributed to
  experimental error.

  When anomalies accumulate to crisis
  level, a PARADIGM SHIFT may occur.
  The old paradigm is replaced by a
  new one.

  The critical claim: INCOMMENSURABILITY.
  Successive paradigms cannot be directly
  compared using a common, neutral
  standard. The concepts, vocabulary,
  and criteria of one paradigm do not
  fully translate into the next.

  You cannot evaluate a paradigm from
  within the paradigm itself.
  The paradigm defines what counts
  as evidence, method, and success.
  Using those criteria to evaluate
  the paradigm is circular.

  Paradigm choice involves rational
  factors but also sociological,
  psychological, and historical ones.
  It is not purely rational.

  Kuhn's key text:
    The Structure of Scientific Revolutions
    (1962)
```

### Gödel (Kurt Gödel, 1906-1978)

```
CORE CLAIM (First Incompleteness Theorem):
  Any consistent formal system
  that is sufficiently expressive
  (can express arithmetic) contains
  true statements that cannot be proven
  within the system.

CORE CLAIM (Second Incompleteness Theorem):
  Such a system cannot prove its own
  consistency from within itself.

  To prove the completeness or consistency
  of System S, you need to step outside S
  into a meta-system S'.
  But S' has its own unprovable truths,
  requiring S''.
  And so on.

  THE GEOMETRIC CONSEQUENCE:
  Every sufficiently complex system has
  truths about itself that are inaccessible
  from within.
  The view from inside the system is
  always incomplete.
  Completeness requires a perspective
  outside the system.

  Gödel's theorem is a mathematical proof
  of what Kuhn described sociologically
  about science:
  You cannot fully evaluate a system
  (a paradigm, a formal theory) using
  only the resources of that system.

  Gödel's key work:
    On Formally Undecidable Propositions
    of Principia Mathematica and Related
    Systems (1931)
```

```

```
