## 1. THE FRAMEWORK

The attractor geometry framework:

    S + N + G → R

S = structural substrate (genome and encoded
    build capacity)
N = coherence gradient field (the external
    field specifying which attractor basin
    is occupied)
G = developmental geometry (the global
    attractor landscape, basin structure,
    energy barriers between basins)
R = resulting phenotype

The Sus scrofa domestication paper established
the clean single-mechanism case: N_domestic
arrests NCC migration via EZH2/H3K27me3.
S is unmodified. G is unchanged. Remove N
and the system recovers to Basin A.

The canine reasoning artifact established that
above a genomic fixation threshold, G itself
is remodelled. Basin A becomes inaccessible.
A third basin (Basin C) emerges. The coupled
social attractor (Basin B*) is maintained
by a bidirectional inter-species field.

This document establishes the third class:
the symmetric case, in which G is minimally
modified, both basins remain deep and
accessible, both barriers are low, and the
organism navigates between basins under its
own motivational field.

The three cases together define a complete
taxonomy of domestication geometry.

## 3. THE FELINE LANDSCAPE IN DETAIL

### 3.1 Why The Landscape Is Symmetric

Cat domestication involved selection at
approximately 13 genes (Montague et al.,
2014, PNAS). Every one of these genes is
behavioural — fear response, memory,
reward processing. Not one is morphological.

Compare:
- Sus scrofa domestication: epigenetic
  changes at NCC migration loci. Morphology
  changes. Genome unchanged.
- Canis lupus familiaris domestication:
  hundreds of fixed alleles including
  craniofacial, body size, and social
  cognition architecture loci. Morphology
  changes dramatically. Genome remodelled.
- Felis catus domestication: 13 behavioural
  genes. Morphology essentially unchanged.
  Genome minimally modified.

The domestic cat looks almost identical to
the Near Eastern wildcat (Felis silvestris
lybica). Same body size. Same skull shape.
Same coat pattern (tabby is the ancestral
wildcat pattern). The only observable
differences are coat colour variants that
arose under human proximity and mild
behavioural differences in fear threshold.

Geometrically: if S is almost unchanged
and G encodes the landscape from S, then
G is almost unchanged. Basin A (wild)
remains fully specified in the domestic
cat genome. Basin B (domestic) was
accessed not by suppressing Basin A but
by lowering the energy barrier between
the two basins at the fear/reward gate.

The cat's landscape was not reshaped.
The gate between the two basins was
widened slightly. Both basins remain
fully intact. Both remain accessible.

### 3.2 The Fear/Reward Gate

The specific mechanism of the threshold
lowering is identifiable from Montague's
13 selected genes.

The selected genes cluster in:
- Fear response circuitry (amygdala,
  HPA axis reactivity)
- Memory and associative learning
  (capacity to form positive associations
  with specific individuals)
- Reward processing (dopaminergic pathways
  mediating approach behaviour)

What this means functionally: the domestic
cat's fear threshold toward humans is lower
than the wildcat's. The reward signal for
human proximity is higher. This lowers the
energy barrier at the gate between Basin A
and Basin B in the direction A→B.

Critically: it does NOT raise the barrier
in the direction B→A. The domestic cat
retains the full wildcat survival programme.
It retains the complete predator behavioural
repertoire. It retains normal NCC-derived
morphology. It retains normal adrenal gland
size and HPA axis reactivity (unlike domestic
dogs and pigs, which show reduced adrenal
size as a domestication syndrome marker).

The gate is lower in one direction.
Everything else is intact.
Both basins remain accessible.
The result is a symmetric landscape with
a slightly asymmetric gate (easier to enter
B from A than in the wildcat, but not
locked into B once entered).

### 3.3 The Motivational Field

Because the barriers in both directions
are low, the factor determining which basin
the animal occupies at any given moment
is not the external field N but the animal's
own internal motivational state.

Internal motivational state in this context
means: current HPA axis activation level,
current reward signal from the environment,
current fear/threat assessment.

Low threat + high human reward signal
→ Basin B occupation (affiliative, domestic
  behaviour toward specific humans)

High threat + low human reward signal
→ Basin A occupation (predatory, territorial,
  independent behaviour)

The animal crosses the barrier in either
direction based on its own internal field.
The external field N_domestic can increase
the probability of Basin B occupation by
providing a consistently low-threat, high-
reward environment. But it cannot lock the
animal in Basin B. As soon as the internal
motivational state shifts, the animal
crosses back.

This is volitional basin navigation.
Not conscious deliberation in the human
sense. But motivationally driven, context-
dependent, individual-level basin switching
that operates faster than any other
domesticated species — within the lifespan
of a single individual, within a single
day if conditions change.

### 3.4 Simultaneous Partial Basin Occupation

The most striking prediction of the symmetric
low-barrier landscape is that the animal can
partially occupy both basins simultaneously.

In a high-barrier landscape (dog, pig),
the animal is in one basin or the other.
The barriers are high enough that crossing
requires sustained energy input over time.
Simultaneous occupation of both basins
is not possible.

In a low-barrier landscape (cat), the
barriers are low enough that the animal
can be in a superposition — expressing
Basin A behaviours (predation, territory,
independence) while retaining Basin B
access (recognition of specific humans,
affiliative behaviour toward specific
individuals) without having to fully exit
Basin A to do so.

This is confirmed by Christian the Lion.
The lion was:
- Established in wild territory (Basin A)
- Part of a wild pride (Basin A)
- Functioning as a wild predator (Basin A)
- AND recognised and greeted specific
  humans affectionately (Basin B access)

Simultaneously. Without crossing fully
into Basin B. Without losing Basin A
occupation.

This is not possible in the dog or pig
landscape. It requires the symmetric
low-barrier topology unique to Felidae.

## 4. THE SCALE INVARIANCE ACROSS FELIDAE

### 4.1 The Claim

The symmetric low-barrier dual-basin
landscape is a conserved property of
the Felidae genome, not something created
by domestication of house cats.

Domestication of house cats slightly
lowered the already-low barrier in one
direction. The underlying symmetric
topology was already present in the
ancestral Felidae genome.

This predicts that the same dual-basin
accessibility should be observable across
the entire Felidae family, scaled to the
ecological context of each species.

### 4.2 Confirmation Across The Family

House cat (Felis catus):
Basin switching within single individual
within days. Complete. Rapid. Documented
in every household with a cat.

Cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus):
Wild-caught adults tamed within weeks to
months. Multiple independent civilisations
confirmed this: ancient Egypt, Persia,
Mughal India. No selective breeding was
ever achieved — every tamed cheetah was
wild-caught. The wild genome contains
Basin B accessibility without any
domestication modification. The barrier
from Basin A to Basin B is crossable
by a wild-caught adult individual within
a single lifetime. No other large predator
achieves this reliably.

Leopard (Panthera pardus):
Sanctuary literature documents individual
variation in Basin B accessibility. Some
individuals approach specific human
caretakers and display affiliative behaviour
while retaining complete predator competence
in all other contexts. Simultaneous partial
occupation confirmed.

Lion (Panthera leo):
Christian the Lion (Rendall & Bourke / Adamson,
1969--1972). Wild release followed by
recognition and affiliative greeting of
specific humans one year post-release.
Basin A occupation (wild territory, pride
membership) with Basin B access (human
recognition, affiliative behaviour) confirmed
simultaneously in a single individual.

Tiger (Panthera tigris):
Sanctuary literature confirms individual
variation in Basin B accessibility. Low-
cortisol-reactivity individuals show
affiliative behaviour toward specific humans
while retaining full predator competence.

The pattern is present in every major
Felidae genus examined. The topology
is conserved. The geometry is a clade
property, not a domestication artefact.

### 4.3 Why The Ancestral Felidae Genome
### Encodes This Topology

The evolutionary basis for the symmetric
low-barrier landscape in Felidae is
derivable from their ecological niche.

Felids are obligate solitary ambush
predators. Unlike wolves (cooperative pack
hunters) and unlike pigs (herd prey
animals), felids operate primarily alone.

The solitary predator niche creates a
specific cognitive requirement: the ability
to rapidly assess whether an encountered
organism is a threat, prey, or a potential
conspecific social partner — and to switch
behavioural mode rapidly based on that
assessment.

A wolf encounters a novel organism and
applies pack social logic. A pig encounters
a novel organism and applies herd flight
logic. A cat encounters a novel organism
and performs a rapid individual assessment
and switches to the appropriate mode: hunt,
flee, affiliate, or ignore.

This rapid individual contextual switching
is the ecological antecedent of the low-
barrier basin topology. Natural selection
built a landscape where rapid Basin A ↔
Basin B switching is low-cost because
the solitary predator's survival depends
on contextual behavioural flexibility at
the individual level.

Domestication did not create the cat's
volitional basin navigation. It exploited
a pre-existing landscape topology that
natural selection had already built for
an entirely different ecological purpose.

## 5. THE UNMANAGEABILITY PREDICTION

### 5.1 Why Cats Are Unmanageable

The symmetric low-barrier landscape
produces a specific management prediction:

An organism in a symmetric low-barrier
landscape cannot be reliably locked into
either basin by external field application.

N_domestic can shift the probability
distribution of basin occupation. A high-
resource, low-threat domestic environment
increases the time the animal spends in
Basin B. But it cannot set the barrier
high enough to prevent Basin A re-entry
when the animal's internal motivational
state shifts.

This is confirmed by every cat owner's
experience and by every wildlife sanctuary's
operational reality.

The domestic cat:
- Will be a lap cat for three days
- Will then kill and not return for two days
- Will return and resume lap cat behaviour
- This is not inconsistency or defect
- This is the symmetric landscape operating
  correctly under shifting internal
  motivational states

The rescued big cat:
- Will approach and allow handling from
  a specific caretaker
- Will injure or kill a different human
  in the same hour
- This is not unpredictability in the
  sense of randomness
- This is the low barrier allowing rapid
  Basin A re-entry when the specific
  learned human-recognition signal is
  absent

### 5.2 The Specific Individual Recognition Point

The cat's Basin B occupation is
INDIVIDUAL-SPECIFIC, not SPECIES-GENERAL.

This is the critical distinction from dogs.

Dogs in Basin B* orient toward the human
species generally. The dog recognises
humans as the Basin B maintenance field.
Any human can potentially maintain the
coupled field.

Cats in Basin B orient toward SPECIFIC
individuals with whom the associative
learning has been established. The Basin B
access is gated by individual recognition,
not species recognition.

This is why:
- A tame lion will greet its specific
  human keepers and attack an unfamiliar
  human in the same hour
- A domestic cat will be affectionate
  with its specific household members
  and feral toward strangers
- Cheetahs in Mughal India were tamed
  to specific handlers and could not
  be transferred to new handlers without
  re-taming

The fear/reward threshold genes selected
during cat domestication are in the memory
and associative learning pathway, not in
the general social orientation pathway.
Cats learned to form positive associations
with specific individuals. They did not
learn to generalise positive orientation
to the human species.

Dogs generalise. Cats individualise.
This is the molecular consequence of
which 13 genes were selected during
cat domestication vs. which hundreds
of genes were fixed during dog
domestication.

DERIVED: Cat genome minimally modified,

behavioural. Not one is morphological.

identical to Near Eastern wildcats.
Described in the literature as
"semi-domesticated" or "only partially
domesticated at the genetic level."
SOURCE: Montague et al. (2014, PNAS).

DERIVED: The cat is the agent. The cat

DERIVED: A lion can be in Basin A (wild)

DERIVED: Cat Basin B access is gated by

DERIVED: The same individual cat switches

## 7. THE NOVEL PREDICTIONS

The following predictions are derived from
this geometric analysis and stated here
before any experimental data is generated
and before any literature search on these
specific predictions has been performed.

### P-FELINE-1: H3K27me3 at NCC Loci
### Absent in Domestic Cat vs. Wildcat Embryos

PREDICTION:
H3K27me3 ChIP-seq on embryonic neural tube
tissue from domestic cat embryos vs. Near
Eastern wildcat embryos at equivalent
developmental stage will show NO significant
differential enrichment at the pre-registered
NCC migration driver loci (BMP4, BMP7, WNT1,
WNT3A, CXCL12, CXCR4, EDNRB, SOX10, SNAI2).

RATIONALE:
Cat domestication did not proceed via NCC
migration arrest. The domestication syndrome
traits (floppy ears, depigmentation, shortened
snout, reduced adrenal gland) are ABSENT or
minimal in domestic cats compared to domestic
dogs and pigs. Domestic cats do not have
floppy ears as a breed characteristic. They
do not have substantially shortened snouts.
Adrenal gland size in domestic cats is not
significantly reduced relative to wildcats.

The absence of the morphological domestication
syndrome in cats is the phenotypic confirmation
that NCC migration was not arrested. NCC
migration arrest produces the syndrome. The
cat lacks the syndrome. Therefore the
mechanism is not NCC arrest. Therefore
H3K27me3 at NCC loci should be absent.

DISCRIMINATING VALUE:
This prediction discriminates Class 1
(Sus scrofa — H3K27me3 present at NCC loci)
from Class 2 (Felis catus — H3K27me3 absent
at NCC loci). Both in the same ChIP-seq
experiment design, same antibodies, same
analytical pipeline (P9 protocol), different
species.

### P-FELINE-2: The 13 Selected Genes Map
### to Fear/HPA Threshold, Not NCC Machinery

PREDICTION:
Pathway analysis of Montague's 13 selected
genes will show specific enrichment for:
- Amygdala fear circuitry genes
- HPA axis reactivity threshold genes
- Dopaminergic reward pathway genes
- Memory consolidation and associative
  learning genes

And specific absence of:
- NCC migration guidance genes
- NCC specification genes
- EMT/delamination genes
- BMP/WNT signalling pathway genes

RATIONALE:
If the mechanism is fear threshold lowering
rather than NCC arrest, the selected genes
must be in the fear/reward pathway, not the
NCC pathway. This is a specific, testable
claim about the identity of the 13 genes.

### P-FELINE-3: Domestication Syndrome
### Traits Absent in Domestic Cats Relative
### to Wildcat Controls

PREDICTION:
Morphometric comparison of domestic cat
skulls vs. Near Eastern wildcat skulls will
show no significant reduction in snout length,
no increase in cranial vault rounding, and no
reduction in brain size relative to body mass
— the standard domestication syndrome
morphological markers.

Adrenal gland mass normalised to body weight
will not differ significantly between domestic
cats and Near Eastern wildcats at equivalent
age and sex.

RATIONALE:
This is the phenotypic confirmation that NCC
migration was not arrested in cat domestication.
Wilkins et al. (2014) established that all
domestication syndrome morphological traits
are NCC-derived. If cats lack the morphological
syndrome, cats lack NCC arrest. If cats lack
NCC arrest, Class 2 (threshold lowering) is
confirmed as the correct mechanism class for
Felidae.

### P-FELINE-4: Basin Switching Speed in
### Domestic Cats Scales With Individual
### Cortisol Reactivity, Not Breed

PREDICTION:
Individual domestic cats measured for cortisol
stress response (standardised stressor protocol,
equivalent to P7 design) will show cortisol
reactivity that predicts Basin B dwell time:
Low cortisol reactor → more time in Basin B
                       (affiliative, domestic
                       behaviour toward humans)
High cortisol reactor → more time in Basin A
                        (independent, predatory,
                        avoidant behaviour)

This relationship will be present WITHIN
breeds as well as across breeds, and will
show greater predictive power than breed
identity alone.

RATIONALE:
If the fear/reward threshold is the gate
between the basins, and individual cortisol
reactivity is the measure of that threshold,
then cortisol reactivity should predict
basin dwell time. This is an individual-level
prediction that does not require group
comparisons or different fields. It requires
only within-population cortisol measurement
correlated with behavioural basin occupancy
scoring.

### P-FELINE-5: Wild Felid Basin B Access
### Scales With Individual HPA Reactivity,
### Not Captivity Duration

PREDICTION:
Across a sanctuary population of wild-born
large felids (lions, tigers, or cheetahs)
with varying captivity durations, individual
HPA axis reactivity (measured by cortisol
response to standardised stressor) will be
a stronger predictor of affiliative behaviour
toward specific human caretakers (Basin B
access) than captivity duration or age at
capture.

RATIONALE:
If Basin B access is gated by the fear
threshold and the fear threshold is an
individual genomic property within the
low-barrier Felidae landscape, then the
low-cortisol-reactivity individuals should
show Basin B access regardless of how long
they have been in captivity. High-cortisol-
reactivity individuals should remain in
Basin A regardless of captivity duration.

This prediction distinguishes the geometric
model (individual threshold determines access)
from a learning model (captivity duration
and positive reinforcement determine access).

### P-FELINE-6: Cheetah Genome Encodes
### Basin B Accessibility Without Domestic
### Allele Modification

PREDICTION:
Genome sequencing of cheetah (Acinonyx
jubatus) at the orthologues of Montague's
13 domestic cat selected genes will show
that cheetahs already carry alleles
conferring low fear threshold and high
reward-pathway sensitivity in the wild
genome, without any human-imposed
selective pressure.

RATIONALE:
Cheetahs have been tamed from wild-caught
adults for 5,000 years without any
captive breeding programme. This means the
wild cheetah genome already encodes Basin B
accessibility. The alleles that lower the
gate are present in wild cheetahs without
human selection. This would confirm that
the symmetric low-barrier topology is
an ancestral Felidae property that predates
domestic cat domestication — exactly as the
framework predicts.

INTERMEDIATE / PLASTIC CANCER STATE

The cell is not locked. It is navigating
under microenvironmental signals.

lock the cell in the identity basin.
