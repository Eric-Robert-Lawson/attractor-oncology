## 1. THE FRAMEWORK

The attractor geometry framework:

    S + N + G → R

S = structural substrate (genome and encoded
    build capacity)
N = coherence gradient field (the external
    field specifying which attractor basin
    is occupied)
G = developmental geometry (the global
    attractor landscape, basin structure,
    energy barriers between basins)
R = resulting phenotype

The Sus scrofa domestication paper established
that the domestic phenotype is produced by
N_domestic arresting neural crest migration
at the juvenile-proximate basin. The genome
(S) is not modified. The field (N) changes.
The landscape (G) is fixed by the genome.
The phenotype (R) changes.

This is the clean single-mechanism case.

The canine case extends this framework into
a regime where:

1. G has been modified by genomic fixation
   over 15,000--40,000 years of domestication
2. N is not purely external — it is co-
   constructed by the coupled dog-human
   social system
3. The landscape contains three basins rather
   than two

These extensions do not contradict the framework.
They reveal its full generative capacity.

## 2. THE SUS SCROFA LANDSCAPE (REFERENCE CASE)

### 2.1 Structure

```
BASIN A                    BASIN B
(Wild Boar)                (Domestic Pig)

Depth:    VERY DEEP        Depth:    SHALLOW
Field:    N_wild           Field:    N_domestic
Genome:   Unmodified       Genome:   Unmodified
NCC:      Full migration   NCC:      Arrested
          complete                   (juvenile-
                                     proximate)

Energy barrier A→B:  HIGH
  (many generations of sustained N_domestic)

Energy barrier B→A:  LOW
  (remove N_domestic; system exits B immediately)
```

### 2.2 Why Basin A Is Deep

Basin A in Sus scrofa is the product of
millions of years of natural selection as
a prey/omnivore organism in complex
ecological environments. The genome encodes
Basin A completely. Every allele required
for wild boar morphology and behaviour is
present in the domestic pig genome at near-
wild frequencies. The field-arrest model
predicts this explicitly: the genome was
never modified. Only the epigenetic state
of NCC migration driver loci was altered
by N_domestic.

Remove N_domestic → H3K27me3 at NCC loci
cleared → NCC migration resumes → wild-type
morphology emerges.

This is the cleanest possible attractor
geometry: one deep basin, one shallow basin,
one maintenance mechanism, fully reversible.

### 2.3 Why Feralization Is Fast

Basin A is deep and fully specified in the
genome. The energy barrier from B→A is low
because B is maintained only by epigenetic
silencing, not by genomic modification.
Remove the field. The epigenetic state
reverts. The developmental trajectory
completes. Feralization in months to one
generation.

## (THE THREE-BASIN CASE)

```

### 3.2 Basin A: Why It Becomes Inaccessible

In Sus scrofa, Basin A remains accessible
because the genome was not modified.
In Canis lupus familiaris, the genome
has been modified.

The specific modifications:

- BMP4 alleles affecting craniofacial
  development fixed in domestic dogs,
  absent from wolves
- IGF1 small-dog haplotype fixed in all
  small breeds, unknown in wolves
- CXCR4 variants fixed correlating with
  reduced aggression and increased social
  orientation toward humans
- Social cognition architecture modified:
  human-directed gaze, human social cue
  reading, oxytocin receptor system tuned
  for inter-species rather than
  intra-species bonding

These are not epigenetic changes.
These are allele frequency changes —
in some cases complete fixation.

This means the developmental geometry G
has been permanently modified.
Basin A (wolf morphology and behaviour)
requires a genome that encodes wolf-type
social cognition, craniofacial development,
and predator behaviour. Highly derived
domestic dog breeds do not carry this
genome. Basin A is not suppressed by
H3K27me3. It is absent from the landscape
because the alleles required to specify
it have been replaced.

The pig can feral because Basin A is still
in its genome.
The Labrador cannot become a wolf because
Basin A is not in its genome.

This is the genomic fixation threshold.
Below threshold: field-arrest model operates
cleanly, feralization possible.
Above threshold: landscape has been
remodelled, Basin A inaccessible, system
converges on Basin C under field removal.

### 3.3 Basin C: The Emergent Third Attractor

When N_domestic is removed from a canine
system that cannot access Basin A, the
system does not remain in Basin B* (which
requires the co-constructed human field)
or collapse to Basin A (which is partly
or fully inaccessible). It converges on
Basin C.

Basin C is confirmed by the worldwide
convergent morphology of feral and
primitive dog populations:

- Indian pariah dog
- Australian dingo
- African village dog
- New Guinea singing dog
- Carolina dog (American dingo)
- Street dogs of South America,
  Eastern Europe, Southeast Asia

All converge on the same phenotype
regardless of breed starting point:
- Medium body size (15--25 kg)
- Wedge-shaped skull
- Erect or semi-erect ears
- Short coat
- Tan/sandy colouration
- Curled or upright tail
- Pack social structure — loose,
  hierarchical, semi-territorial

This is not random drift. It is
convergence to a stable attractor.
Basin C is real, it has a specific
morphological and behavioural signature,
and it is accessible from most domestic
dog genomes even when Basin A is not.

Basin C is the scavenger/generalist
social canid attractor. It emerged as
a stable basin because it is adaptive
for the ecological niche that dogs have
occupied at the edge of human settlements
for 15,000 years. It is not wolf. It is
not domestic dog. It is a third phenotype
with its own deep history.

### 3.4 Basin B*: The Co-Constructed Attractor

This is the most important structural
novelty in the canine landscape compared
to all other domesticated species.

In Sus scrofa, Basin B is maintained by
N_domestic applied externally. The pig
is passive. The field acts on the pig.
Remove the field and the pig exits Basin B.

In Canis lupus familiaris, Basin B* is
maintained by a COUPLED field — the
joint field generated by the dog-human
dyad together. Neither organism alone
maintains the field. Both organisms
contribute to it simultaneously.

The mechanism of this coupling is the
oxytocin gaze loop (Nagasawa et al.,
2015, Science):

1. Dog makes sustained eye contact
   with human
2. Human oxytocin rises
3. Human social bonding behaviour
   toward dog increases
4. Dog oxytocin rises in response
5. Dog social orientation toward
   human increases
6. Loop reinforces itself

This loop is absent in wolves raised
identically to dogs with identical
human exposure. It is unique to
Canis lupus familiaris. It was not
present in the ancestor. It was built
during domestication.

Geometrically: the co-evolutionary
process did not just place dogs in
Basin B. It constructed a new kind
of basin — one that is maintained by
a self-reinforcing inter-species field
coupling that gets stronger the longer
it operates.

Basin B* is deeper than Basin B
(pig domestic) in one specific sense:
the maintenance mechanism is internal
to the coupled system, not external.
You cannot remove the field that
maintains Basin B* by removing
N_domestic alone because the dog
itself generates part of the field.

This is why highly domesticated dogs
do not feral cleanly. When the human
component of the coupled field is
removed, the dog is in a state where:
- Basin B* is destabilised (missing
  half of its maintenance mechanism)
- Basin A is inaccessible (genomic
  fixation threshold exceeded)
- Basin C requires behavioural
  reorganisation that is stressful
  and energetically costly for
  highly derived breeds

The predicted outcome is stress
pathology during the transition, not
smooth feralization. This is exactly
what is observed in abandoned highly
derived domestic breeds placed in
feral conditions.

## 5. LITERATURE CONFIRMATIONS

The following were derived before literature
consultation and confirmed after:

### 5.1 Feralization rate asymmetry
DERIVED: Pigs feral fast and completely.
Dogs feral slowly and incompletely.
CONFIRMED: Feral pigs revert to near-wild-
boar morphology in 3--5 generations.
Feral dogs do not revert to wolf morphology
in any documented timeframe.
SOURCE: Coppinger & Coppinger (2001);
Price (1999); comparative feralization
literature.

### 5.2 Basin C convergence
DERIVED: Feral dogs converge on a third
attractor, not Basin A (wolf) or Basin B
(domestic dog).
CONFIRMED: Worldwide convergence of feral
and primitive dog populations on the pariah
dog phenotype (medium size, wedge head,
erect ears, tan coat) regardless of breed
starting point.
SOURCE: Bhadra et al. (2016); dingo
morphology literature; Indian pariah dog
studies.

### 5.3 Genomic fixation in dogs
DERIVED: The canine genome has been
modified, remodelling the landscape and
making Basin A inaccessible.
CONFIRMED: Fixed alleles at BMP4, IGF1,
CXCR4, and social cognition loci in
domestic dogs, absent from or rare in
wolves. These represent permanent genomic
changes, not epigenetic states.
SOURCE: Freedman et al. (2016);
Lindblad-Toh et al. (2005); dog
domestication genomics literature.

### 5.4 Co-constructed social attractor
DERIVED: The canine domestication basin
is maintained by a coupled inter-species
field, not by an external field acting
on a passive organism.
CONFIRMED: The oxytocin gaze loop is
unique to domestic dogs among all canids.
Socialised wolves raised identically do
not develop it. It is bidirectional —
both dog and human generate the field
that maintains the bond.
SOURCE: Nagasawa et al. (2015, Science).

### 5.5 Stress crisis in derived breeds
DERIVED: Highly derived domestic dogs
placed in feral conditions undergo stress
pathology rather than smooth feralization.
CONFIRMED: Highly selected breeds
abandoned in feral conditions show high
mortality, aberrant behaviour, and failure
to establish functional feral social
structures. The transition to Basin C is
costly and not always successful for
highly derived breeds.
SOURCE: Comparative feral dog literature;
Coppinger & Coppinger (2001).

### 5.6 Social gradient depth
DERIVED: The cognitive social depth of
canines is qualitatively greater than
pigs and has its own attractor geometry.
CONFIRMED: Dogs possess human-like social
skills (Hare & Tomasello, 2005) including
gaze following, pointing comprehension,
and human social cue reading at a level
not seen in any other non-human species
and not present in wolves with equivalent
socialisation.
SOURCE: Hare & Tomasello (2005);
Miklósi et al. (2003, 2004).

## 6. THE GENOMIC FIXATION THRESHOLD

This is the central theoretical contribution
of the canine derivation relative to the
pig derivation.

### 6.1 Definition

The genomic fixation threshold is the
point at which the degree of genomic
modification during domestication crosses
from:

BELOW THRESHOLD:
Epigenetic arrest only. Genome encodes
Basin A in full. Field removal allows
Basin A recovery. Landscape G is unchanged.
Sus scrofa is below threshold.

ABOVE THRESHOLD:
Genomic allele fixation has remodelled G.
Basin A is reduced or absent in the
landscape. Field removal does not allow
Basin A recovery. System converges on
Basin C or destabilises.
Derived Canis lupus familiaris is above
threshold for most breeds.

### 6.2 What Determines The Threshold

The threshold is crossed when the intensity
and duration of selection is sufficient
to fix alleles that:

1. Alter the developmental architecture
   (not just the epigenetic state)
2. Affect the specification of the wild-type
   attractor (Basin A) directly — i.e.,
   the alleles that the wild-type basin
   requires to exist in G are replaced

In Sus scrofa:
~10,000 years of domestication.
Selection primarily for HPA suppressibility,
fat deposition, fertility.
Core wild-type developmental architecture
intact. No alleles required for Basin A
have been replaced.

In Canis lupus familiaris:
~15,000--40,000 years of domestication.
Selection specifically for the inter-species
social cognition program — the very
architecture that defines the wolf
attractor in the first place.
Alleles required for wolf-type social
cognition, predator behaviour, and
craniofacial development have been
replaced by domestic-type fixed alleles.
Basin A requires those alleles. They are
gone. Basin A is inaccessible.

### 6.3 The Irony

The dog was domesticated by selecting
precisely the traits that defined it as
a wolf — its social depth, its capacity
for reading minds, its ability to bond
with a pack. These traits were redirected
from the wolf pack toward the human.

In doing so, the social depth that was
Basin A's deepest feature was not
eliminated. It was redirected. Basin B*
is as deep as Basin A was. But it points
in a different direction. The dog has as
much social capacity as the wolf. It is
simply directed inter-species rather than
intra-species.

The genomic fixation that made Basin A
inaccessible simultaneously deepened
Basin B*. The same alleles that prevent
wolf morphology from re-emerging also
maintain the human-oriented social
architecture that makes Basin B* self-
reinforcing.

This is the geometric consequence of
co-evolutionary domestication. The pig
was domesticated by suppressing its
wild program. The dog was domesticated
by redirecting its deepest program.
Suppression is reversible. Redirection
is not.

## 7. THE NOVEL PREDICTIONS

The following predictions are derived from
this geometric analysis. They are stated
here before any experimental data is
generated and before any literature search
on these specific predictions.

### P-CANINE-1: Basin C Convergence Rate
Scales With Breed Derivation Depth

Prediction: Feralization rate to Basin C
morphology (pariah phenotype) scales
negatively with breed genomic distance
from the primitive canid genome.

Primitive breeds (Basenji, Canaan Dog,
New Guinea Singing Dog) → fastest
convergence to Basin C under feral
conditions (fewest generations).
Derived breeds (Bulldog, Poodle, Pug) →
slowest convergence, highest stress
pathology, highest mortality in feral
conditions.

This gradient of convergence rate is a
direct prediction of the genomic fixation
threshold model. It is not predicted by
a simple learning/socialisation model
(which would predict equivalent
convergence rates across breeds given
equivalent feral exposure).

### P-CANINE-2: Oxytocin Loop Absence
in Primitive Breeds Is Intermediate

Prediction: The oxytocin gaze loop
(Nagasawa et al., 2015) shows a gradient
across canine breeds that tracks genomic
distance from wolf ancestry.

Primitive breeds (Basenji, Shiba Inu,
Dingo) → weaker oxytocin loop signal
than derived breeds (Golden Retriever,
Labrador).
Wolves (hand-raised) → oxytocin loop
absent (already confirmed).

This gradient maps the progressive
deepening of Basin B* across the canine
domestication spectrum. It has not been
tested across primitive vs. derived
breeds systematically.

### P-CANINE-3: H3K27me3 at Social
Cognition Loci — Absent in Dogs

Prediction: Unlike Sus scrofa, where the
domestication arrest is maintained by
H3K27me3 at NCC loci (P9), the canine
domestication is NOT primarily maintained
by H3K27me3 at equivalent loci. Instead,
the domestication is maintained by fixed
allele differences at social cognition
and craniofacial loci.

Test: H3K27me3 ChIP-seq at NCC migration
loci in domestic dog embryos vs. wolf
embryos at equivalent developmental stage.
Prediction: No differential H3K27me3 at
NCC loci between domestic dog and wolf
embryos. The mechanism in the dog is
genomic, not epigenetic.

This would establish the two regimes:
Sus scrofa = epigenetic arrest (below
genomic fixation threshold).
Canis lupus familiaris = genomic
remodelling (above genomic fixation
threshold).
Different mechanisms. Same framework.

### P-CANINE-4: Feral Stress Pathology
Scales With Basin B* Depth

Prediction: The degree of stress pathology
(cortisol dysregulation, behavioural
disorganisation, mortality) observed in
domestic dogs placed in feral conditions
scales with the depth of Basin B* in
that breed — operationalised as the
strength of the oxytocin gaze loop.

Breeds with stronger oxytocin loops
(deeper Basin B*) show more severe stress
pathology when the human coupling field
is removed, because the maintenance
mechanism for Basin B* is more deeply
embedded in the animal's physiology.

### P-CANINE-5: The Dingo Is Basin C,
Not Basin A

Prediction: The dingo genome, analysed
at the NCC migration loci and social
cognition loci identified in domestic
dog domestication, will show:

- Partial reversion toward wolf-type
  alleles at craniofacial loci (Basin C
  is morphologically intermediate)
- Retention of domestic dog-type alleles
  at social cognition loci (the dingo
  is not in Basin A — it retains the
  human-oriented social architecture
  in a reduced form)
- Absence of H3K27me3 enrichment at
  NCC loci compared to domestic dogs
  (consistent with partial genomic
  reversion not epigenetic arrest)

This would confirm that the dingo is
Basin C — a stable intermediate attractor
with its own distinct molecular signature,
not a partial return to Basin A.

## 9. RELATION TO CANCER BIOLOGY

The genomic fixation threshold has a
direct analogue in cancer biology.

Early-stage cancer (pre-invasive):
Attractor arrest is maintained primarily
by epigenetic mechanisms — EZH2/H3K27me3,
DNA methylation, histone modification.
The wild-type program is suppressed but
the underlying genome is intact.
Field perturbation (EZH2 inhibition,
demethylation) can de-arrest the cell.
Reversible. Analogous to Sus scrofa.

Late-stage cancer (genomically unstable,
highly aneuploid):
The cancer genome has been modified by
mutation accumulation, copy number
alteration, chromosomal rearrangement.
The wild-type program is no longer
encoded in the genome. The normal
identity attractor (Basin A) may no
longer exist in the genomic landscape
of a highly evolved cancer clone.
Epigenetic intervention alone cannot
restore a phenotype whose genomic
specification has been deleted.
Analogous to highly derived Canis
lupus familiaris.

The clinical implication:

Tazemetostat (EZH2 inhibitor) is
predicted to work in cancers that are
BELOW the genomic fixation threshold —
where the identity program is epigenetically
silenced but genomically intact.

Tazemetostat is predicted to FAIL in
cancers that are ABOVE the genomic
fixation threshold — where the identity
program genes have been deleted, mutated,
or made inaccessible by genomic
remodelling.

This is a pre-specified prediction for
the clinical failure mode of tazemetostat
in cancer. Not all EZH2-expressing cancers
will respond to EZH2 inhibition. The ones
that will not respond are those where the
target program (Basin A) has been
genomically eliminated.

The FOXA1/EZH2 ratio work
(doi:10.5281/zenodo.18883922) identifies
FOXA1 expression as a marker of Basin A
accessibility. If FOXA1 is expressed
(Basin A accessible), EZH2 inhibition
can de-arrest. If FOXA1 is deleted or
permanently silenced beyond epigenetic
reversal, EZH2 inhibition will fail.

The pig showed us the clean case.
The dog showed us the threshold.
The cancer showed us why the threshold
matters clinically.

## 10. THE UNIFIED STATEMENT

```
The attractor geometry framework operates
across a spectrum defined by one variable:

The degree to which the domestication/
arrest-maintaining process has modified
the structural substrate (S) itself,
as opposed to only the epigenetic state
of S.

When S is unmodified:
The landscape G is unchanged.
All basins encoded by S remain accessible.
The external field N alone maintains the
arrest. Remove N. The system recovers.
Sus scrofa. Early-stage cancer.

When S is partially modified:
Basin A becomes shallower.
New basins (Basin C) emerge.
Recovery is partial and slow.
The transition requires more energy.
Primitive dog breeds. Heritage pig breeds.
Intermediate-stage cancer.

When S is substantially modified:
Basin A is inaccessible.
The organism cannot recover the original
program regardless of field manipulation.
New attractors (Basin C, or cancer
progression states) are the only reachable
states.
Derived domestic dog breeds.
Genomically unstable late-stage cancer.

In all three cases:
The mechanism of arrest involves the
same geometric logic.
The field specifies the basin.
The genome encodes the landscape.
When the field changes, the system moves
if the landscape permits.
When the landscape has been modified,
field manipulation alone is insufficient.

This is the complete framework.
The pig made it visible.
The dog revealed the threshold.
The cancer requires the distinction
to be made clinically.
```
