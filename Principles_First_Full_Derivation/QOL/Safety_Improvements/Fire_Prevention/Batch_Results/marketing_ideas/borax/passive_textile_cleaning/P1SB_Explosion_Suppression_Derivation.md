## PART I: THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

### 1.1 — Why Water Suppresses Explosions

```
THE ESTABLISHED PHYSICS:

When an explosive detonates in open air:

  A high-velocity pressure wave
  expands spherically from the
  detonation point.
  Behind the pressure wave:
  superheated combustion gases
  expanding at high velocity.
  The combination — overpressure
  wave + gas expansion — is what
  causes destruction.

  The overpressure wave travels
  through air at ~340 m/s initially,
  decaying with distance by the
  inverse cube law.
  Energy density at any point
  decreases rapidly with distance
  but remains destructive over
  a significant radius.

When the same explosive detonates
submerged in water:

  The pressure wave still forms.
  But water has fundamentally
  different properties than air:

  PROPERTY 1 — DENSITY:
    Water density: 1,000 kg/m³.
    Air density: 1.2 kg/m³.
    Water is 833× denser than air.
    The pressure wave must accelerate
    833× more mass per unit volume.
    Energy is consumed doing this.
    The wave loses energy rapidly.

  PROPERTY 2 — INCOMPRESSIBILITY:
    Water is effectively incompressible
    at the pressures involved in
    most explosive events.
    The pressure wave cannot
    compress the medium it travels through.
    Instead it must displace it.
    Displacement of dense, incompressible
    mass consumes energy far more
    rapidly than compression of
    compressible low-density air.

  PROPERTY 3 — ACOUSTIC IMPEDANCE MISMATCH:
    At the water-air interface,
    the extreme impedance mismatch
    (water: 1.5 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m,
    air: 415 Pa·s/m — a ratio of ~3,600)
    causes most of the wave energy
    to reflect back into the water
    rather than transmitting into the air.
    The destructive energy is
    trapped inside the water medium.

  PROPERTY 4 — THERMAL ABSORPTION:
    Water absorbs the combustion heat
    of the explosion.
    Combustion gases expanding into
    water are immediately cooled
    and quenched.
    The chemical reaction is terminated
    by thermal quenching.
    Secondary combustion is suppressed.
    The explosion is chemically arrested.

  PROPERTY 5 — CONFINEMENT:
    Water surrounding the detonation
    provides hydrostatic confinement.
    The expanding gases must do work
    against the hydrostatic pressure
    of the surrounding water mass.
    More surrounding mass =
    more work required for expansion =
    less net expansion = less destruction.

THE RESULT OF ALL FIVE PROPERTIES COMBINED:

  An explosion detonated in sufficient
  depth of water produces:
    Drastically reduced overpressure
    at the water surface.
    Dramatically reduced fireball
    (combustion quenched).
    Contained fragmentation
    (fragments decelerate rapidly
    in dense medium).
    Localised damage rather than
    distributed destruction.

  This is not theoretical.
  Underwater demolition, naval mine
  disposal, and controlled demolition
  of submerged structures all
  rely on this principle operationally.

THE GEOMETRIC CLAIM OF THIS DOCUMENT:

  A sufficient mass of jelly-state P1S-B
  surrounding an explosive device
  replicates the five suppression
  properties of water because:

  P1S-B is water-dominant in composition.
  The SAP matrix holds that water
  in a semi-solid form that can be
  stacked, shaped, and engineered
  into a geometric encapsulation
  without requiring a body of water.

  The ocean is not special.
  The ocean is just enough water
  in the right geometry.
  P1S-B is that geometry
  in a deployable format.
```

### 2.2 — The Stacking and Compression Geometry

```
THE DEPLOYMENT INSIGHT:

  Jelly-state P1S-B can be:
    Produced in bulk.
    Packaged in standard units
    (bags, blocks, panels).
    Stacked and arranged around
    a target volume.
    Compressed by outer containment
    to increase effective density
    and reduce void space between units.

  Compression of the stacked gel
  increases effective density
  toward the density of the gel
  matrix itself.
  Higher effective density =
  better suppression per unit thickness.

  THE STACKING PRINCIPLE:

    Individual gel units (bags, panels)
    placed against each other
    with minimal air gaps between them.
    An outer rigid containment shell
    (steel, concrete, or welded
    mesh panels) applies compressive
    force to the stacked gel units.
    Compression squeezes out void
    space and brings gel units
    into full contact.
    The compressed gel mass behaves
    as a continuous semi-solid
    medium rather than discrete units.
    The acoustic and mechanical
    properties approach those of
    a continuous water mass.

  WHY COMPRESSION MATTERS:

    Air gaps between stacked units
    would create impedance discontinuities —
    surfaces where the wave transitions
    from high-impedance gel to
    low-impedance air and back.
    At each air gap, part of the
    wave reflects and part transmits —
    but the transmitted portion
    accelerates rapidly through the
    low-density air gap, delivering
    full energy to the next gel surface.
    Air gaps defeat the suppression
    mechanism.

    Compression eliminates air gaps.
    Compression is therefore not
    optional for the stacking geometry.
    It is the mechanism that makes
    stacked discrete units behave
    as a continuous medium.

  THE CONTAINMENT SHELL SERVES DUAL PURPOSE:
    Structural: applies compression
    to the gel stack.
    Geometric: defines the outer
    boundary at which residual
    overpressure is assessed.
    The shell must be designed to
    withstand the residual overpressure
    after attenuation through the gel —
    not the initial overpressure.
    If the gel attenuates by 90%,
    the shell sees 10% of the
    original destructive force.
    Shell engineering is therefore
    dramatically simpler than
    engineering a structure to
    withstand the full explosion directly.
```

## PART V: ENGINEERING REQUIREMENTS

### 5.1 — What Must Be Determined by Testing

```
THE ATTENUATION COEFFICIENT:

  The single most important
  engineering parameter is:

    How much overpressure attenuation
    does P1S-B gel provide per
    unit thickness at a given
    initial overpressure?

  This is expressed as the
  pressure attenuation coefficient
  of the gel medium.

  It is analogous to the acoustic
  attenuation coefficient used in
  underwater explosion engineering
  but measured for P1S-B specifically.

  ONCE THIS COEFFICIENT IS KNOWN:
    Required gel thickness for any
    charge size and target overpressure
    is directly calculable.
    System volume requirements
    are directly calculable.
    Field load planning is
    directly calculable.

  HOW IT IS MEASURED:
    Small-scale blast tests with
    precisely measured charge masses.
    Pressure gauges at known distances
    through gel medium.
    Comparison to same charge in air
    and in water to establish
    relative performance.
    Tests at multiple charge masses
    to characterise the coefficient
    across the relevant range.

  THIS IS THE PRIORITY TEST.
  Everything else is secondary
  to establishing this number.

THE COMPRESSION REQUIREMENT:

  What compression pressure is
  required to eliminate air gaps
  between stacked gel units
  and achieve continuous-medium
  acoustic behavior?

  This determines the structural
  requirement for the outer
  containment shell.

THE STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY LIMIT:

  At what overpressure level does
  the gel matrix fail mechanically
  (fragment rather than deform)
  such that suppression ceases?

  This is the upper bound of
  the system's operating range.
  Charges above this limit require
  more gel, not the same amount.

THE FRAGMENT CAPTURE DEPTH:

  How deep into a gel mass does
  a fragment of given mass and
  velocity penetrate before
  decelerating to rest?

  This determines the minimum gel
  depth required to capture fragments
  within the containment boundary.

  Fragment capture and blast
  attenuation may have different
  thickness requirements —
  the system is designed to the
  more demanding of the two.
```

## PART VII: FALSIFICATION CONDITIONS

```
F1 — PRIMARY ATTENUATION CONFIRMATION:
  A 100mm layer of compressed
  jelly-state P1S-B reduces peak
  overpressure from a calibrated
  test charge by at least 50%
  compared to the same charge
  in open air at equivalent distance.

  FALSIFIED IF:
  No statistically significant
  overpressure reduction is measured
  through a 100mm gel layer
  at any test charge mass.

F2 — COMPRESSION CONTINUITY:
  Stacked gel bricks under
  a defined compression pressure
  behave acoustically as a
  continuous medium — the attenuation
  measured through compressed
  stacked units is not significantly
  different from attenuation through
  a single continuous gel mass
  of equivalent total thickness.

  FALSIFIED IF:
  Air gaps between stacked units
  under compression create acoustic
  discontinuities that allow
  the pressure wave to propagate
  through the stack at higher
  velocity and lower attenuation
  than through a continuous mass.

F3 — FRAGMENT CAPTURE:
  A steel fragment of defined
  mass and velocity (representative
  of device fragmentation) is
  decelerated to rest within
  a 200mm depth of compressed
  jelly-state P1S-B under
  controlled test conditions.

  FALSIFIED IF:
  Fragments of representative
  mass and velocity penetrate
  completely through 200mm of
  gel, indicating the gel provides
  insufficient resistance for
  fragment capture at realistic
  threat parameters.

F4 — CHEMICAL SUPPRESSION:
  A test charge detonated within
  a P1S-B encapsulation produces
  measurably less fireball volume
  and duration than the same
  charge detonated in open air
  under equivalent conditions.

  FALSIFIED IF:
  Post-detonation thermal imaging
  shows no statistically significant
  reduction in fireball volume
  or duration for gel-encapsulated
  versus open-air detonation.

F5 — CONTAINMENT INTEGRITY:
  The outer containment shell
  and gel mass remain structurally
  intact (no shell rupture,

  the containment boundary)

  test charge within the design

  FALSIFIED IF:
  The containment shell ruptures

  beyond the containment boundary

  indicating the containment
  design is insufficient.
```
