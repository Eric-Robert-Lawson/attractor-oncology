### 1.2 — What the Commercial Products Do Not Have

```
WHAT P1S-B HAS THAT NO COMMERCIAL
FIRE GEL CURRENTLY INCLUDES:

BENTONITE CLAY:
  Commercial gels: none.
  P1S-B: 1.5% by mass.
  Contribution:
    Physical oxygen exclusion
    at the gel-flame interface.
    Mineral non-combustible matrix
    that remains as a residual
    barrier after water evaporates.
    When the water is gone,
    bentonite particles coat the
    substrate surface — creating
    a secondary inert barrier.
    Commercial gels, once their
    water is evaporated, leave
    only a dry SAP polymer film —
    no residual mineral barrier.

BORAX (P1S-B formula only):
  Commercial gels: none.
  P1S-B: 1.0% by mass.
  Contribution:
    Sodium borate is a recognised
    flame retardant in its own right.
    Borax is used in fireproofing
    treatments for wood and textiles
    independently of the gel mechanism.
    At fire temperatures, borax
    melts (~740°C) and forms a
    glassy borate coating that
    physically excludes oxygen
    from the substrate surface.
    This adds a CHEMICAL flame
    retardant mechanism on top of
    the physical water evaporation
    mechanism.
    Commercial gels have only
    the water mechanism.
    P1S-B (with borax) has:
      Water evaporation (primary).
      Bentonite oxygen exclusion (secondary).
      Borax borate glass formation (tertiary).
    Three mechanisms vs. one.

ANTIMICROBIAL PROPERTY:
  Commercial gels: not present.
  P1S-B: borax provides continuous
  passive antimicrobial suppression.
  In wildfire applications this
  is irrelevant — short deployment.
  In extended deployments
  (permanent structural protection,
  bunker walls, HVAC treatment):
  the antimicrobial property prevents
  the gel from becoming a biological
  hazard during storage or deployment.
  Commercial fire gels left on
  structures for weeks develop
  mould and bacterial growth.
  P1S-B does not.

MULTI-APPLICATION DESIGN:
  Commercial gels are single-purpose:
  fire suppression.
  P1S-B is multi-purpose:
  fire suppression + odor treatment +
  thermal management + explosion
  suppression + cooling.
  The customer who buys P1S-B
  for fire protection can use
  the same product for other
  applications.
  This is a purchasing simplification
  that commercial fire gels cannot offer.
```

### 2.2 — Ingredient-by-Ingredient Environmental Assessment

```
INGREDIENT 1 — SAP POLYMER
(SODIUM POLYACRYLATE):

  AQUATIC TOXICITY:
    Not classified as acutely toxic
    to aquatic organisms.
    LC50 (fish, 96h): >1,000 mg/L
    (effectively non-toxic at
    realistic environmental concentrations).
    Does not bioaccumulate.
    Insoluble in water when cross-linked
    (gel form) — does not readily
    enter aquatic systems.

  SOIL IMPACT:
    Degrades slowly in soil via
    microbial action and UV exposure.
    Degradation products: polyacrylate
    fragments → ultimately acrylate
    monomers → CO2 and water.
    Timeline: months to years depending
    on soil conditions.
    Used agriculturally as a soil
    amendment — demonstrated safe
    at application rates far exceeding
    any fire gel deployment.

  HUMAN SAFETY:
    Non-toxic. Not a skin irritant
    in gel form.
    SAP polymer is used in diapers,
    wound dressings, and food packaging.
    FDA-reviewed for indirect food contact.
    No carcinogenicity data of concern.

  REGULATORY STATUS:
    Not regulated as a hazardous material.
    Not restricted for environmental
    release in any major jurisdiction.
    USDA approved in commercial
    fire gels containing SAP.

  ENVIRONMENTAL VERDICT: CLEAN.
  No concerns at realistic
  deployment concentrations.

─────────────────────────────────────

INGREDIENT 2 — BENTONITE CLAY:

  AQUATIC TOXICITY:
    Mineral clay. Inert.
    No aquatic toxicity.
    Used as a drinking water
    treatment agent (removes
    turbidity and contaminants).
    Bentonite is literally used
    to clean water — it cannot
    meaningfully pollute it.

  SOIL IMPACT:
    Bentonite IS soil — it is a
    naturally occurring clay mineral.
    Adding bentonite to soil is
    adding clay to soil.
    No negative impact.
    Widely used as a soil amendment
    to improve water retention
    in sandy soils.

  HUMAN SAFETY:
    Food-grade bentonite is consumed
    as a dietary supplement.
    Used in wine and juice clarification.
    Cosmetic-grade bentonite is in
    face masks, toiletries.
    Industrial bentonite is the
    lining of nuclear waste repositories.
    No toxicity concerns at any
    realistic exposure level.

  REGULATORY STATUS:
    GRAS (Generally Recognised as
    Safe) by FDA for food applications.
    Not regulated as hazardous.
    Not restricted in any jurisdiction.

  ENVIRONMENTAL VERDICT: COMPLETELY CLEAN.
  Bentonite is an environmental
  benefit in most deployment contexts —
  it improves soil and water
  quality rather than degrading it.

─────────────────────────────────────

INGREDIENT 3 — SODIUM BICARBONATE
(BAKING SODA):

  AQUATIC TOXICITY:
    No aquatic toxicity at realistic
    concentrations.
    Naturally present in all
    freshwater and marine systems
    as part of the carbonate buffer.
    Adding small amounts of baking
    soda to water does not change
    its ecological character.

  SOIL IMPACT:
    Completely benign.
    Decomposes to CO2 and water.
    Does not accumulate.
    No soil toxicity at any
    realistic application level.

  HUMAN SAFETY:
    Food ingredient. Universally safe.

  REGULATORY STATUS:
    GRAS. No restrictions.

  ENVIRONMENTAL VERDICT: COMPLETELY CLEAN.

─────────────────────────────────────

INGREDIENT 4 — BORAX
(SODIUM TETRABORATE DECAHYDRATE):

  THIS IS THE INGREDIENT THAT
  DIFFERENTIATES THE TWO FORMULAS.
  FULL ASSESSMENT REQUIRED.

  AQUATIC TOXICITY:
    LC50 (rainbow trout, 96h):
    ~100 mg/L.
    LC50 (Daphnia, 48h): ~130 mg/L.
    Classification: moderately toxic
    to aquatic organisms at elevated
    concentrations.
    NOT classified as acutely toxic
    at environmental concentrations
    (the 100 mg/L threshold is far
    above typical runoff concentrations
    from gel applications).
    However: aquatic toxicity IS real
    and recognised.
    Borax is not "non-toxic to
    aquatic life" — it has a
    demonstrated dose-response
    relationship with aquatic organisms.

  SOIL IMPACT:
    Boron is an essential plant
    micronutrient at low concentrations
    (0.2–0.5 mg/kg soil optimal).
    At elevated concentrations,
    boron becomes phytotoxic —
    it suppresses plant growth.
    Phytotoxicity threshold: ~2–5 mg/kg
    soil for sensitive species.
    At high application rates
    (>5 litres/m²) on the same
    soil area repeatedly,
    boron accumulation could
    approach phytotoxic levels
    over time.
    At single-application rates
    typical of fire protection:
    boron added is well below
    phytotoxicity threshold.
    Concern is real but dose-dependent.

  HUMAN SAFETY:
    Borax has been used as a
    household cleaning product
    for 100+ years.
    Oral LD50 (rat): 2,660 mg/kg
    (moderately low toxicity —
    comparable to table salt at
    3,000 mg/kg).
    Skin contact: not a significant
    irritant at gel concentrations.
    Eye contact: mild irritant —
    avoid eye contact.
    NOT classified as carcinogenic,
    mutagenic, or reprotoxic at
    typical exposure levels.
    (EU has a Repr. 1B classification
    for very high chronic exposure —
    relevant for workers, not consumers.)

  REGULATORY STATUS:
    EU: Classified as Repr. 1B
    (reproductive toxicant, category 1B)
    at high exposure.
    This means: EU regulations
    restrict borax in consumer products
    where skin contact is prolonged
    or where it may be inhaled.
    Not banned — restricted.
    Many borax-containing cleaning
    products are sold legally in the EU
    with appropriate labeling.
    US (EPA): Not a restricted pesticide.
    Registered as a pesticide
    active ingredient (USEPA Reg.)
    for specific applications.
    USDA: not restricted in outdoor
    applications at fire gel concentrations.
    Australia: not restricted.

  THE HONEST REGULATORY SUMMARY:

    Borax is NOT freely clean
    in the way bentonite and
    baking soda are freely clean.
    It has real aquatic and soil
    boron accumulation concerns
    at high loading.
    It has a reproductive toxicity
    classification in the EU at
    high chronic exposure.

    These concerns are dose-dependent
    and real-world exposure at P1S-B
    concentrations is well below
    concerning thresholds for
    most applications.

    But the regulatory and
    environmental profile of the
    no-borax formula is simpler,
    cleaner, and carries no
    classification concerns.

  ENVIRONMENTAL VERDICT FOR BORAX:
    ACCEPTABLE AT LOW-TO-MODERATE
    APPLICATION RATES.
    REQUIRES DISCLOSURE AND LABELING.
    RESTRICTED FOR SOME APPLICATIONS
    (prolonged skin contact,
    EU consumer products,
    waterway-adjacent application).
    NOT FREELY CLEAN.
```
