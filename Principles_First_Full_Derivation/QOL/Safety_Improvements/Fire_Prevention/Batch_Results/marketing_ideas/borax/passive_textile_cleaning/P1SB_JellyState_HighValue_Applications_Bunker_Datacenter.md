### A1.1 — What Hardened Structures Face

```
A hardened structure — a bunker,

  through structural walls over time.
  Even reinforced concrete, which does
  not burn, conducts heat.
  Interior temperatures rise.
  Occupants and equipment are degraded
  not by the flame but by the
  heat conducted through the structure.

  Current mitigation: ceramic fiber
  insulation, intumescent coatings,
  spray-applied fire suppressant foam.
  Limitation: passive thermal resistance
  only — these materials slow heat
  conduction but do not actively
  absorb and dissipate thermal energy.

THREAT CLASS 2 — BLAST OVERPRESSURE:
  An explosion generates a pressure
  wave that travels through solid
  material and air simultaneously.

    Deforms ductile materials.

    directly to the interior.

  overpressure efficiently —
  the rigidity that provides
  structural strength is the same
  property that transmits the
  pressure wave to the interior.

  rubber isolation mounts, mass.
  Limitation: sandbags are passive
  mass — they attenuate by inertia.

  Ground shock from nearby explosions

  vibration through the structure.
  Interior equipment and occupants

  Sensitive equipment (electronics,
  communications, medical) is
  particularly vulnerable.

  Current mitigation: isolation
  mounts, foam padding, suspended
  floor systems.
  Limitation: passive elastic systems
  only — they store and release
  energy, they do not dissipate it.

THREAT CLASS 4 — SUSTAINED EVENT DURATION:

    Multiple blast waves.
    Ongoing thermal load.

  consumed or degraded by the

  for subsequent events.

  the critical variable, not
  peak resistance to a single event.

  material addresses all four
  threat classes simultaneously

  Concrete: blast and fire resistant,
  poor thermal management, rigid

  thermal management, no chemical

### A2.2 — Blast Overpressure Attenuation

```
THE PHYSICS:

  A blast overpressure wave
  traveling through a medium
  is attenuated by:
    Impedance mismatch between media
    (wave reflects at boundary
    between materials of different
    acoustic impedance).
    Viscoelastic absorption (energy
    dissipated as heat in the
    deforming medium).
    Geometric spreading (if the
    medium is thick enough relative
    to wavelength).

  The acoustic impedance of a material
  is the product of its density
  and the speed of sound through it.

  Concrete acoustic impedance:
  ~8 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m (rigid, high density,
  high sound speed).

  Water acoustic impedance:
  ~1.5 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m.

  Air acoustic impedance:
  ~415 Pa·s/m.

  The large impedance mismatch between
  concrete and the P1S-B gel layer,
  and between the gel layer and
  the interior air, means that
  a significant fraction of the
  incident blast wave energy is
  reflected at each interface
  rather than transmitted through.

  Additionally, the viscoelastic
  deformation of the gel absorbs
  the transmitted fraction.

  The combination of impedance
  mismatch reflection and
  viscoelastic absorption
  is the mechanism for
  blast attenuation in this system.

  WHAT IS CONFIRMED:
    The impedance mismatch is real —
    the acoustic impedances are known.
    Viscoelastic materials attenuate
    pressure waves — this is
    established physics.

  WHAT IS NOT CONFIRMED:
    The actual attenuation magnitude

  The physics says the mechanism

  released — it does not disappear.

  Current evaporative cooling systems
  (cooling towers, evaporative coolers)

  The latent heat stored in the

    a different time — where that

    This is the thermodynamic basis

## PART B2: THE CLOSED-LOOP EVAPORATIVE SYSTEM

### B2.1 — The Thermodynamic Architecture

```
THE SYSTEM — STATED PRECISELY:

  This is a closed-loop evaporative
  heat recovery system.
  P1S-B gel is one component
  of the system, not the entire system.

STAGE 1 — HEAT ABSORPTION AT SOURCE:
  Server hardware generates heat.
  A cooling medium (liquid P1S-B
  at low SAP ratio, or standard
  water with P1S-B surface treatment)
  absorbs heat from the hardware.
  The cooling medium temperature rises.
  It is pumped away from the hardware
  to the evaporation stage.

STAGE 2 — EVAPORATION:
  The heated cooling medium
  enters the evaporation chamber.
  A fraction of the water content
  evaporates, absorbing latent
  heat from the remaining fluid.
  The remaining fluid is cooled
  by the evaporation process.
  The water vapor is captured —
  not released to atmosphere —
  and directed to the condensation stage.
  The cooled fluid is returned
  to Stage 1 to absorb more
  heat from the hardware.

STAGE 3 — CONDENSATION AND
ENERGY RECOVERY:
  The captured water vapor
  is directed to a condenser.
  The vapor condenses back to liquid.
  Condensation releases the
  latent heat stored in the vapor.
  This released heat is the
  recovered energy.

  WHERE DOES THE RECOVERED ENERGY GO?

    OPTION A — SPACE HEATING:
      The condenser is a heat exchanger
      connected to the building's
      heating system.
      The latent heat from condensation
      warms the building's air or water.
      In cold climates, this directly
      offsets heating energy consumption.
      The data center's waste heat
      becomes the building's heating source.
      This is already done with
      conventional liquid cooling
      at some data centers —
      P1S-B adds the antimicrobial
      and surface area contributions
      but does not change the
      fundamental thermodynamic recovery.

    OPTION B — THERMOELECTRIC GENERATION:
      The condenser heat is applied
      to a thermoelectric generator
      (Peltier device or Stirling engine).
      The temperature differential
      between the hot condensate
      and ambient is converted
      to electrical energy.
      This directly returns energy
      to the data center's power supply.

      THE HONEST CONSTRAINT:
        Thermoelectric efficiency
        for low-temperature differentials
        (50–80°C waste heat typical
        of data centers) is 3–8%.
        A 1 MW waste heat stream
        at 5% thermoelectric efficiency
        returns 50 kW of electricity.
        That is 5% of the cooling
        energy recovered as electricity.
        It is real. It is not large.
        At scale (100 MW facility):
        5 MW of electricity recovered.
        At $0.10/kWh: $4.4M/year.
        Real value. Not transformative
        value in isolation.

    OPTION C — ABSORPTION CHILLING:
      The condensation heat drives
      an absorption refrigeration cycle
      (like a gas refrigerator but
      driven by waste heat rather
      than a flame).
      The absorption chiller produces
      cold, which is returned to
      the cooling circuit.
      This is a heat-driven refrigeration
      system that amplifies the
      cooling capacity of the
      recovered waste heat.
      Coefficient of performance (COP)
      for absorption chillers: 0.6–0.9.
      For every 1 MW of waste heat
      input, 0.6–0.9 MW of cooling
      is produced.
      This reduces the electrical
      energy needed for mechanical
      cooling by 60–90% of the
      recovered heat stream.
      This is the highest-value
      recovery option for data centers.

STAGE 4 — WATER RETURN:
  Condensed water (now liquid again)
  is returned to the evaporation
  stage to re-enter the cycle.
  The loop is closed.
  No water is lost to the atmosphere.
  No makeup water is required
  (a significant operational cost
  in conventional cooling towers).
  The borax in the P1S-B formulation
  prevents microbial growth in
  the closed loop — eliminating
  the biocide treatment cost
  of conventional systems.
```
