# P1S-B Hydrated Jelly State — New Batch Documentation
## 12g SAP / 750mL — Emergent Intermediate State Between Standard Gel and Jelly Solid
## OrganismCore — Eric Robert Lawson
## 2026-05-13

---

## WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

```
This document records a new physical
state of P1S-B that emerged from a
batch using 12g SAP in 750mL water —
above the double-dose jelly state
(8g / 750mL) but produced with
different hydration behaviour.

The batch did not achieve full
SAP hydration at 8g, requiring
an additional 4g mid-process.
The result is a physically distinct
state from the previous jelly batch:
more moist, more pliable, less solid —
but still clearly gel-structured
rather than pourable.

This state is documented precisely
because it may be more useful
than either the standard gel
or the firm jelly for specific
applications that require:
  Some degree of surface conformity.
  Sustained moisture at the interface.
  Softer mechanical behaviour.
  More intimate contact with
  irregular surfaces than the
  firm jelly can achieve.

The state is named here as:
HYDRATED JELLY STATE
to distinguish it from:
  Standard gel (1× SAP, pourable).
  Jelly state (2× SAP, firm solid).
  Hydrated jelly (1.6× SAP effective,
  moist semi-solid — this batch).
```

## PART I: BATCH PARAMETERS

### 1.1 — What Was Made

```
BATCH PARAMETERS — AS EXECUTED:

  Water volume: 750mL distilled.

  SAP added (initial dry blend): 8g.
  Post-rest assessment: gel too watery,
  SAP had not fully hydrated to
  expected jelly consistency.
  SAP added (mid-process): 4g.
  TOTAL SAP: 12g.

  Bentonite: standard ratio for 750mL
  (~13.5g — confirm against protocol).
  Borax: standard ratio for 750mL
  (~9.0g).
  Baking soda: standard ratio for 750mL
  (~5.0g).

  SAP RATIO RELATIVE TO STANDARD:
  Standard (1×): ~4g / 750mL.
  Jelly state (2×): 8g / 750mL.
  This batch (3×): 12g / 750mL.

  NOTE ON EFFECTIVE CONCENTRATION:
  Despite higher SAP mass than the
  jelly batch, the result is LESS SOLID.
  This is a hydration behaviour
  anomaly that requires explanation.
  See Part 1.2 below.

RESULTING STATE:
  More moist than the jelly batch.
  More pliable and moldable.
  Not pourable — retains shape
  under gravity.
  Not firm solid — deforms easily
  under light pressure.
  Not putty — too hydrated to
  hold a pressed shape without
  relaxing back.
  Surface feels moist/wet to touch
  (unlike jelly state which had
  a non-wet surface).
  No visible free water pooling.
  SAP crystals likely present
  but less visually prominent
  than in the jelly batch
  (more fully hydrated, therefore
  less crystalline appearance).

CLASSIFICATION:
  HYDRATED JELLY STATE.
  Semi-solid, moist, pliable.
  Intermediate between:
    Standard gel (fully pourable).
    Jelly state (firm, non-wet surface,
    shape-holding under gravity and
    light handling).
```

### 1.2 — Why More SAP Produced Less Solid Result

```
THE APPARENT PARADOX:

  Jelly batch: 8g SAP / 750mL → firm solid.
  This batch: 12g SAP / 750mL → softer.

  More SAP should produce firmer gel.
  This batch produced the opposite.
  This requires explanation because
  understanding the mechanism is
  necessary for reproducible
  batch production.

HYPOTHESIS 1 — IONIC INTERFERENCE
FROM SAP ADDITION SEQUENCE:

  The most likely explanation is
  the addition sequence.

  In the jelly batch:
  All SAP was in the initial dry blend
  (or added early enough to hydrate
  in a controlled sequence with
  the other dry ingredients).
  The SAP hydrated in relatively
  pure water before significant
  mineral ion concentration built up.

  In this batch:
  The initial 8g SAP was added with
  the full mineral load (borax,
  bentonite, baking soda).
  The rest period showed insufficient
  hydration — the SAP was not
  achieving full swelling.
  The additional 4g was added
  AFTER the mineral ions were
  fully dissolved in the water.

  MECHANISM:
  SAP polymer hydration is suppressed
  by ionic concentration.
  Borax and baking soda both
  contribute dissolved ions to
  the water.
  High ionic concentration in
  the surrounding water reduces
  the osmotic gradient that drives
  water into the SAP polymer chains.
  Reduced osmotic gradient =
  less water absorbed per gram of SAP =
  less swelling = less gel firmness.

  The 4g added mid-process was
  added into water already heavily
  loaded with dissolved ions from
  the full mineral batch.
  That 4g could not hydrate fully —
  it was working against ionic
  suppression from the start.

  RESULT:
  Effective SAP hydration was below
  what 12g in pure water would achieve.
  The total SAP mass is higher than
  the jelly batch but the effective
  swollen SAP volume is lower
  because ionic suppression reduced
  hydration efficiency.

  The gel is therefore softer
  despite having more SAP —
  the SAP did not fully hydrate,
  so free water remains in the
  system rather than being
  absorbed into the polymer matrix.
  That free water is what makes
  the surface feel moist and the
  texture more pliable.

HYPOTHESIS 2 — BENTONITE RATIO MISMATCH:

  If bentonite was at standard ratio
  for 750mL (designed for 4g SAP),
  then the ratio of SAP to bentonite
  in this batch is 3× what the
  standard formula assumes.
  Excess SAP relative to bentonite
  means the clay matrix structure
  is diluted relative to the
  swelling polymer.
  The clay provides rigidity through
  its platelet structure.
  Diluted clay = softer overall texture.
  This is a contributing factor,
  not the primary mechanism.

CONCLUSION ON THE PARADOX:

  The softer result from more SAP
  is consistent with ionic suppression
  of the additional 4g added mid-process.
  The batch effectively has:
    ~8g fully or partially hydrated SAP
    (the initial addition).
    ~4g partially hydrated SAP
    (the mid-process addition,
    suppressed by ionic environment).
    Remaining free water from the
    partially hydrated SAP mass.

  This explains: moist surface,
  pliable texture, shape retention
  without firmness.

IMPLICATION FOR PRODUCTION PROTOCOL:

  To reproduce the jelly state reliably:
    ALL SAP must be in the initial
    dry blend before water contact.
    The dry blend order matters:
    mix SAP with bentonite first,
    then add borax and bicarb,
    so the SAP is coated with
    clay before the ionic compounds
    dissolve — partially buffering
    the ionic suppression.
    Do not add SAP after water
    contact has begun.
    Do not add SAP after minerals
    have dissolved.

  To reproduce the hydrated jelly
  state intentionally:
    Use the sequence of this batch:
    full mineral load first,
    then add partial SAP mid-process.
    Or: use 12g SAP total in
    the initial dry blend with
    increased water ratio to
    compensate for higher SAP mass.
    Testing required to characterise
    the reproducible protocol for
    this state.
```

## PART II: PHYSICAL CHARACTERISATION

### 2.1 — Confirmed Properties

```
WHAT IT IS:

  TEXTURE: Moist semi-solid.
    The closest reference is a
    thick hydrogel dressing or
    a well-hydrated aloe vera gel —
    holds its form but yields
    immediately under light pressure
    and feels wet at the surface.

  SURFACE: Moist/wet.
    Unlike the jelly state (dry surface).
    Free moisture is present at
    the gel surface.
    This is the residual free water
    from partially hydrated SAP.

  SHAPE RETENTION: PARTIAL.
    Holds shape under gravity: YES.
    Holds shape under light handling: YES.
    Holds shape when placed on a surface: YES.
    Deforms under moderate pressure: YES.
    Returns to original shape after
    pressure removed: PARTIAL —
    some elastic recovery, some
    permanent deformation.
    This is viscoelastic behaviour
    weighted toward viscous
    (flows slowly) rather than
    elastic (springs back).

  SURFACE CONFORMITY:
    When pressed against a surface,
    the moist gel wets that surface
    and conforms more closely
    than the firm jelly would.
    This is a new property not
    present in the jelly state.
    The jelly state maintains a
    gap between its surface and
    an irregular surface —
    the hydrated jelly fills that gap.
    This is significant for applications
    requiring surface contact.

  CUTTABILITY: YES — WITH MODIFICATION.
    Can be cut like the jelly state
    but the cut surfaces are
    wet and do not hold a
    clean edge as well.
    Cut surfaces tend to slowly
    merge if pressed together —
    unlike the jelly state where
    cut surfaces do not rejoin.
    This is because the free water
    acts as a partial adhesive
    between cut surfaces under pressure.
    NOT putty adhesion.
    Slow, pressure-dependent, partial
    surface merging only.

  MOLDABILITY: LIMITED.
    More conforming than jelly state.
    Less conforming than putty.
    Under sustained pressure against
    a surface, the hydrated jelly
    will slowly conform to that surface
    over minutes to hours.
    It does not spring back fully.
    It does not hold the pressed
    shape when pressure is removed —
    it relaxes partially toward
    its resting form.
    This is flow-based conformity,
    not plastic deformation.
```

## PART III: BENEFITS OF THE HYDRATED JELLY STATE

### 3.1 — What the Moist State Enables

```
BENEFIT 1 — SUSTAINED SURFACE
MOISTURE DELIVERY:

  The hydrated jelly state has
  free water at its surface that
  is available to wet any surface
  it contacts.
  The standard jelly state does not —
  its dry surface does not wet
  adjacent surfaces.

  APPLICATIONS THIS ENABLES:
    Any application where sustained
    moisture at the interface between
    the gel and an adjacent surface
    is the desired outcome.
    Cooling of a surface (evaporative
    cooling at the interface rather
    than only at the open surface).
    Sustained chemical delivery to
    the contacted surface (borax
    antimicrobial at the interface,
    not just in the air above it).
    Wound-adjacent cooling:
    the moist surface is gentler
    against skin or tissue than
    a firm dry-surface solid.

BENEFIT 2 — PRESSURE-DRIVEN
SURFACE CONFORMITY:

  Under sustained pressure, the
  hydrated jelly conforms to the
  contacting surface geometry.
  Irregular surfaces, curved surfaces,
  and textured surfaces receive
  more intimate contact from this
  state than from the firm jelly.

  APPLICATIONS THIS ENABLES:
    Body-contour applications
    (cooling packs, therapeutic gel
    applications against skin).
    Irregular industrial surface
    coverage where the firm jelly
    would leave air gaps.
    Pipe and curved surface coverage
    in thermal or chemical treatment
    applications.
    Mine or tunnel surface treatment
    where surfaces are never flat.

BENEFIT 3 — PARTIAL SELF-HEALING
AT CUT SURFACES:

  The fact that cut surfaces can
  merge under pressure means
  the hydrated jelly can be shaped
  by cutting and then pressed together
  to form a continuous mass —
  something the firm jelly cannot do.

  PRACTICAL IMPLICATION:
    Pieces can be shaped separately
    and assembled in place.
    A panel with a cut-out for
    a pipe or protrusion can be
    made by cutting two pieces
    and pressing them together
    around the obstacle.
    The pieces merge at the joint
    under the pressure of the
    surrounding containment.
    The firm jelly would leave
    a gap at the joint.
    The hydrated jelly closes it.

BENEFIT 4 — LOWER SAP CRYSTAL
VISIBILITY / SMOOTHER TEXTURE:

  The SAP crystals in the hydrated
  jelly are more fully surrounded
  by free water and present a
  smoother overall texture than
  the firm jelly's visually prominent
  crystal inclusions.

  FOR APPLICATIONS WHERE AESTHETICS
  OR SURFACE UNIFORMITY MATTER:
  (consumer products, medical-adjacent,
  visible installations)
  the hydrated jelly presents better.

BENEFIT 5 — ENHANCED EVAPORATION
RATE AT OPEN SURFACE:

  The wet surface of the hydrated
  jelly has more immediately available
  free water for evaporation than
  the dry-surface jelly state.
  In applications where evaporation
  rate is the desired property
  (cooling, thermal protection,
  energy extraction), the hydrated
  jelly may deliver higher initial
  evaporation rate per unit surface
  area than the firm jelly.

  The firm jelly must first allow
  water to migrate from the polymer
  matrix to the surface before
  it can evaporate.
  The hydrated jelly has free water
  already present at the surface.
  Initial evaporation rate is higher.
  Total duration may be shorter
  (free water depletes before
  polymer-bound water) —
  this is a trade-off, not a
  pure advantage.

BENEFIT 6 — MORE FORGIVING TO
PRODUCE:

  The hydrated jelly is produced
  by adding SAP mid-process —
  a less controlled procedure
  than the precise dry-blend protocol
  required for the firm jelly.
  It is more tolerant of imprecise
  SAP addition timing.
  For field production or situations
  where precise dry-blend sequencing
  is not possible, the hydrated
  jelly may be the more reliably
  achievable state.
  This is a production benefit,
  not a product benefit.
  It lowers the skill threshold
  for producing a functional state.
```

## PART IV: COSTS AND LIMITATIONS

### 4.1 — Where the Hydrated State Is Inferior

```
COST 1 — REDUCED SHAPE RETENTION
UNDER HANDLING:

  The hydrated jelly deforms more
  easily than the firm jelly.
  In applications where the gel
  must hold a specific geometry
  under physical handling —
  being placed, moved, positioned —
  the hydrated state is harder
  to work with.
  It sags more. It deforms on contact.
  The firm jelly can be handled
  like a solid object.
  The hydrated jelly must be
  treated more carefully.

COST 2 — WET SURFACE CREATES
RESIDUE ON CONTACT SURFACES:

  The wet surface leaves a moisture
  trail on any surface it touches.
  The firm jelly's dry surface does not.
  In applications where the gel
  is placed inside equipment,
  clothing, or finished surfaces,
  the wet surface causes marking
  and potential staining from the
  bentonite clay fraction.
  Bentonite is a fine mineral clay
  that stains porous surfaces.
  The moist surface delivers bentonite
  to the contact surface — the dry
  surface does not.

COST 3 — SHORTER ACTIVE LIFE
IN OPEN-AIR DEPLOYMENT:

  Free surface water evaporates
  faster than polymer-bound water.
  The initial evaporation rate
  is higher but the free water
  is depleted sooner.
  After free water depletion, the
  hydrated jelly approaches the
  firm jelly state.
  Total active life until dry:
  may be shorter than the firm jelly
  in the same conditions.

  FOR APPLICATIONS REQUIRING
  SUSTAINED LONG-DURATION PERFORMANCE:
  The firm jelly's polymer-bound
  water releases more slowly and
  consistently over a longer period.
  The hydrated jelly front-loads
  its moisture delivery.

COST 4 — REDUCED FIRMNESS FOR
  MESH CONTAINMENT APPLICATIONS:

  The HVAC mesh insert application
  requires the gel to hold its form
  inside a mesh housing under airflow.
  The firm jelly does this reliably —
  it holds shape under gravity
  and light airflow.
  The hydrated jelly, being more
  pliable, may flow slowly through
  larger mesh apertures over time
  or sag within the housing.
  For mesh insert applications,
  the firm jelly is the preferred state.
  The hydrated jelly requires
  smaller mesh apertures or more
  contained housing geometry
  to prevent slow extrusion.

COST 5 — LESS PREDICTABLE
MECHANICAL BEHAVIOUR:

  The firm jelly has well-defined
  mechanical properties:
  it is a soft solid with consistent
  behaviour across the batch.
  The hydrated jelly's properties
  depend on how much free water
  is present — which depends on
  the degree of ionic suppression
  during SAP hydration.
  Batch-to-batch variability is
  higher for the hydrated jelly
  because the degree of ionic
  suppression varies with
  mixing speed, temperature,
  and mineral dissolution rate.
  The firm jelly made from a
  precise dry-blend protocol
  is more reproducible.

COST 6 — NOT SUITABLE FOR
EXPLOSION SUPPRESSION STACKING:

  The compression geometry derived
  for explosion suppression requires
  the gel to be stackable and
  compressible to eliminate air gaps.
  The firm jelly stacks reliably —
  it holds its shape until compressed.
  The hydrated jelly would deform
  and flow under stacking weight
  before outer compression is applied.
  Stacking geometry is harder
  to maintain during assembly.
  For explosion suppression applications,
  the firm jelly is the preferred state.
```

## PART V: APPLICATIONS FAVOURED BY
## THE HYDRATED JELLY STATE

```
Based on the benefit/cost analysis:

APPLICATION 1 — BODY-CONTOUR COOLING
AND THERAPEUTIC SURFACE CONTACT:

  The moist, conforming surface
  is ideal for applications against
  skin or body surfaces.

  Examples:
    Cooling pack for fever, injury,
    or post-operative swelling.
    Heat stress mitigation in
    industrial or emergency settings.
    Equine or veterinary cooling
    (large animal surface cooling
    is a real application for
    hydrogel-type materials).
    Personal protective equipment
    cooling inserts (helmet liner,
    vest insert) where the gel
    conforms to the head or body
    and provides sustained cooling
    through evaporation.

  WHY THIS STATE SPECIFICALLY:
    Soft, moist surface is tolerable
    against skin.
    Slow conformity means it shapes
    to the body over time.
    Wet surface provides immediate
    cooling contact without requiring
    evaporation delay.
    Free water at interface provides
    sustained moisture to the skin
    surface.

APPLICATION 2 — IRREGULAR SURFACE
THERMAL OR CHEMICAL TREATMENT:

  Pipe insulation, curved duct
  surfaces, rough stone or concrete
  walls, corrugated metal surfaces.
  The hydrated jelly, pressed into
  place, conforms more closely
  than the firm jelly and maintains
  closer contact over time.

  For bunker wall encapsulation:
    In areas where the wall surface
    is irregular (rough concrete,
    penetrations, seams), the hydrated
    jelly in mesh containment
    fills irregularities better
    than the firm jelly.
    The two states can be used
    in combination:
    Firm jelly for flat regular areas
    (efficient production, reliable geometry).
    Hydrated jelly for irregular areas
    (better surface contact, gap filling).

APPLICATION 3 — SEEP-BASED TREATMENT
WHERE HIGHER INITIAL DELIVERY IS DESIRED:

  In the shoe packet application,
  the gel must seep through
  perforations to deliver treatment
  chemistry to the shoe interior.
  The hydrated jelly seeps more
  readily through perforations than
  the firm jelly — the free surface
  water reduces the resistance to
  extrusion through small holes.
  A hydrated jelly shoe packet
  may deliver more immediate
  chemistry to the shoe interior
  at the beginning of the treatment
  window.

  TRADE-OFF:
    Earlier delivery but shorter
    active window.
    For a 24-hour shoe treatment:
    firm jelly may sustain treatment
    longer. Hydrated jelly delivers
    more in the first 8 hours.
    Application requirement determines
    which is preferred.

APPLICATION 4 — GEL JOINT FILLING
IN MULTI-PIECE ASSEMBLIES:

  Where two gel pieces must meet
  at a joint and the joint must
  not leave a gap, the hydrated
  jelly's partial self-healing
  under pressure seals the joint
  in a way the firm jelly cannot.
  This is useful in:
    Bunker wall encapsulation at
    corners and penetrations.
    Explosion suppression assembly
    at the joints between panels
    (used as a joint filler,
    not as the main panel material).
    Any multi-piece installation
    where a continuous gel mass
    is required but must be
    assembled from sections.

APPLICATION 5 — EVAPORATIVE COOLING
ARRAY WHERE FRONT-LOADED DELIVERY
IS DESIRED:

  In the thermodynamic closed-loop
  energy extraction application,
  the evaporation rate determines
  energy output.
  A hydrated jelly array delivers
  higher initial evaporation rate
  than the firm jelly.
  For systems where peak output
  matters (e.g., solar thermal
  systems where peak production
  is at midday when demand is
  also highest), the hydrated jelly's
  front-loaded moisture delivery
  profile may be preferred.
  The array is recharged (rehydrated)
  periodically — the front-loaded
  profile is not a limitation
  if recharge frequency is matched
  to the depletion rate.
```

## PART VI: PROTOCOL NOTES FOR
## REPRODUCING THIS STATE

```
TO REPRODUCE THE HYDRATED JELLY
STATE INTENTIONALLY:

  METHOD A — REPLICATE THIS BATCH:
    Full mineral dry blend at
    standard ratios for 750mL.
    Add 8g SAP in initial dry blend.
    Blend per standard protocol.
    Rest 60 minutes.
    If gel is too watery after rest:
    add 4g SAP directly to the
    partially set gel.
    Blend again for 5 minutes.
    Rest additional 30 minutes.
    Result: hydrated jelly state.

    CAVEAT: This method has high
    batch-to-batch variability
    because the outcome depends on
    the degree of SAP suppression
    in the first rest period,
    which is sensitive to blending
    conditions.

  METHOD B — CONTROLLED REPRODUCTION:
    Reduce mineral ratios slightly
    to reduce ionic suppression
    while increasing SAP to 12g.
    Example:
      750mL distilled water.
      12g SAP (all in dry blend).
      10g bentonite (reduced from 13.5g).
      7g borax (reduced from 9g).
      4g baking soda (reduced from 5g).
    Lower ionic load allows more
    SAP hydration while the higher
    SAP mass is partially suppressed —
    the balance may produce a
    reproducible hydrated jelly
    with more consistency than
    Method A.
    THIS IS UNTESTED — it is a
    derived protocol requiring
    a test batch to confirm.

  METHOD C — WATER ADDITION TO
  FIRM JELLY:
    Produce a firm jelly batch
    per the confirmed 2× SAP protocol.
    Add water incrementally (20–30mL
    at a time) to the firm jelly,
    working it in by hand or
    with brief blending.
    Stop at the hydration level
    that produces the desired texture.
    This gives the most control
    over the final state.
    The firm jelly is the starting
    point and water is added
    until the target consistency
    is reached.
    More controllable than Methods
    A or B.

TO REPRODUCE THE FIRM JELLY STATE
(as a reminder — to avoid producing
the hydrated state unintentionally):

  ALL SAP in the initial dry blend.
  Never add SAP after water contact.
  Blend dry ingredients together
  for minimum 90 seconds before
  adding to water.
  Use the correct SAP ratio (8g/750mL).
  Do not exceed the SAP ratio —
  more SAP with full ionic mineral
  load produces hydrated jelly,
  not firmer jelly.
```
