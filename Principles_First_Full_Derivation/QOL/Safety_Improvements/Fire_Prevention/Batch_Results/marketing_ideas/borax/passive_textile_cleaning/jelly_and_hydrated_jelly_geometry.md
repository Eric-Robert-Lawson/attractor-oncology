## WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

```
Two physically distinct states of P1S-B
have been confirmed:

  FIRM JELLY STATE (2× SAP):
    Dry surface.
    Shape-holding under gravity and handling.
    Cuttable, non-adhesive, non-reformable.
    Slow sustained moisture release.
    Visible SAP crystals.
    Consistent, reproducible from
    controlled dry-blend protocol.

  HYDRATED JELLY STATE (3× SAP

    Wet surface.
    Pliable, slowly conforming
    under sustained pressure.
    Partial cut-surface merging.
    Higher initial evaporation rate.
    Front-loaded moisture delivery.
    More tolerant of irregular surfaces.

Prior documents treated these states
as alternatives — one preferred over
the other depending on the application.

This document derives a different
geometric insight:

  The two states are not competitors.
  They are complements.
  Each has properties the other lacks.
  When used together in a single
  system, each state occupies the
  role it is structurally suited for,
  and the combined system has
  capabilities neither state can
  achieve alone.

This is not a novel chemical insight.
It is a structural observation about
what happens when you assign functions
to materials based on what they
actually do rather than forcing
one material to do everything.

What emerges is derived below.

### 1.2 — The Structural Insight

```
THE LAYERED COMPOSITE PRINCIPLE:

  Engineering materials science has
  a well-established principle:
  composite materials outperform
  their individual components
  when the components are arranged
  so that each operates in the
  regime where it performs best
  and is shielded from the regime
  where it performs worst.

  Concrete + steel rebar:
    Concrete: excellent in compression,
    brittle in tension.
    Steel: excellent in tension,
    inefficient in compression.
    Together: reinforced concrete
    handles both — neither could
    do it alone.

  Carbon fibre + epoxy resin:
    Carbon fibre: extremely high
    tensile strength, brittle in shear.
    Epoxy: transfers shear between
    fibres, distributes load.
    Neither alone produces the
    stiffness-to-weight ratio
    of the composite.

  Cortical bone + trabecular bone:
    Cortical: dense, hard outer shell,
    load-bearing in compression.
    Trabecular: porous inner lattice,
    energy-absorbing, lightweight.
    Together: bone handles both
    sustained load and impact
    absorption with minimum mass.

  P1S-B DUAL-STATE FOLLOWS THE
  SAME GEOMETRIC PRINCIPLE:

    FIRM JELLY: structural layer.
    Holds geometry. Maintains position.
    Provides sustained slow-release
    chemistry. Analogous to the
    cortical bone or the concrete.

    HYDRATED JELLY: interfacial layer.
    Fills gaps. Transfers chemistry
    across surfaces. Provides
    immediate surface activity.
    Analogous to the trabecular bone
    or the epoxy matrix.

    Together: a system that holds
    its shape (firm) while maintaining
    active surface chemistry across
    all interfaces (hydrated) and
    delivering both immediate and
    sustained release profiles
    simultaneously.

  THE COMPOSITE GEOMETRY IS:

    FIRM JELLY BODY (bulk volume):
      Provides mass.
      Provides structural integrity.
      Provides sustained chemistry.
      Provides shape definition.

    HYDRATED JELLY INTERFACE
    (surface and joint layer):
      Fills gaps between firm units.
      Provides immediate surface
      moisture to adjacent surfaces.
      Seals interfaces between
      discrete firm jelly pieces.
      Acts as chemical transfer
      medium between the firm
      body and the contact surface.

  WHAT THIS COMPOSITE ACHIEVES:

    The firm body holds everything
    in place and delivers chemistry
    over the long term.

    The hydrated interface eliminates
    the gaps that the firm body
    cannot fill and provides
    immediate surface activity
    that the firm body cannot deliver.

    Together they produce:
      Structural integrity (firm).
      Gap elimination (hydrated).
      Immediate surface chemistry (hydrated).
      Sustained bulk chemistry (firm).
      Shape retention (firm).
      Surface conformity (hydrated).

    No single state achieves all six.
    The composite achieves all six.

## PART II: THE COMPOSITE ARCHITECTURES

### 2.1 — Architecture A: Core and Jacket

```
GEOMETRY:
  Firm jelly core.
  Hydrated jelly jacket surrounding
  the core on all exterior surfaces.

ANALOGY:
  An orange.
  The flesh (firm) is the bulk medium.
  The surface moisture on each
  segment interface (hydrated) is
  the contact transfer medium.

HOW IT IS ASSEMBLED:

  STEP 1:
    Firm jelly batch produced at 2× SAP.
    Cut into blocks of required size.
    (100mm × 100mm × 50mm blocks,
    or any geometry suited to
    the containment system.)

  STEP 2:
    Hydrated jelly batch produced
    at 3× SAP.
    While still highly hydrated
    (immediately after rest period),
    spread a 5–10mm layer over
    all surfaces of each firm block.
    The hydrated jelly adheres
    to the firm block surface
    because the firm surface
    has enough free moisture
    at its interface to create
    surface tension contact,
    even though it does not
    feel wet to the touch.

  STEP 3:
    Jacketed blocks are placed
    into the containment system
    (mesh housing, enclosure, or
    direct stacking).
    As blocks are placed adjacent
    to each other, the hydrated
    jackets come into contact.
    Under the weight of stacking
    and any applied compression,
    the hydrated interfaces
    merge slowly.
    Gaps between blocks disappear
    as hydrated material fills them.

  RESULT:
    A continuous semi-solid mass
    of firm jelly blocks bonded
    together at their interfaces
    by hydrated jelly that has
    flowed into and filled all
    inter-block gaps.
    Structurally: like individual
    bricks (firm) mortared together
    (hydrated).
    The brick is structural.
    The mortar fills the joints.
    The wall is stronger than
    either alone.

THE BRICK-AND-MORTAR ANALOGY IS PRECISE:

  Traditional masonry:
    Brick: compressive strength,
    dimensional stability.
    Mortar: gap filling, load
    distribution, surface sealing.
    Neither does the other's job.
    Both are required for a wall.

  Dual-state P1S-B composite:
    Firm jelly brick: compressive
    resistance, shape stability,
    sustained chemistry.
    Hydrated jelly mortar: gap filling,
    surface chemistry transfer,
    interface sealing, immediate
    moisture delivery.
    Neither does the other's job.
    Both are required for a
    continuous active composite.

APPLICATIONS WHERE CORE-JACKET
ARCHITECTURE IS OPTIMAL:

  BLAST SUPPRESSION PANELS:
    Each panel is a stack of
    firm-core/hydrated-jacket blocks.
    The firm core provides the
    mass and viscoelastic resistance.
    The hydrated jacket eliminates
    air gaps between blocks —
    the single greatest source
    of acoustic discontinuity
    in a stacked suppression system.
    The composite panel behaves
    more like a continuous water
    mass than a stack of discrete
    units precisely because the
    hydrated mortar has eliminated
    the inter-block air interfaces.
    Blast attenuation is improved
    not by changing the chemistry
    but by eliminating the
    acoustic discontinuities
    that defeat the suppression
    geometry.

  BUNKER WALL ENCAPSULATION:
    Firm blocks provide the
    thermal mass and structural
    layer against the wall.
    Hydrated jacket conforms to
    the wall surface irregularities
    (concrete is not perfectly flat —
    the hydrated layer fills the
    micro-gaps between the block
    and the wall, improving
    thermal contact and preventing
    air pocket formation that
    would reduce thermal protection).

  HVAC REGISTER INSERT:
    Firm block fills the mesh housing.
    Thin hydrated layer on the
    air-facing surface provides
    immediate high-surface-area
    moisture to passing air.
    Firm body beneath provides
    the sustained release that
    the hydrated surface depletes
    and is replenished from.
    The register insert now has
    a two-phase release profile:
    immediate burst from hydrated
    surface, sustained delivery
    from firm core over weeks.
    This matches the two different
    odor exposure profiles:
    acute odor events (cooking,
    pet activity) and chronic
    background odor (mold, dust).
```

### 2.2 — Architecture B: Layered Sandwich

```
GEOMETRY:
  Alternating horizontal layers
  of firm and hydrated jelly.
  Firm layer → Hydrated layer →
  Firm layer → Hydrated layer →
  Firm layer.

ANALOGY:
  A club sandwich.
  The bread slices are structural.
  The filling layers are the
  active transfer media.
  The structure holds everything
  in position.
  The filling is where the
  chemistry happens at each
  interface.

THE FUNCTIONAL LOGIC:

  In any application where the
  threat or stimulus arrives
  from one direction (a blast
  wave, a heat source, an airflow),
  the stimulus must propagate
  through the composite.

  As it propagates:
    It first encounters the
    firm layer: structural resistance,
    impedance mismatch, viscoelastic
    damping.
    It then encounters the
    hydrated layer: interfacial
    chemistry, gap elimination,
    additional moisture transfer.
    It then encounters the next
    firm layer: renewed structural
    resistance.

  Each transition between layers
  is an impedance discontinuity
  that reflects a fraction of
  the wave back toward the source.
  Multiple transitions multiply
  the reflections.
  Each firm layer dissipates
  energy viscoelastically.
  Each hydrated layer transfers
  chemistry and provides
  additional thermal absorption.

  THE SANDWICH ARCHITECTURE
  IS A MULTI-STAGE ATTENUATOR.

  It is not more material —
  the total volume is the same
  as a single-state block of
  equivalent dimensions.
  It is the same material arranged
  so that each layer transition
  adds an attenuation stage.

  This is the principle behind:
    Multi-layer ballistic armour
    (each layer is a different
    material or density that
    presents an impedance discontinuity).
    Acoustic panels in recording
    studios (alternating dense
    and porous layers).
    Blast-resistant glazing
    (alternating glass and
    polymer interlayers).

  The dual-state P1S-B sandwich
  applies this established
  attenuation principle to a
  single chemical system expressed
  at two state levels.

HOW IT IS ASSEMBLED:

  STEP 1:
    Pour or cut firm jelly to
    form the base layer.
    Thickness: 30–50mm per layer.

  STEP 2:
    Spread or pour hydrated jelly
    over the firm layer surface.
    Thickness: 10–15mm.
    The hydrated layer rests on
    and slowly conforms to
    the firm layer surface.

  STEP 3:
    Add next firm layer on top
    of the hydrated layer.
    The firm layer's weight
    compresses the hydrated layer
    below it, pressing out any
    air and ensuring full contact
    at both interfaces.

  STEP 4:
    Repeat to desired total thickness.

  STEP 5:
    Outer containment applied
    to maintain compression
    and prevent layer separation
    under operational loads.

APPLICATIONS WHERE LAYERED SANDWICH
ARCHITECTURE IS OPTIMAL:

  FLOOR-MOUNTED BLAST ATTENUATION:
    Vehicle underbody protection.
    Building floor protection
    against subsurface blast.
    The blast arrives from below
    and must propagate through
    multiple attenuating layers
    before reaching the protected
    surface.
    Horizontal layering matches
    the threat geometry precisely.

  THERMAL PROTECTION PANELS:
    In bunker wall applications,
    radiant heat arrives as a
    flux perpendicular to the
    wall surface.
    Layered sandwich presents
    multiple evaporation fronts
    to the incoming heat flux.
    As the outer firm layer dries,
    the hydrated layer beneath
    it maintains active evaporation
    from a fresh wet surface.
    As that depletes, the next
    firm layer provides further
    sustained release.
    The panel has a sequential
    depletion profile rather than
    depleting all at once —
    thermal protection duration
    is extended beyond what
    either state alone provides
    at the same total volume.

  DATA CENTER COOLING ARRAYS:
    Horizontal arrays where
    warm air rises through the
    composite.
    Air contacts multiple
    evaporation surfaces in
    sequence as it rises.
    Each hydrated layer provides
    immediate evaporative cooling.
    Each firm layer provides
    sustained moisture replenishment
    of the hydrated layer above it.
    The air exits at the top
    having passed through multiple
    active cooling stages rather
    than a single surface.
    Cooling efficiency per unit
    height of composite is higher
    than a single-state array
    of equivalent height.
```

### 2.3 — Architecture C: Gradient Composite

```
GEOMETRY:
  Not discrete layers but a
  continuous gradient from
  maximum hydration (high SAP,
  wet surface) at the active
  face to minimum hydration
  (lower SAP, dry structural)
  at the backing face.

ANALOGY:
  Functionally graded materials
  (FGMs) — engineered composites
  where composition varies
  continuously across the
  material thickness.
  Used in aerospace thermal
  protection systems, where
  the outer face must tolerate
  extreme temperature while
  the inner face must maintain
  structural properties.
  The gradient eliminates the
  stress concentrations at
  discrete layer interfaces
  that cause delamination
  in layered systems.

HOW IT IS PRODUCED:

  STEP 1:
    Prepare several batches at
    different SAP ratios:
    Batch A: 2.0× SAP (firm).
    Batch B: 2.3× SAP.
    Batch C: 2.6× SAP.
    Batch D: 3.0× SAP (maximum hydration).

  STEP 2:
    Layer in order from backing
    to active face:
    A → B → C → D.
    Each layer is thinner than
    in the discrete sandwich —
    the gradient is created by
    more, thinner layers.

  STEP 3:
    Under compression, the
    interfaces between adjacent
    gradient layers are more
    compatible than discrete
    firm-to-hydrated transitions.
    Partial merging occurs at
    each interface because the
    adjacent layers differ only
    slightly in water content.
    The result is a genuinely
    graded material with no
    sharp interfaces.

THE ADVANTAGE OF THE GRADIENT:

  Sharp interfaces in layered
  composites are stress
  concentration points under
  mechanical load (blast, impact).
  The shear stress at a discrete
  boundary between firm and
  hydrated material under
  blast loading can cause
  delamination — the layers
  separate rather than
  attenuating together.

  A gradient eliminates sharp
  boundaries.
  The mechanical transition
  from firm to hydrated is
  continuous — no point at
  which there is a sudden
  change in stiffness that
  concentrates stress.
  Under blast loading, the
  gradient composite deforms
  as a continuous medium rather
  than as discrete layers
  that can separate.

APPLICATIONS WHERE GRADIENT
ARCHITECTURE IS OPTIMAL:

  HIGH-RATE LOADING APPLICATIONS:
    Blast suppression where
    the loading rate is very high
    (millisecond timescale).
    At high loading rates,
    delamination is the primary
    failure mode of layered systems.
    The gradient eliminates
    delamination by eliminating
    the sharp interfaces where
    it initiates.

  BODY PROTECTION SYSTEMS:
    If P1S-B composite is used
    in protective suit applications
    (as derived in the thermal
    protection suit document),
    the layer closest to the
    body should be firm (structural,
    dry, comfortable in direct
    contact with the body).
    The layer facing the threat
    should be maximally hydrated
    (immediate evaporative response).
    The gradient between them
    ensures the composite flexes
    with body movement without
    delamination at the
    suit-body interface.

  ACOUSTIC PANELS:
    In data center noise management
    or bunker acoustic isolation,
    a gradient composite presents
    a continuously varying
    acoustic impedance to the
    sound wave.
    Each infinitesimal layer
    is a reflection surface,
    but because the gradient
    is continuous the reflections
    are distributed across the
    material thickness rather
    than concentrated at discrete
    interfaces.
    This produces broadband
    acoustic absorption rather
    than frequency-specific
    reflection from discrete
    interfaces.
```

## PART III: THE TEMPORAL DIMENSION

### 3.1 — The Release Profile Engineering

```
THE MOST UNDERAPPRECIATED PROPERTY
OF THE DUAL-STATE COMPOSITE:

  Single-state materials have a
  single release profile.
  Hydrated jelly: front-loaded,
  depletes quickly.
  Firm jelly: slow, sustained,
  depletes gradually.

  The dual-state composite can
  be engineered to produce
  any release profile between
  these extremes by varying
  the ratio, thickness, and
  geometric arrangement of
  the two states.

  This is release profile engineering —
  the same principle used in
  pharmaceutical drug delivery
  where active compounds are
  embedded in polymer matrices
  of varying density to produce
  immediate-release, extended-release,
  or pulsed-release profiles.

THE PROFILE OPTIONS:

  FRONT-LOADED BURST + SUSTAIN:
    Thick hydrated outer layer.
    Firm inner core.
    Application: acute odor event
    treatment followed by chronic
    maintenance. Or: immediate
    thermal protection on event
    onset followed by sustained
    protection through the event.

  UNIFORM SUSTAINED:
    Alternating thin layers of
    hydrated and firm in equal
    thickness.
    Application: any application
    requiring consistent chemistry
    delivery over a long period
    without a pronounced initial burst.

  SLOW-START SUSTAINED:
    Firm outer layer.
    Hydrated inner layer.
    Firm backing.
    The hydrated inner layer
    is accessed only after the
    outer firm layer has partially
    depleted.
    Application: protection systems
    where the initial exposure
    is handled by the outer firm
    layer and the hydrated layer
    is held in reserve for
    sustained protection after
    the outer layer is depleted.
    This is a reserve activation
    profile — the secondary
    capability is held until needed.

  PULSED:
    Multiple alternating layers
    of unequal thickness —
    thin hydrated between thick firm.
    Each thin hydrated layer
    is rapidly depleted when exposed,
    then the next thick firm layer
    provides sustained delivery
    before the next thin hydrated
    layer is reached.
    Application: multi-event protection
    where each pulse of activity
    is followed by a sustained
    maintenance phase.

THE TEMPORAL ENGINEERING PRINCIPLE:

  The geometry of the composite
  IS the release schedule.
  To change the release profile:
  change the geometry.
  No chemical modification required.
  No new compounds.
```

### 3.2 — The Depletion Cascade

```
THE DEPLETION CASCADE IS A DESIGNED
FEATURE, NOT A FAILURE MODE:

  In single-state systems,
  depletion is uniform.
  The material depletes from the
  outside in. When the surface
  dries, the surface stops working.
  The only question is how fast.

  In the dual-state composite,
  depletion is sequential and
  structured. Each state depletes
  on its own timeline. The transition
  from one state's depletion to
  the next is not a failure —
  it is a stage transition.

THE CASCADE SEQUENCE IN
CORE-JACKET ARCHITECTURE:

  T=0 (deployment):
    Hydrated jacket is fully active.
    High surface moisture.
    High immediate chemistry delivery.
    Firm core is dormant —
    its moisture is polymer-bound,
    not yet accessible at the surface.

  T=hours to days:
    Hydrated jacket progressively depletes.
    Surface moisture decreasing.
    Chemistry delivery rate declining.
    The firm core's outer layer
    begins slow-release as the
    jacket moisture equilibrates
    with the drying surface.

  T=days to weeks:
    Hydrated jacket fully depleted
    or dried to near-firm state.
    The composite surface now looks
    and behaves like firm jelly.
    The original jacket material
    has dried into a thin crust —
    not inert, still chemically
    active but at low rate.
    The firm core is now the
    primary delivery vehicle.
    Sustained slow release continues.

  T=weeks to months:
    Firm core progressively depletes
    from the outside in.
    Sustained chemistry maintained
    at low but consistent rate.
    The system does not stop
    working when the hydrated
    jacket depletes.
    It transitions to a different
    delivery rate.

  T=depletion endpoint:
    Both states fully dried.
    Residual bentonite/borax/SAP
    matrix remains — not inert.
    Borax is still present and
    still antimicrobial.
    Bentonite is still present
    and still adsorptive.
    The dried composite retains
    passive chemistry at low rate.
    It is not dead. It is slow.
    Rehydration restores it
    to near-original function.

THE REHYDRATION RESTORATION:

  This is the property that
  distinguishes the dual-state
  composite from all conventional
  materials in protective applications:

  When water is added to the
  depleted composite:
    SAP polymer re-absorbs water.
    Firm blocks re-swell to
    near-original jelly state.
    The dried jacket crust
    re-hydrates and the interface
    layer re-activates.
    The composite returns to
    active state.

  THE COMPOSITE IS SELF-RESTORABLE.
  Conventional blast suppression
  materials (sandbags, rigid panels,
  foam) do not restore after depletion.
  They are replaced.
  The dual-state composite is
  recharged with water.
  The mineral matrix is permanent.
  Only the water is consumed.
  Water is the cheapest possible input.

  IN THE THERMODYNAMIC CLOSED LOOP
  CONTEXT (from the prior derivation):
  The water evaporated from the
  composite during operation is
  recovered by the closed loop
  condenser and returned to the
  composite for rehydration.
  The system recharges itself
  from its own evaporation.
  The depletion cascade runs.
  The closed loop reverses it.
  The composite cycles between
  depleted and recharged states
  continuously — the cycling
  IS the thermodynamic engine.

  This integration was implicit
  in the thermodynamic derivation.
  The dual-state composite makes
  it explicit: the material is
  designed to deplete and recharge
  cyclically, and the cycling
  is the operational mode,
  not a failure to be avoided.
```

### 3.3 — Time-Locked Activation

```
THE SLOW-START ARCHITECTURE
REVISITED AS A DESIGN TOOL:

  The slow-start profile derived
  in 3.1 — firm outer, hydrated
  inner, firm backing — does more
  than extend the release timeline.

  It creates a time-locked activation:
  a secondary capability that is
  physically inaccessible until
  the primary layer has been
  consumed.

  This has a specific strategic value
  in protective applications:

THE MILITARY EOD APPLICATION:

  An IED is encapsulated in a
  dual-state composite system
  for controlled detonation.
  The outer firm layer provides
  structural containment and
  initial blast attenuation.

  If the device detonates prematurely
  before the encapsulation team
  is ready:
    The outer firm layer absorbs
    the initial blast.
    The heat pulse of the detonation
    is absorbed by the outer firm
    layer's sustained evaporation.
    The hydrated inner layer,
    shielded by the outer firm layer,
    is exposed as the outer layer
    is consumed by the event.
    The hydrated inner layer then
    provides a second stage of
    thermal and chemical suppression
    for any secondary combustion
    or residual thermal activity
    following the initial detonation.

  THE COMPOSITE RESPONDS IN TWO STAGES
  THAT ARE TRIGGERED IN SEQUENCE
  BY THE EVENT ITSELF.
  No active control system required.
  No sensors. No actuation.
  The event consumes the outer layer
  and exposes the inner layer.
  The inner layer responds to
  what the outer layer did not
  fully absorb.

  This is passive sequential response —
  a multi-stage protection system
  with no moving parts, triggered
  by the threat itself.

THE BUNKER THERMAL PROTECTION APPLICATION:

  In a sustained fire event,
  a bunker wall panel with
  slow-start architecture:

  STAGE 1 (early fire — low heat flux):
    Outer firm layer provides
    sustained slow-release thermal
    absorption. No burst evaporation.
    The surface does not wet —
    it evaporates slowly and
    continuously, maintaining
    a cool surface temperature
    without obvious visible activity.
    To an observer, nothing appears
    to be happening.

  STAGE 2 (fire intensifies — high heat flux):
    Outer firm layer depletion
    accelerates under increased flux.
    As the outer layer dries,
    the hydrated inner layer is
    approached.
    The transition to the hydrated
    layer occurs precisely when
    the thermal load exceeds what
    the firm layer alone can manage.
    The hydrated layer activates
    at the moment it is needed —
    not before, not after.
    High-rate evaporation begins.
    Visible steam at the surface.
    Maximum thermal absorption rate.

  STAGE 3 (fire maintained — sustained flux):
    Hydrated layer depleted.
    Second firm layer exposed
    (if sandwich architecture).
    Sustained slow-release resumes.

  THE COMPOSITE SELF-MODULATES
  ITS RESPONSE RATE TO MATCH
  THE THERMAL LOAD.
  Low load: low response rate
  (firm layer, slow evaporation).
  High load: high response rate
  (hydrated layer activated,
  burst evaporation).
  The material is a passive
  load-responsive system.
  The response is hardwired
  into the geometry.
  No control system. No sensors.
  No actuation.
  Geometry IS the control system.
```

### 4.1 — Blast Suppression: Dual-State Application

```
THE SINGLE-STATE LIMITATION:

  Firm jelly alone in a blast
  suppression panel:
    Good structural mass.
    Good sustained thermal absorption.
    LIMITATION: inter-block air gaps
    create acoustic discontinuities
    that allow pressure wave
    channelling between blocks.
    The wave finds the gaps and
    passes through them preferentially,
    bypassing the attenuating mass.

  Hydrated jelly alone:
    Good gap-filling.
    Good surface conformity.
    LIMITATION: lower structural
    integrity, higher extrusion risk
    through mesh apertures under
    blast loading, faster depletion
    reduces sustained protection.

THE DUAL-STATE SOLUTION:

  Firm blocks as the primary
  structural mass.
  Hydrated jelly as the gap-filling
  mortar between firm blocks.

  THE CRITICAL RESULT:
  Air gaps are eliminated.
  The pressure wave propagates
  through a continuous semi-solid
  medium — the acoustic properties
  of water — not through a
  stack of discrete blocks
  separated by air.

  The attenuation achieved by
  the dual-state composite is
  closer to the theoretical
  maximum for water-equivalent media
  than either state alone achieves
  because the acoustic continuity
  of water is the source of
  water's suppression efficiency.

  Air gaps are the primary
  engineering failure mode
  of all current sand and
  gel-based suppression systems.
  The hydrated jelly mortar
  eliminates this failure mode
  in the firm jelly structural system.

PANEL CONSTRUCTION PROTOCOL:

  1. Pre-cut firm jelly blocks:
     200mm × 200mm × 75mm.
  2. Prepare hydrated jelly in
     dispenser bag (like a
     pastry bag or caulk gun format).
  3. Place first layer of firm blocks
     in mesh containment frame.
  4. Pipe hydrated jelly into all
     visible gaps between blocks —
     top faces and side faces.
  5. Place second layer of firm blocks
     offset from first layer
     (like brick coursing —
     joints offset to prevent
     continuous channels through
     the panel depth).
  6. Repeat piped gap-filling.
  7. Continue to required panel depth.
  8. Close containment frame.
  9. Apply compression.
  10. Rest 2 hours — hydrated jelly
      flows into remaining micro-gaps
      under compression.

  RESULT: a structurally coherent
  panel with no inter-block air gaps.
  Behaves as a near-continuous
  water-equivalent medium for
  acoustic and thermal purposes
  while maintaining structural
  integrity from the firm block matrix.
```

### 5.1 — Manufacturing Both States in the Same Facility

```
THE PRODUCTION CHALLENGE:

  Producing two states requires
  two protocols.
  Two protocols at industrial scale
  requires either two production
  lines or sequential use of
  one line.

    Immersion blend into water.
    Rest 60 minutes.
    Output: firm jelly blocks.
    Cutting and portioning after rest.

  HYDRATED JELLY (3× SAP + ionic suppression):
    Mid-process SAP addition.
    Extended blending.
    Rest 90 minutes.
    Output: highly hydrated jelly.
    Portioning before full gelation.

  THE PRODUCTION SEQUENCING STRATEGY:

    STEP 1: Produce firm jelly batch.
    Cut into required block sizes.
    Store in sealed containers.
    Production time: ~2 hours per batch.

    STEP 2: Produce hydrated jelly batch.
    Keep in dispenser format
    (piping bags, dispensing containers).
    Use within 4–8 hours before
    it begins to equilibrate toward
    firm state.

    STEP 3: Assembly.
    Combine firm blocks and hydrated
    jelly in required architecture
    (core-jacket, sandwich, gradient).
    Seal immediately.

  THE HYDRATED JELLY HAS A
  WORKING WINDOW OF 4–8 HOURS.
  Assembly must occur within
  this window after hydrated
  batch production.
  The production schedule is:
  firm jelly in advance (can be stored),
  hydrated jelly on assembly day,
  assembly completed same day.

  AT INDUSTRIAL SCALE:
  Continuous production with
  firm jelly produced in morning shift,
  hydrated jelly produced in afternoon shift,
  assembly and packaging in
  late afternoon/evening.
  One production line operating
  in two phases.
  No duplication of equipment.
  Scheduling is the only complexity.

THE QUALITY CONTROL ADVANTAGE:

  Firm jelly quality is assessed
  and confirmed before hydrated
  jelly is produced.
  If a firm jelly batch fails
  the thixotropy check, it is
  discarded before any hydrated
  jelly resources are committed.

  Hydrated jelly quality is assessed
  and confirmed before assembly.
  If a hydrated batch has incorrect
  viscosity, it is discarded before
  any firm blocks are used.

  The two-stage production creates
  two quality gates.
  Neither state is wasted on
  assembly if the other fails.
  This is more efficient than
  producing a single composite
  batch where a failure at any
  point wastes all inputs.

## PART VI: THE SYSTEM-LEVEL INSIGHT

### 6.1 — What the Dual-State Composite Represents

```
THE GEOMETRIC STATEMENT COMPLETED:

  The two states are not competing
  versions of the same thing.
  They are different tools that
  happen to be made from the
  same ingredients.

  A hammer and a chisel are both
  made of steel and wood.
  A hammer used where a chisel
  is needed produces a worse result
  than either tool correctly applied.
  A chisel used where a hammer
  is needed fails entirely.
  Used together in the correct
  sequence, they produce results
  neither can produce alone.

  The dual-state composite is this:
    Firm jelly: the hammer.
    Structural, sustained, slow.
    Hydrated jelly: the chisel.
    Precise, immediate, surficial.
    The composite: both tools
    in one material, each active
    where and when its properties
    are required, passive elsewhere.

THE DEEPER PRINCIPLE:

  Every material property exists
  on a spectrum.
  The spectrum has two ends.
  Most applications need both ends
  at different times or in different
  spatial regions.
  Most material science tries to
  find the optimal single point
  on the spectrum for the
  dominant requirement.
  This always sacrifices performance
  at the secondary requirement.

  The dual-state composite abandons
  the search for the optimal
  single point.
  It instead places the two ends
  of the spectrum in the same
  system, spatially or temporally
  separated by geometry.
  The geometry determines which
  end is active where and when.

  THIS IS THE GENERAL PRINCIPLE:
  Not specific to P1S-B.
  Not specific to hydrogels.
  The general principle is:
    When a material must serve
    two requirements that conflict
    on a single property spectrum,
    spatially or temporally separate
    two versions of the material —
    one optimised for each requirement —
    and let geometry determine
    which version is active.

  P1S-B is the specific case
  where this principle can be
  applied at low cost because:
    Both states are made from
    the same ingredients.
    The difference between states
    is a single parameter: SAP ratio.
    Adjusting SAP ratio is trivial
    in production.
    The principle generalises
    to any SAP-based hydrogel system.
    P1S-B is the first instance.
    It will not be the last.
```

### 7.1 — The N-State System

```
THE BINARY HAS BEEN DERIVED.
THE QUESTION IS: WHY STOP AT TWO?

  Firm jelly and hydrated jelly
  are two points on the SAP
  concentration spectrum.
  The spectrum is continuous.
  Any point on the spectrum
  is producible by adjusting
  the SAP ratio.

  A THREE-STATE SYSTEM:

    STATE A — ULTRA-FIRM (3× SAP):
    Near-solid. Minimal free water.
    Almost no evaporation.
    Maximum structural integrity.
    Maximum sustained release duration
    (weeks to months).
    Analogous to: bedrock.
    Use: deepest structural layer,
    outermost blast-facing surface
    where structural integrity
    under overpressure is the
    primary requirement.

    STATE B — FIRM (2× SAP):
    Current firm jelly definition.
    Balanced structure and release.
    Analogous to: dense soil.
    Use: primary body layer.

    STATE C — HYDRATED (1× SAP):
    Current hydrated jelly definition.
    Low structure, high evaporation.
    Analogous to: wet clay.
    Use: surface interface layer.

    STATE D — FLUID GEL (0.5× SAP):
    Barely gelled — thick viscous
    liquid. Maximum free water.
    Maximum immediate evaporation.
    Flows slowly under pressure.
    Analogous to: thick mud.
    Use: gap-filling mortar,
    immediate surface burst layer,
    pump-able working fluid.

  FOUR STATES, ONE MATERIAL SYSTEM,
  ONE INGREDIENT SET.
  The architecture designer selects
  which states go where based on
  the spatial and temporal
  requirements of the application.

THE GRADIENT AS A LIMITING CASE:

  When N states become large,
  the discrete stack approaches
  a continuous gradient.
  A continuous gradient from
  ultra-firm (centre) to fluid
  gel (surface) is achievable
  by controlled production:
    Fill mould with fluid gel first.
    Allow partial gelation.
    Add hydrated jelly layer.
    Allow partial gelation.
    Add firm jelly layer.
    Allow partial gelation.
    Add ultra-firm layer.
    Allow full gelation.
  The boundaries between layers
  are not sharp — each layer
  partially diffuses into the
  adjacent layer during gelation.
  The result is a gradient,
  not a stack of discrete layers.

  THE GRADIENT HAS A SPECIFIC ADVANTAGE
  OVER THE DISCRETE STACK:
    No interface failures.
    A discrete interface between
    states is a mechanical weakness —
    the modulus mismatch between
    firm and hydrated creates a
    stress concentration at the
    interface under load.
    A gradient eliminates the
    discrete interface.
    Modulus changes continuously.
    No stress concentration.
    No delamination failure mode.

  THIS IS THE SAME PRINCIPLE USED IN
  FUNCTIONALLY GRADED MATERIALS (FGM)
  IN AEROSPACE AND BIOMEDICAL ENGINEERING:
    Gradients in composition or
    microstructure to eliminate
    interface failures in
    high-performance applications.
  P1S-B dual (and N-state) composites
  are a low-cost, low-technology
  implementation of the same
  FGM principle.
  FGMs typically require expensive
  manufacturing processes
  (sintering, CVD, additive manufacturing).
  P1S-B gradients require only
  sequential pour-and-wait.
  Kitchen-table FGM.
  The principle does not change
  because the process is simple.
```

### 7.2 — Anisotropic Composite Architecture

```
THE CURRENT DERIVATION IS ISOTROPIC:
  Properties are the same in all directions.
  A core-jacket or sandwich structure
  has rotational symmetry around
  the deposition axis.
  The same properties in X and Y.

THE ANISOTROPIC EXTENSION:

  Some applications require different
  properties in different directions.
  Not just through the thickness —
  also along the surface.

  CASE 1 — DIRECTIONAL BLAST:
    In a bunker wall facing a
    known threat direction,
    the primary threat comes from
    outside the wall (perpendicular
    to the surface).
    But blast overpressure also
    travels along the wall surface
    through wall joints and gaps
    (parallel to the surface).
    An isotropic panel addresses
    the perpendicular threat equally
    in all directions but does
    not specifically address
    the parallel threat path.

    AN ANISOTROPIC PANEL:
    Firm jelly columns oriented
    perpendicular to the surface
    (parallel to the primary
    threat direction).
    Hydrated jelly filling the
    spaces between columns
    (filling the parallel-to-surface
    void that would otherwise
    be an air gap).
    The firm columns provide
    structural resistance along
    the threat axis.
    The hydrated fill eliminates
    acoustic channels along
    the surface.
    The composite resists the
    primary threat with structural
    elements and prevents
    flanking paths with gap fill.

    MANUFACTURE:
    Produce a mesh containment
    frame with internal vertical
    dividers spaced 50–100mm apart.
    Fill alternate cells with
    firm jelly blocks.
    Fill intervening cells
    with hydrated jelly.
    The dividers maintain
    the column geometry while
    the hydrated jelly gels.
    Remove dividers after gelation
    (or leave in place if the
    divider material is compatible —
    thin mesh dividers remain
    as structural reinforcement).

  CASE 2 — DIRECTIONAL HEAT FLOW:
    In a thermal protection suit,
    heat flows primarily from
    outside to inside (perpendicular
    to the suit surface).
    Evaporation should be maximum
    in this direction.
    But the suit must also flex —
    movement occurs parallel
    to the surface.
    A material that is stiff
    in the through-thickness direction
    (resisting heat flow efficiently)
    and flexible in the
    along-surface direction
    (allowing body movement)
    is the ideal.

    AN ANISOTROPIC SUIT PANEL:
    Hydrated jelly ribbons oriented
    perpendicular to the suit surface
    (like fins — presenting maximum
    evaporative surface area
    in the direction of heat flow).
    Firm jelly matrix surrounding
    and connecting the ribbons
    (providing structural integrity
    and flexibility for the
    along-surface direction).
    The ribbons are the thermal
    conductors in the threat direction.
    The firm matrix is the
    structural element for movement.

    This is equivalent to a
    heat sink fin architecture —
    fins oriented toward the
    heat source for maximum
    surface area, structural
    base providing mechanical
    integrity — but implemented
    in the P1S-B composite
    without any machining or
    manufacturing complexity.

  CASE 3 — CHANNELLED EVAPORATION
  FOR ENERGY ARRAYS:
    In a thermodynamic evaporation
    array, maximum evaporation
    requires maximum surface area.
    A flat horizontal surface has
    one-dimensional evaporation
    (upward only).
    A channelled surface has
    three-dimensional evaporation —
    upward from the top AND
    sideward from the channel walls.
    AN ANISOTROPIC EVAPORATION ARRAY:
    Firm jelly forms the structural
    base and channel walls.
    Hydrated jelly fills the
    channel interiors.
    The channels are oriented
    to direct air flow from
    the HVAC or wind over the
    maximum hydrated surface area.
    Evaporation occurs from:
      Top surface of hydrated fill.
      Both sidewalls of each channel
      (hydrated surface exposed
      to airflow on both sides).
      Bottom of channel (slowly,
      as the firm base releases
      moisture upward into the
      hydrated fill above it).

    SURFACE AREA MULTIPLIER:
    A flat surface has 1m² of
    evaporation surface per 1m²
    of floor area.
    A channelled surface with
    channel depth D and channel
    width W and wall spacing S:
    Effective evaporation surface
    = 1 + 2D/(W+S) per m² floor area.
    At D=100mm, W=20mm, S=10mm:
    = 1 + 200/30 = 7.67×
    multiplier over flat surface.

    THE SAME FLOOR AREA PRODUCES
    7.67× MORE EVAPORATION.
    7.67× MORE VAPOR.
    7.67× MORE ENERGY EXTRACTION.
    FROM THE SAME FOOTPRINT.

    This is the surface area geometry
    principle used in heat exchangers,
    catalytic converters, and
    biological structures (lung alveoli,
    intestinal villi) — maximise
    surface area within a fixed volume.
    P1S-B channel architecture
    applies this principle to the
    evaporative energy array.

    MANUFACTURE:
    Produce a mould with channel
    dividers at the required spacing.
    Pour firm jelly into the mould.
    Allow to gel around the dividers.
    Remove dividers — the channels
    are defined by the firm jelly walls.
    Fill channels with hydrated jelly.
    Allow to gel in channels.
    The resulting panel has firm
    walls and hydrated fill —
    anisotropic composite with
    the surface area multiplier
    of the channel geometry.

THE ANISOTROPIC PRINCIPLE — GENERAL:

  The insight that applies across
  all three cases:

    When a single-direction threat
    or function is dominant,
    orient the composite geometry
    to maximise performance
    in that direction specifically.
    Do not waste material
    performing equally in all
    directions when only one
    direction is critical.

  This is the efficiency gain
  of anisotropy over isotropy:
  the same material volume
  performs better in the
  critical direction because
  it is no longer divided
  equally across all directions.

  In every case, the anisotropy
  is achieved by geometry alone.
  Not by chemistry.
  Not by new materials.
  By the spatial arrangement
  of firm and hydrated states
  that are already defined
  and producible.

  The production complexity
  is the complexity of the mould
  or containment geometry.
  The material is unchanged.
  The performance improvement
  is architectural.
```

### 7.3 — Dynamic State Transition as an Active System

THE PRIOR DERIVATION TREATED STATE
AS FIXED AT PRODUCTION:
