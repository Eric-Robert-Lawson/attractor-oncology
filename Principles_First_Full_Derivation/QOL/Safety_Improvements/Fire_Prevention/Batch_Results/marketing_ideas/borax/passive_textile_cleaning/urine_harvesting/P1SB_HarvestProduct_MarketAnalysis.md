## PART IV: YIELD FAILURES

### 4.1 — What Reduces or Eliminates Harvest Yield

```
YIELD FAILURE IS DISTINCT FROM
MECHANISM FAILURE.
The mechanism works — that is established.
Yield failure means the quantity
or quality of the harvest product
is less than predicted.

YIELD FAILURE CATEGORY 1 —
INCOMPLETE DRYING:

  DESCRIPTION:
    The gel does not reach zero
    moisture content before harvest.
    The product is semi-solid or
    sticky rather than dry powder.

  CAUSE:
    High ambient humidity (>80% RH).
    Insufficient airflow.
    Too deep a gel layer (>5cm).
    Cold conditions (low vapour pressure
    differential).
    Sealed storage between drying and harvest.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Product mass is higher than expected
    (water retained = false mass).
    Nutrient concentration lower on
    dry-weight basis (diluted by residual water).
    Product cannot be bagged easily.
    Clumping during storage.
    Possible mould growth if stored wet.
    Shelf life dramatically reduced.

  MITIGATION:
    Fan-assisted drying (forced air).
    Spread gel to <3cm depth.
    Dry in covered but ventilated space.
    Measure moisture before bagging:
    squeeze test (bone dry = crumbles,
    too wet = compresses without cracking).
    Formal: moisture meter ($30–$80).
    Target: <10% moisture content.
    At industrial scale: spray drying
    or forced air tunnel eliminates this.

  SEVERITY: MEDIUM.
    Does not destroy product.
    Reduces quality and shelf life.
    Fully recoverable by extending
    drying time.
    In humid tropical climates:
    a real operational challenge.
    Solved by drying infrastructure.

─────────────────────────────────────

YIELD FAILURE CATEGORY 2 —
NITROGEN LOSS (PREMATURE DECOMPOSITION):

  DESCRIPTION:
    Urease activity partially overcomes
    borax inhibition, producing ammonia
    before the gel is dry.
    The ammonia escapes as gas.
    The nitrogen in the harvest product
    is lower than expected.

  CAUSE:
    Insufficient borax in the formula
    (under-dosing, measurement error,
    degraded borax stock).
    Gel left in high-temperature, high-humidity
    conditions for extended periods (weeks).
    Heavy bacterial contamination of
    the bucket from prior use.
    Borax stored improperly (borax is
    stable but avoid contamination).

  INDICATORS:
    Ammonia smell from the bucket.
    Strong ammonia = nitrogen being lost.
    Faint earthy smell = normal.
    Ammonia smell = yield failure in progress.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    N% of product lower than expected.
    Economic value reduced in proportion
    to N loss (N is the primary value driver).
    At severe N loss: product is a
    soil amendment without the N claim.
    Still valuable (CEC, water retention)
    but less so.

  MITIGATION:
    Do not under-dose borax.
    The formula is precise. 21g minimum.
    Use fresh borax (not decades-old stock).
    Monitor for ammonia smell.
    If detected: add additional borax
    to the surface of the gel (10–15g).
    A loose cover reduces surface
    ammonia loss even if inhibition
    is slightly imperfect.
    Store dry mix in sealed container —
    borax is hygroscopic and can
    cake if exposed to humidity,
    which may affect dosing consistency.

  SEVERITY: MEDIUM-HIGH.
    The primary value of the product
    is in its N content.
    N loss above 15% materially
    reduces revenue.
    First principles analysis shows
    this is unlikely with correct
    borax dosing.
    But it is the failure mode that
    matters most if it occurs.

─────────────────────────────────────

YIELD FAILURE CATEGORY 3 —
SAP SATURATION (OVERFLOW):

  DESCRIPTION:
    The SAP's absorption capacity
    is exhausted before the visual
    indicator triggers a cycle rotation.
    Free liquid pools in the bucket,
    potentially overflowing.
    Events occurring after SAP
    exhaustion are not absorbed —
    the liquid sits exposed.

  CAUSE:
    Higher-than-design urine volume
    per event (>400mL per event).
    More events than expected
    before indicator check (>18 events).
    SAP batch with lower absorption
    capacity than specified (quality
    variance in SAP supply).
    Urine with very high ionic strength
    (reduces SAP absorption ratio).

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Liquid overflow (messy, unsanitary).
    Unabsorbed urine exposed to air
    with reduced borax inhibition
    (diluted or not in contact with gel).
    Potential nitrogen loss from the
    unabsorbed fraction.
    Product volume is less than
    expected (some urine not captured).

  MITIGATION:
    Check the bucket more frequently
    in the early cycles to calibrate
    personal event volume.
    The visual indicator (pooling
    after event does not absorb
    within 5 minutes) is the correct
    early-warning.
    Do not defer bucket checks.
    At industrial scale: weight sensor
    or level sensor on the collection
    vessel triggers automatic rotation.
    Use SAP from consistent-quality
    supplier with specified absorption
    grade (>40× in deionised water).

  SEVERITY: LOW-MEDIUM.
    Self-indicating — the user sees
    the overflow before it becomes
    a major loss.
    Easily managed by attentive operation.
    At industrial scale: automated.

─────────────────────────────────────

YIELD FAILURE CATEGORY 4 —
CONTAMINATION OF THE PRODUCT:

  DESCRIPTION:
    Foreign matter enters the gel
    during the fill or drying cycle,
    contaminating the product.

  CAUSE:
    Faecal contamination (mixed
    defecation into the urine bucket).
    Chemical contamination (cleaning
    agents, pharmaceuticals in urine).
    Physical contamination (dust, debris
    in uncovered drying area).

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Pathogen risk from faecal contamination.
    Heavy metal or pharmaceutical residue
    in product from contaminated urine.
    Regulatory non-compliance.
    Product unsaleable.
    Potentially harmful to plants
    (some pharmaceuticals are phytotoxic).

  MITIGATION:
    The bucket is dedicated to urine only.
    Simple protocol: no defecation in
    the urine bucket.
    For commercial product: pathogen
    testing on representative batches.
    For pharmaceutical residue:
    the primary concern is users on
    heavy medication (antibiotics,
    chemotherapy).
    At community or industrial scale:
    batch testing is standard practice.
    At individual household scale:
    the risk is self-limited — the
    product is used on the same household's
    garden (the user is already exposed
    to their own pharmaceutical excretion
    through existing water systems).

  SEVERITY FOR COMMERCIAL PRODUCT: HIGH.
    Faecal contamination is the single
    most important quality failure for
    commercial regulatory compliance.
    The mitigation is simple
    (dedicated bucket) and reliable.
    Testing confirms compliance.
    This is not a systemic risk.
    It is a user-protocol risk.
    Protocol eliminates it.

─────────────────────────────────────

YIELD FAILURE CATEGORY 5 —
PHYSICAL LOSS DURING HARVEST:

  DESCRIPTION:
    Product is lost during the
    drying-to-harvest transfer.
    Product remains stuck to bucket walls.
    Product spills during packaging.
    Fine dust loss during handling.

  CAUSE:
    Gel dried too hard, adhering to bucket.
    Inadequate scraping/cleaning.
    Poor packaging workflow.
    Fine powder generated during drying
    lost to air movement.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    3–8% physical yield loss (as derived
    in principles document).
    Minor revenue impact at small scale.
    Meaningfully recoverable at scale
    (bucket wash, enclosed transfer).

  MITIGATION:
    Line the bucket interior with a
    removable liner (a simple plastic bag
    or dedicated liner product).
    Liner peels off with the dry product.
    Zero wall adhesion loss.
    At industrial scale: tumbler or
    vibrator loosens dry material from
    vessel walls. Automated.

  SEVERITY: LOW.
    Recoverable. Manageable.
    Liner solution costs <$0.10/cycle.
```

## PART V: QUALITY ISSUES
## AND MITIGATIONS

### 5.1 — Product Quality Variables

```
QUALITY ISSUE 1 — VARIABLE N%:

  THE ISSUE:
    The nitrogen content of the
    harvest product varies from
    batch to batch and person to person.
    Range: 2.9–12.7% N on dry weight basis.
    A buyer who needs a consistent
    product cannot rely on the
    harvest without standardisation.

  ROOT CAUSE:
    N% in the harvest is a function of:
    (a) N concentration in the source urine
        (determined by diet, hydration,
        metabolic state).
    (b) Total urine volume per fill cycle
        (determines dilution of dry mix).
    Both vary. The product therefore varies.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Highly variable product quality.
    Cannot label a guaranteed minimum
    N% without knowing the source
    urine composition and volume.
    Commercial fertiliser registration
    requires a guaranteed analysis.
    If the product varies 2.9–12.7% N,
    the guaranteed minimum is 2.9% —
    which is low (similar to a mediocre compost)
    and understates the typical product.

  MITIGATION:
    BLENDING:
    Harvest from multiple sources
    (multiple people, multiple cows)
    blended together reduces variance.
    Law of large numbers applies:
    a batch from 100 sources has
    much tighter variance than a
    single source.
    At livestock scale: 500 cows
    produce 500 independent observations
    that average to the herd mean.
    The variance is negligible.
    Single-source product: label
    "minimum guaranteed 3% N."
    Blended product: label "guaranteed 5% N."
    Industrial blended product:
    "guaranteed 6–8% N" is achievable.

    DILUTION/CONCENTRATION CONTROL:
    At industrial scale: adjust the
    dry mix ratio for the known
    urine concentration of the source.
    High-TDS urine (livestock):
    reduce dry mix slightly to concentrate
    N% in final product.
    Low-TDS urine (heavily hydrated humans):
    increase dry mix ratio.
    This is a production process variable,
    not a fundamental limitation.

─────────────────────────────────────

QUALITY ISSUE 2 — BORON CONTENT:

  THE ISSUE:
    The harvest contains 0.447% B
    from the borax component.
    Boron is a plant micronutrient
    at low concentrations and
    phytotoxic at high concentrations.
    The phytotoxicity threshold varies
    by crop: sensitive crops (some fruits,
    some vegetables) at 1–3mg/kg soil.
    Less sensitive crops: 5–10mg/kg.
    At standard application rates
    (100–200g/m²), the boron added
    per application is within safe
    limits for all but the most
    boron-sensitive crops.
    But repeated heavy applications
    could accumulate boron in soil.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Boron accumulation in soil with
    chronic heavy application.
    Risk to boron-sensitive crops.
    The label must carry application
    rate guidance and a boron advisory
    for sensitive species.
    This is not a recall-level issue.
    It is a label-level issue.
    Every trace-nutrient-containing
    fertiliser carries this caveat.

  MITIGATION:
    Specify application rate on label:
    100–200g/m²/year.
    Include boron content in guaranteed
    analysis (standard practice).
    Note crops with boron sensitivity
    (strawberries, beans, some stone fruits).
    If boron becomes a market barrier:
    wash the product with water to
    dissolve and remove some borax
    before drying (reduces B% from 0.447%
    to <0.1%). Trade-off: loses some
    antimicrobial benefit.
    OR: switch to a boron-free urease
    inhibitor (NBPT — N-butyl thiophosphoric
    triamide — used in commercial
    urea fertiliser). Higher cost,
    but eliminates the boron concern entirely.

─────────────────────────────────────

QUALITY ISSUE 3 — ODOUR AT POINT OF USE:

  THE ISSUE:
    When the dry harvest product is
    rehydrated by rain or irrigation
    after application to soil,
    residual organic compounds
    may release a transient odour.
    Not ammonia (borax inhibition preserved
    the urea; it does not become ammonia
    in the dry product — it slowly converts
    to plant-available NH₄⁺ in soil via
    soil urease, which is the intended
    slow-release mechanism).
    The odour is from other volatile
    organic compounds: creatinine,
    indole/skatole traces, short-chain
    organic acids.
    Brief, earthy-organic. Not persistent.
    Similar to applying fish meal or
    blood meal to soil.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Consumer acceptance issue for
    retail home garden buyers.
    "It smells weird when I water it."
    Not a health or safety issue.
    Not a regulatory issue.
    A perception and branding issue.

  MITIGATION:
    Label expectation management:
    "Earthy organic scent when wet —
    a sign of natural nutrient activity."
    Granulation process at industrial
    scale encapsulates the product
    in a low-odour outer layer.
    Activated bentonite adsorbs
    volatile organics — the product
    already has this character.
    In practice, the odour from a
    borax-inhibited, well-dried product
    is very mild.
    The issue is more theoretical
    than practical based on
    the chemistry of the product.

─────────────────────────────────────

QUALITY ISSUE 4 — SHELF LIFE AND CAKING:

  THE ISSUE:
    The dry product, if stored at
    high humidity, can reabsorb water
    (SAP is hygroscopic in moderate
    concentrations).
    Rehydrated product cakes, clumps,
    and becomes difficult to apply.
    Urea in the product can slowly
    convert to biuret (an inhibitory
    compound) at high temperatures
    over long storage.

  CONSEQUENCE:
    Short shelf life in humid storage.
    Physical caking reduces ease of use.
    Nutrient availability changes
    with extended or improper storage.

  MITIGATION:
    Moisture-barrier packaging:
    sealed polyethylene bag or
    laminated paper bag.
    Desiccant sachet inside bag.
    Storage in cool, dry conditions.
    At industrial scale: standard
    dry fertiliser packaging practice.
    This is solved by packaging, not
    by changing the product.
    Shelf life with proper moisture-barrier
    packaging: 12–24 months
    (consistent with other dry organics).

## PART VII: ROOM FOR ERROR

### 7.1 — The Product's Tolerance for Variation

```
THE HARVEST PRODUCT HAS NATURAL
TOLERANCE FOR VARIATION IN SEVERAL DIMENSIONS.

DIMENSION 1 — N% VARIATION:

  The product is a soil amendment,
  not a pharmaceutical.
  Soil amendments are expected to vary.
  Compost: 0.5–3% N.
  Manure: 0.5–2% N.
  Blood meal: 10–15% N (even this varies).
  The fertiliser industry accepts
  ±15–20% variance from guaranteed
  minimum on label.
  The P1S-B product labelled at
  "minimum 3% N" that actually
  delivers 5–8% N is not a problem —
  it is overdelivering.
  ROOM FOR ERROR: HIGH.
  Variation in N% is not a failure.
  It is normal for the product category.

DIMENSION 2 — HARVEST MASS VARIATION:

  414–722g per fill (derived range).
  The operator makes money on every
  gram of harvest.
  Less harvest = less revenue,
  but not a loss.
  The minimum viable harvest
  (414g at $5/kg = $2.07)
  still exceeds input cost ($0.153)
  by 13.5×.
  ROOM FOR ERROR: VERY HIGH.
  Even the low-yield scenario
  is highly profitable.

DIMENSION 3 — PRODUCT NOT FULLY DRY:

  A product with 15% residual moisture
  weighs 15% more than a fully dry product.
  The buyer gets 15% more weight
  for the same nutrient quantity.
  On a dry-weight nutrient basis,
  the product is slightly diluted.
  But: the seller is selling by weight
  (as all fertilisers are sold).
  The buyer gets 15% more product weight.
  ROOM FOR ERROR: MEDIUM.
  Does not damage the buyer.
  Does reduce nutrient concentration
  per kg sold — which matters for labelling.
  At 15% moisture, the label must
  state moisture content or adjust
  the guaranteed analysis to
  as-received basis.
  Standard fertiliser labelling practice.

DIMENSION 4 — PATHOGEN NON-COMPLIANCE:

  ROOM FOR ERROR: VERY LOW.
  This is the zero-tolerance dimension.
  A product that fails pathogen testing
  is not sellable commercially.
  A recall from a pathogen failure
  destroys brand equity.
  There is no "acceptable level" of
  E. coli or Salmonella in a
  commercial fertiliser product.
  MITIGATION: Testing, not tolerance.
  Test every batch or on a regular
  sampling schedule.
  The borax antimicrobial and full
  drying protocol is expected to
  produce consistently clean product.
  But expectation is not substitute
  for measurement.
  Test. Label. Sell.

DIMENSION 5 — BORON AT ELEVATED RATES:

  If the product is over-applied
  (>500g/m² on boron-sensitive crops):
  potential phytotoxicity.
  ROOM FOR ERROR: MEDIUM.
  Clear label application rate guidance
  prevents misuse.
  Most buyers will not over-apply
  organic soil amendment — it is
  expensive and heavy.
  The risk is theoretical for most users.
  Real risk for growers who
  mass-apply as a primary fertiliser.
  Mitigated by clear rate guidance and
  crop-specific advisory on label.

DIMENSION 6 — STORAGE FAILURE:

  Product stored wet, uncovered,
  or in humid conditions for months:
  mould growth, caking, nutrient degradation.
  ROOM FOR ERROR: MEDIUM.
  Standard moisture-barrier packaging
  eliminates this entirely.
  The failure mode only exists if
  packaging is absent or compromised.
  At household scale: any sealed container.
  At commercial scale: standard
  fertiliser bag.
  The error is in the packaging decision,
  not in the product itself.

OVERALL ROOM FOR ERROR ASSESSMENT:

  The product is forgiving in all
  the ways that matter commercially:
  yield, N%, moisture.
  It is unforgiving in one dimension:
  pathogen compliance.
  The single area where there is
  effectively zero tolerance for error
  is also the area most easily
  controlled by testing.
  The product can absorb significant
  variation in everything except
  what a $50 lab test addresses.
  That is a manageable risk landscape.
