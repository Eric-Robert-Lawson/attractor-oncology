## WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

```
This document derives and analyses
a class of inputs to the P1S-B
harvest product that have not been
previously accounted for:

  The passive, inevitable, non-designed
  contributions to the final dry
  harvest product that arrive through
  predictable biological and
  environmental processes.

These are not contaminants in the
harmful sense of the word.
They are the organic and mineral
materials that are inevitably present
in the urine stream and the operating
environment of the bucket system —
and that are captured by the SAP
and bentonite matrix along with
the urine itself.

The argument of this document:

  These passive contributions are
  predictable from first principles.
  They are largely benign or beneficial.
  They add mass to the harvest product.
  Several of them add genuine
  agronomic value that was not
  previously counted.
  One class of them — microbial biomass —
  may be the most commercially
  significant unexpected finding,
  representing an overlap with
  the premium biostimulant market.

The document:
  Identifies each predictable passive
  input by its biological mechanism.
  Estimates its quantity and composition.
  Derives its contribution to
  the harvest product profile.
  Revises the product quality assessment
  in light of these additions.
  Integrates the revised profile
  into the market analysis from
  the prior document, updating
  the pricing and positioning picture.

Nothing is invented here.
Everything is derived from what
is predictably present in and around
the human or animal body,
the urine stream, and a bucket
sitting in a domestic or farm environment.
```

## PART I: THE TAXONOMY OF PASSIVE INPUTS

```
PASSIVE INPUTS ARE DEFINED AS:

  Any material that enters the
  P1S-B gel system through predictable
  biological or environmental processes
  other than the intentional addition
  of dry mix and urine.

  They are predictable because:
  The processes that produce them
  are continuous and universal.
  Every human and animal sheds skin.
  Every environment contains bacteria.
  Every bucket sitting in air
  receives airborne material.
  The processes cannot be turned off.
  Therefore the contributions are certain,
  though variable in magnitude.

TAXONOMY:

  CLASS 1 — SHED EPITHELIAL CELLS
  (dead skin and mucosa)

  CLASS 2 — RESIDENT MICROBIOME
  (bacteria, fungi from body surfaces)

  CLASS 3 — FECAL TRACE MATERIAL
  (inevitable perineal contamination)

  CLASS 4 — AIRBORNE ORGANIC MATTER
  (dust, pollen, fungal spores,
  aerosol food particles)

  CLASS 5 — INSECT AND ARTHROPOD MATERIAL
  (dead insects, insect frass, chitin)

  CLASS 6 — FOOD AND DIETARY RESIDUE
  (urinary excretion of dietary metabolites,
  water-soluble food compounds)

  CLASS 7 — PHARMACEUTICAL AND
  XENOBIOTIC RESIDUE
  (drugs, metabolites, environmental
  chemicals excreted in urine)

  CLASS 8 — DISSOLVED ORGANIC CARBON
  FROM BODY SURFACES
  (sweat, sebum, amino acids from
  skin contact with stream)

  EACH CLASS IS DERIVED SEPARATELY.
  EACH HAS ITS OWN:
    Biological origin.
    Mass estimate per fill cycle.
    Chemical composition.
    Fate in the SAP/bentonite matrix.
    Agronomic or commercial significance.
```

## PART II: CLASS 1 — SHED EPITHELIAL CELLS

### 2.1 — Origin and Quantity

```
WHAT THEY ARE:
  Dead skin cells (corneocytes) shed
  continuously from the epidermal
  surface layer (stratum corneum).
  Also: shed epithelial cells from
  the urethra and surrounding mucosa
  during urination.
  These cells are primarily keratin
  (a protein) with lipid bilayer remnants.

QUANTIFICATION:
  Human shed rate: 0.5–1.5g dry weight/day.
  Of total body surface, the perineal
  and genital area contributes a
  small fraction — approximately
  0.1–0.5% of total body surface area.
  Per-event shed from urination stream
  contact zone: extremely small.
  Estimate: 0.1–0.5mg per urination event.
  Per fill cycle (15 events):
  1.5–7.5mg shed cells per fill.
  Rounding: ~5mg per fill.

  Additional airborne skin flakes
  settling into the open bucket
  between events (if indoors, bucket
  open for days):
  Indoor dust is 30–50% dead skin cells.
  Dust deposition rate in indoor
  environments: 1–10mg/m²/day.
  Bucket opening area: ~0.02m² (for a
  20cm diameter bucket).
  Deposition over 3-day fill period:
  0.02m² × 5mg/m²/day × 3 days = 0.3mg.
  Negligible contribution.

TOTAL CLASS 1 INPUT: ~5mg per fill.
  = 0.001% of 400g dry mix.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION: NEGLIGIBLE.
```

### 2.2 — Composition and Fate

```
KERATIN PROTEIN COMPOSITION:
  Carbon: ~52%.
  Nitrogen: ~16%.
  Sulfur: ~3.5% (high S — characteristic).
  Hydrogen: ~7%.
  Oxygen: ~22%.

  At 5mg per fill:
  N contribution: 5 × 0.16 = 0.8mg N.
  S contribution: 5 × 0.035 = 0.175mg S.
  Mass contribution: negligible.
  But: pure protein — high N%.

FATE IN THE MATRIX:
  Keratin is relatively insoluble in
  cold water (beta-sheet structure).
  It does not dissolve completely
  in the urine but is physically
  trapped in the SAP gel matrix.
  During drying: keratin remains
  in the product.
  In soil: keratin is slowly hydrolysed
  by keratin-decomposing bacteria
  (keratinolytic bacteria) — releasing
  amino acids and S as plant-available
  sulfate.

AGRONOMIC NOTE:
  Keratin is the basis of feather meal
  (a commercial fertiliser at
  $2–$5/kg for 10–15% N).
  The quantity here is microscopic.
  The mechanism of value is identical.
  No commercial significance at this scale.
  SIGNIFICANCE: NEGLIGIBLE.
```

## PART III: CLASS 2 — RESIDENT MICROBIOME

### 3.1 — Origin and Quantity

```
WHAT THEY ARE:
  Bacteria, fungi, and archaea from
  the human or animal body surface:
    Skin microbiome.
    Urethral microbiome.
    Environmental bacteria that
    colonise the bucket between fills.

  Urine itself is not sterile.
  It contains 10²–10⁴ CFU/mL from
  the urethral and perineal microbiome.
  The gel matrix, once established,
  provides a substrate for microbial
  growth during the fill period
  (days to weeks).
  The borax inhibits urease-producing
  bacteria but does not sterilise
  the system — it has antimicrobial
  activity but at the concentrations
  present it suppresses, not eliminates,
  microbial populations.

QUANTIFICATION:

  Bacteria per mL of fresh urine:
  10²–10⁴ CFU/mL.
  Per fill cycle total urine: 3,750mL.
  Bacteria in urine input: 3.75 × 10⁵
  to 3.75 × 10⁷ CFU from urine alone.

  Bacteria that colonise the gel
  surface between events:
  This is harder to quantify precisely.
  Bacterial dry weight: ~10⁻¹² g per cell.
  At 10⁸ cells in the gel (conservative
  surface growth estimate over the
  fill period):
  10⁸ × 10⁻¹² g = 0.0001g = 0.1mg.
  At 10¹⁰ cells (more active surface):
  10¹⁰ × 10⁻¹² g = 0.01g = 10mg.

  During the fill period (3–10 days):
  Ambient environmental bacteria settle
  into the gel.
  Gel surface is a nutrient-rich medium.
  Bacterial colony will establish and grow.
  Conservative estimate of total
  microbial biomass in gel at
  end of fill period:
  50–200mg dry weight per fill cycle.

TOTAL CLASS 2 INPUT: 50–200mg dry weight.
  Central: ~100mg per fill.
  = 0.019% of 531g harvest.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION: SMALL BUT REAL.
```

### 3.2 — Composition and Significance

```
MICROBIAL BIOMASS COMPOSITION:
  Protein: ~50–60% of dry weight.
  N in protein: ~16% of protein.
  N contribution: 100mg × 0.55 × 0.16
  = 8.8mg N per fill.
  Small N contribution.
  Not significant for NPK values.

  BUT THE SIGNIFICANCE IS NOT IN MASS.
  IT IS IN BIOLOGICAL FUNCTION.

THE BIOSTIMULANT MARKET CONTEXT:

  The global biostimulant market (2024):
  ~$3.5–4.5B/year.
  CAGR: 10–12%.
  Primary products:
    Humic and fulvic acids.
    Microbial inoculants (Bacillus,
    Trichoderma, mycorrhizae).
    Seaweed extracts.
    Amino acid hydrolysates.
  Premium products: $20–$100/kg.
  Microbial inoculant products:
  $15–$80/kg.

  THE P1S-B HARVEST CONTAINS LIVE
  OR RECENTLY DEAD MICROBIAL BIOMASS.
  The specific microorganisms present
  depend on the source environment,
  but certain categories are predictable:

  PREDICTABLE BACTERIA IN THE GEL:
    Bacillus species — extremely common
    in soil and skin environments.
    Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus cereus.
    Soil-applied Bacillus products
    are major biostimulants ($15–$40/kg).
    Their spores survive drying.
    They may be present and viable
    in the dried harvest product.

    Pseudomonas species — common in
    soil and skin. Some strains are
    phosphate-solubilising bacteria (PSB).
    PSB are sold as premium inoculants
    specifically to improve phosphate
    availability in soil.
    Market: $15–$30/kg for PSB products.

    Streptomyces species — common in
    soil and skin. Produce chitinase
    (degrades chitin). Also produce
    antibiotics that suppress soil
    pathogens. Premium biocontrol agents.

  PREDICTABLE FUNGI:
    Environmental fungi that settle
    into the gel surface.
    Penicillium, Aspergillus species.
    These are ubiquitous.
    Their relevance: phosphate
    solubilisation, organic matter
    decomposition in soil.

  THE CRITICAL QUESTION:
  Does the drying process kill
  the microbial biomass?

  ANSWER: PARTIALLY.
    Vegetative cells: killed by drying.
    Their lysate remains — protein,
    amino acids, cell wall components.
    Bacterial spores (Bacillus): survive drying.
    May remain viable in the dry product.
    Fungal spores: survive drying.
    May remain viable.

  IF THE PRODUCT CONTAINS VIABLE
  BACILLUS SPORES AT THE TIME OF SALE:
    The product qualifies as a
    biostimulant inoculant, not just
    a fertiliser.
    The regulatory path and price
    point change substantially.
    From $10–$14/kg fertiliser
    to $20–$50/kg biostimulant
    if microbial content can be
    verified and standardised.

  IF SPORES ARE NOT VIABLE OR NOT
  STANDARDISED:
    The lysed microbial biomass still
    provides plant-available amino acids
    and peptides — the basis of the
    commercial "amino acid hydrolysate"
    fertiliser market ($15–$25/kg).
    The biological material is not
    wasted whether alive or dead.
    Alive: inoculant value.
    Dead: amino acid hydrolysate value.
    Both represent market premium
    over basic NPK fertiliser.

THE MICROBIAL CONTRIBUTION IS THE
SINGLE MOST COMMERCIALLY SIGNIFICANT
PASSIVE INPUT.
IT CONVERTS THE PRODUCT FROM A
FERTILISER INTO A BORDERLINE BIOSTIMULANT
WITHOUT ANY ADDITIONAL PROCESS.
THE QUESTION IS WHETHER IT CAN
BE STANDARDISED AND DOCUMENTED.
```

## PART IV: CLASS 3 — FECAL TRACE MATERIAL

### 4.1 — Origin and Quantity

```
WHAT IT IS:
  Microscopic fecal contamination
  that inevitably occurs during
  urination due to anatomical proximity
  of the anus and perineum to the
  urine stream.
  This is not defecation into the bucket.
  This is the predictable trace transfer
  of perineal bacteria and microscopic
  fecal material that occurs during
  any urination event, regardless
  of hygiene practices.
  It is the reason midstream urine
  samples contain E. coli even
  in healthy people without UTI.

QUANTIFICATION:
  Contamination rate in midstream
  urine: 10²–10⁵ CFU/mL of fecal-origin
  bacteria (primarily E. coli,
  Enterococcus, Bacteroides).
  This is urine in the collection vessel.
  The bacterial cells themselves are:
  ~1 µm diameter cocci/rods.
  At 10⁴ CFU/mL and 3,750mL total urine:
  3.75 × 10⁷ fecal-origin bacteria.
  Dry weight: 3.75 × 10⁷ × 10⁻¹² g
  = 3.75 × 10⁻⁵ g = 0.0375mg.

  Non-cellular fecal particulate:
  Mucus, bile pigments, undigested
  fiber fragments in trace quantities.
  These are dissolved or suspended
  in the urine stream.
  Quantity per event: sub-microgram.
  Total per fill: <1mg.

TOTAL CLASS 3 INPUT: <1mg per fill.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION: ENTIRELY NEGLIGIBLE.

SIGNIFICANCE:
  The mass contribution of fecal trace
  material is negligible.
  The BIOLOGICAL significance was
  addressed in the quality risk analysis
  of the prior document —
  fecal contamination is a pathogen risk,
  not a nutrient contribution.
  At this quantity:
  E. coli present in the gel is
  not a food safety concern
  (the product is applied to soil,
  not consumed).
  It is a REGULATORY concern if the
  product claims pathogen-free status.
  The borax has bactericidal activity
  against gram-negative bacteria
  (E. coli, Salmonella) at the
  concentrations present in the gel.
  Complete kill is not guaranteed.
  Thermal drying further reduces
  viable populations.
  Lab testing confirms or denies.
  AS DERIVED PREVIOUSLY:
  Testing is the answer.
  The mass contribution is zero.
  The regulatory significance is real
  but manageable.
```

## PART V: CLASS 4 — AIRBORNE ORGANIC MATTER

### 5.1 — Origin and Quantity

```
WHAT IT IS:
  The bucket operates in an environment
  that contains airborne particles:
    Pollen (seasonal, outdoor or
    from houseplants).
    Fungal spores (ubiquitous indoors
    and outdoors).
    Airborne food particles (cooking
    aerosols, flour dust, etc.).
    Dust particulates (organic carbon,
    silicates, trace metals).
    Combustion products (indoors: candles,
    cooking; outdoors: traffic, smoke).

QUANTIFICATION:
  Indoor particulate deposition rate:
  1–10mg/m²/day.
  Outdoor: 10–100mg/m²/day.
  Bucket opening area: ~0.02m²
  (assuming loose cover used —
  if open, the full area is exposed).

  Over a 3–7 day fill cycle, indoors:
  0.02m² × 5mg/m²/day × 5 days = 0.5mg.

  Outdoor deployment:
  0.02m² × 50mg/m²/day × 5 days = 5mg.

  POLLEN SEASONAL CONTRIBUTION:
  Pollen counts peak at 500–5,000
  grains/m³ during spring.
  Pollen grain mass: ~10 nanograms each.
  Bucket exposed to 1m³ air per day:
  5,000 grains × 10ng = 50µg/day.
  Over 5-day fill: 250µg = 0.25mg.
  Seasonal peak. Negligible mass.

TOTAL CLASS 4 INPUT:
  Indoors: 0.5–2mg per fill.
  Outdoors: 2–20mg per fill.
  Central (mixed): ~5mg per fill.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION: NEGLIGIBLE.

COMPOSITION AND VALUE:
  Pollen: rich in protein (10–40% N),
  amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids.
  It is a complete micronutrient source.
  Pollen is sold commercially as
  a health supplement and bee feed.
  At 0.25mg per fill: agronomically zero.

  Fungal spores: chitin cell walls,
  ergosterol (fungal lipid).
  At this quantity: no practical value.

  Dust organic carbon: minor dissolved
  organic carbon input to the gel.
  Adds to the humic precursor pool
  in the final product.
  At sub-mg quantities: unmeasurable.

  SIGNIFICANCE: NEGLIGIBLE.
  The airborne organic matter
  contribution is scientifically
  interesting (the bucket captures
  a representative sample of the
  ambient biological environment)
  but commercially and agronomically
  insignificant at these quantities.
```

## ARTHROPOD MATERIAL

```
WHAT IT IS:
  Small insects, springtails, mites,
  and other arthropods that enter
  the bucket by accident or attraction:
    Fruit flies (Drosophila) attracted
    to organic compounds in the gel.
    Springtails common in damp
    domestic environments.

    insects present on or near the bucket).

QUANTIFICATION:
  In a domestic indoor bucket:
  1–5 small insects per fill cycle
  is a reasonable estimate.
  A fruit fly: 0.5–1mg dry weight.
  5 fruit flies: 2.5–5mg.
  Insect frass from 5 insects over
  the fill period: ~0.5mg.
  In an outdoor deployment:
  5–20 insects per fill cycle.
  10–20mg insect material total.

  In livestock barn deployment:
  Flies are ubiquitous in livestock
  environments.
  A barn with significant fly pressure:
  20–100 insects per fill cycle.
  20–100mg insect material.

TOTAL CLASS 5 INPUT:
  Domestic indoor: ~5mg per fill.
  Outdoor/farm: ~50mg per fill.
  Livestock barn: up to 100mg per fill.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION:
  Indoor: negligible.
  Outdoor farm/barn: small but real.
```

### 6.2 — The Chitin Finding

```
THE CHITIN DISCOVERY:

  Insect exoskeletons are ~25–40% chitin
  by dry weight.
  Chitin is a polysaccharide of
  N-acetylglucosamine.
  It is the same material that gives
  this product unexpected value.

  WHY CHITIN MATTERS AGRONOMICALLY:

  MECHANISM 1 — SYSTEMIC RESISTANCE:
    Chitin fragments (chitosan oligomers)
    are plant immune elicitors.
    Plants have chitin-binding receptors
    (LysM domains) that detect chitin
    as a "microbial/insect danger signal."
    On chitin detection: plants upregulate
    systemic acquired resistance (SAR)
    pathways — producing PR proteins,
    jasmonate signalling, phytoalexins.
    The plant becomes more resistant
    to fungal pathogens and some insects.
    This is an established mechanism.
    It is the basis of the chitosan
    biostimulant market.

  MECHANISM 2 — CHITINOLYTIC MICROBIOME:
    Chitin in soil selectively enriches
    chitinolytic bacteria and fungi
    (Streptomyces, Bacillus, Trichoderma).
    These organisms produce chitinase enzymes
    that degrade chitin — releasing N
    (chitin is ~6.9% N by weight).
    The chitinolytic community also
    produces antibiotics that suppress
    fungal plant pathogens.
    Chitin application to soil is an
    established biocontrol practice
    for suppression of Fusarium,
    Pythium, and Rhizoctonia.

  MECHANISM 3 — SLOW NITROGEN RELEASE:
    Chitin N is slow-release organic N.
    C:N ratio of chitin: ~8:1.
    Degradation timescale: weeks to months.
    Adds to the slow-release character
    of the P1S-B harvest product.

  CHITIN MARKET:
    Chitin and chitosan:
    global market ~$2B/year.
    Agricultural chitosan biostimulants:
    $15–$40/kg.
    Insect frass with chitin:
    $0.25–$0.70/kg bulk.
    High-chitin premium product: $5–$15/kg.

  QUANTIFICATION OF CHITIN IN HARVEST:

    Domestic indoor deployment:
    5mg insect material × 30% chitin
    = 1.5mg chitin per fill.
    In 531g harvest:
    1.5mg / 531,000mg = 0.00028%.
    Negligible.

    Livestock barn (flies, 100mg insects):
    100mg × 30% = 30mg chitin per fill.
    In 4,160g harvest (dairy cow):
    30mg / 4,160,000mg = 0.00072%.
    Still negligible by mass.

    THE MASS IS TINY.
    THE BIOLOGICAL SIGNALLING EFFECT
    IS NOT PROPORTIONAL TO MASS.

    CHITIN IS A SIGNALLING MOLECULE.
    Effective plant immune elicitation
    occurs at nanomolar concentrations
    of chitin oligomers at the root surface.
    The quantity needed to trigger
    plant immune responses is not
    gram quantities — it is microgram
    to milligram per square metre
    of root zone.

    At 1.5mg chitin per fill cycle,
    applied to ~0.5–1m² of garden:
    1.5–3mg/m² chitin application.
    Commercial chitosan applications
    for plant resistance: 0.1–10mg/m².
    THE HOUSEHOLD DEPLOYMENT IS
    WITHIN THE EFFECTIVE RANGE FOR
    PLANT IMMUNE ELICITATION.
    IT IS NOT DOSING THE EQUIVALENT
    OF A COMMERCIAL PRODUCT —
    BUT IT IS WITHIN THE BIOLOGICAL
    THRESHOLD FOR EFFECT.

  THIS IS THE MOST SCIENTIFICALLY
  INTERESTING FINDING IN THIS
  ENTIRE CLASS OF PASSIVE INPUTS.

  THE HARVEST PRODUCT, BY INEVITABLY
  CAPTURING INSECTS, DELIVERS A TRACE
  CHITIN DOSE THAT SITS WITHIN THE
  BIOLOGICALLY ACTIVE RANGE FOR
  PLANT IMMUNE STIMULATION.

  AT LIVESTOCK SCALE (BARN DEPLOYMENT):
  The chitin contribution from
  captured flies and insects is not
  microscopic. At 50–100mg per fill
  cycle and thousands of fill cycles
  per day per facility:
  the chitin contribution becomes
  agronomically measurable.
  This is not a commercial chitin
  product. But it is a meaningful
  trace biological component.
```

## PART VII: CLASS 6 — DIETARY
## EXCRETION BEYOND NPK

### 7.1 — Water-Soluble Vitamins

```
WHAT THEY ARE:
  Excess water-soluble vitamins
  are not stored in the body.
  They are excreted in urine.
  This is why B vitamins turn
  urine yellow when supplemented.
  Every person excretes measurable
  quantities of:
    B1 (Thiamine): 0.1–0.3mg/day.
    B2 (Riboflavin): 0.3–1.5mg/day.
    B3 (Niacin metabolites): 5–10mg/day.
    B5 (Pantothenic acid): 2–7mg/day.
    B6 (Pyridoxine): 0.1–0.5mg/day.
    B7 (Biotin): 0.01–0.05mg/day.
    B9 (Folate): 0.01–0.1mg/day.
    B12 (Cobalamin): 0.0001–0.001mg/day.
    C (Ascorbic acid): 20–200mg/day.
    (Highly variable — diet-dependent.)

PER FILL (assuming 1.75L/day excreted
over 15 events = 3 days' urine per fill):

  B-vitamin total: ~25–30mg per fill.
  Vitamin C: 60–600mg per fill.

FATE IN THE MATRIX:
  Water-soluble vitamins dissolve
  in the urine and are immobilised
  in the SAP gel.
  During drying at ambient temperature:
  most vitamins are preserved.
  B vitamins are relatively heat-stable
  at temperatures below 40°C.
  Vitamin C (ascorbic acid): oxidises
  slowly in air. Some degradation
  during drying.
  At industrial spray drying (high temp):
  significant vitamin degradation.
  At ambient solar drying: moderate degradation.
  Retained fraction at ambient drying:
  B vitamins: 50–80% retained.
  Vitamin C: 20–40% retained.
  (Ascorbic acid is notoriously labile.)

QUANTITY IN HARVEST PRODUCT:

  B vitamins retained per fill:
  ~15–24mg total B vitamins.
  In 531g harvest: 15–24mg.
  Concentration: 28–45mg/kg.
  Vitamin C retained: 12–240mg/fill.
  In 531g harvest: 23–452mg/kg.

AGRONOMIC SIGNIFICANCE:

  B VITAMINS IN SOIL BIOLOGY:
    B vitamins (especially B12, B1)
    are essential for many soil bacteria
    and algae that cannot synthesise them.
    Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a
    growth factor for many plant-associated
    bacteria and algae.
    B1 (thiamine) promotes root growth
    and is exuded by plant roots to
    attract beneficial bacteria.
    At the concentrations present:
    the B vitamin contribution adds
    to the biostimulant character of
    the product.
    This is not the dominant effect.
    But it is real and positive.

  VITAMIN C IN SOIL:
    Ascorbic acid in soil is an
    antioxidant and a reducing agent.
    It protects soil organic matter
    from oxidation.
    Plants synthesise and exude
    ascorbate from roots.
    Exogenous ascorbate can enhance
    antioxidant capacity of the
    rhizosphere — reducing metal toxicity
    and oxidative stress.
    A modest biostimulant effect.

TOTAL CLASS 6 (vitamins) INPUT:
  B vitamins: 15–24mg per fill.
  Vitamin C: 12–240mg per fill.
  MASS CONTRIBUTION: SMALL BUT REAL.
  AGRONOMIC SIGNIFICANCE: MINOR BUT POSITIVE.
```
