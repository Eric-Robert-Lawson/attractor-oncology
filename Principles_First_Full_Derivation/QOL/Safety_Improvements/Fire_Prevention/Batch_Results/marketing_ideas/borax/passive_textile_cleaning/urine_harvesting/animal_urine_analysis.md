## WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

```
This document derives the answer
to one question:

  Can the P1S-B dry ingredient system
  be applied to livestock and animal
  urine harvesting — and if so,
  which animals, at what scale,
  and what is the economic result?

The answer is yes.
The chemistry does not care whether
the urine came from a human or
a cow or a horse.
The mechanisms are identical:
  SAP immobilises the liquid.
  Borax inhibits urease.
  Bentonite adsorbs ammonium and phosphate.
  Baking soda buffers pH.

What changes between species:
  Urine volume per day.
  Urine dissolved solids concentration.
  The specific compounds present.
  The number of animals at a facility.
  The existing infrastructure for
  waste collection.

Some of these changes are minor.
Some of them are enormous.
The scale that emerges from applying
this system to livestock facilities
is categorically different from
the single-person household model.

This document derives all of it.

### 1.1 — The Data

```
SPECIES COMPARISON TABLE:

             VOLUME(L)   SOLIDS     (g/L)     (g/L)     (g/L)
             ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Human      1.0–2.0     20–50      8–14      0.5–2.0   0.4–1.3
  Dairy cow  17–45       25–40      8–14*     4–7       0.013–0.027
  Horse      5–15        20–60      6–12      2–4       0.1–0.3
  Pig        2–6         15–45      6–12      2–5       0.4–1.2
  Sheep      0.5–3       30–50      8–15      2–4       0.3–1.0
  Goat       0.5–2       30–60      8–15      2–5       0.3–1.0
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  *NOTE ON COW URINE NITROGEN:
  The total N in cow urine appears
  similar to human (8–14g/L) but
  the urea concentration is extremely
  high — 39–60g/L urea.
  This appears contradictory until
  the urea N fraction is calculated:
  Urea (NH2)2CO = 28g N per 60g urea.
  At 50g/L urea: N from urea alone
  = 50 × (28/60) = 23.3g/L.
  Total N is typically expressed
  differently in cattle literature —
  the 8–14g/L figure is for total
  Kjeldahl nitrogen in some studies,
  while the urea figure indicates
  a much higher actual nitrogen
  content by direct urea measurement.
  The urea figure is more reliable
  for harvest product calculation.
  Using urea at 50g/L midpoint:
  N per litre of cow urine: ~23g.
  This is 2× higher than human urine.
  The cow is a more concentrated
  nitrogen source per litre.

POTASSIUM NOTE:
  Cow and horse urine is dramatically
  higher in potassium than human urine.
  Cow: 4–7g K/L vs human 0.5–2g K/L.
  3–7× higher potassium concentration.
  Livestock urine produces a harvest
  with a significantly higher K
  component — shifting the NPK
  profile toward a more balanced
  complete fertiliser.

PHOSPHORUS NOTE:
  Cow urine is paradoxically LOW in
  phosphorus compared to other species.
  0.013–0.027g P/L (very low).
  Most phosphorus in cattle is excreted
  via faeces, not urine.
  Pig and sheep urine have more P.
  Horse urine is moderate.
  For a complete NPK product from
  cattle operations, a small fraction
  of processed manure solids could
  supplement the phosphorus fraction —
  but this is optional, not required.
  The product is still N+K dominant
  with trace P from urine alone.
```

### 1.2 — The Special Case: Horse Urine

```
HORSE URINE IS CATEGORICALLY DIFFERENT
FROM ALL OTHER SPECIES.

Beyond its NPK fertiliser value,
horse urine contains compounds with
pharmaceutical and industrial market
value not present in other species
at commercially relevant concentrations.

COMPOUND 1 — CONJUGATED EQUINE
ESTROGENS (CEE):
  Present in: pregnant mares specifically.
  Compounds: estrone sulfate, equilin
  sulfate, equilenin sulfate —
  estrogen forms unique to equines
  or present in much higher concentration
  than in other species.
  Commercial product: Premarin
  (Pfizer/Wyeth) — one of the
  most prescribed medications in
  history. Used for hormone replacement
  therapy (HRT) in postmenopausal women.
  The name "Premarin" = PREgnant MAre
  urINe.
  Market: pharmaceutical HRT market.
  Value: extremely high per litre
  of source urine.
  Current commercial model:
  PMU (pregnant mare urine) farms
  collect urine from confined
  pregnant mares continuously.
  The CEE extraction is the primary
  value — the remainder of the urine
  is currently treated as waste.

  P1S-B OPPORTUNITY IN PMU OPERATIONS:
    PMU farms currently discard or
    poorly utilise the non-pharmaceutical
    fraction of the urine.
    After CEE extraction, the remaining
    urine filtrate is a nitrogen-rich
    liquid with the same fertiliser
    potential as any horse urine.
    Applying P1S-B dry mix to the
    post-extraction waste stream:
    converts currently-discarded material
    into a harvestable fertiliser product.
    Zero additional feedstock cost
    (the feedstock is a waste stream
    of an existing operation).
    This is a pure margin addition
    to an existing pharmaceutical
    operation.

COMPOUND 2 — ALLANTOIN:
  Present in: horse urine at
  ~1–3g/L (high concentration).
  Also in: most other mammals except
  humans and higher primates.
  Use: cosmetic and pharmaceutical
  (skin healing, wound repair,
  moisturising, keratolytic properties).
  Present in commercial skin care,
  wound healing products, and
  some pharmaceuticals.
  Value: $15–40/kg commercial grade.
  Note: allantoin is non-volatile.
  It will be present in the
  P1S-B harvest from horse urine
  at the dried concentration.
  At 2g/L × 10L/day horse urine:
  20g allantoin/day per horse.
  Per 531g harvest equivalent:
  meaningful concentration present.
  A horse-urine P1S-B harvest could
  legitimately carry an allantoin
  content claim for cosmetic or
  horticultural applications.

COMPOUND 3 — HIPPURIC ACID:
  Present in: horse urine at
  10–40g/L (very high — the reason
  horse urine is cloudy).
  Other species: trace amounts only.
  Use: industrial chemical, research
  reagent, formerly used in
  synthesis of atropine and other
  alkaloids.
  Current use: limited industrial
  application.
  Not a primary value driver but
  adds to the total dissolved solids
  and therefore to harvest mass.
  Horse urine total dissolved solids
  (including hippuric acid): 20–60g/L.
  This is among the highest TDS
  of any common animal.
  High TDS = more harvest mass
  per litre processed.

COMPOUND 4 — CALCIUM CARBONATE:
  Horse urine is unique in its
  high calcium carbonate content.
  This is why it is cloudy and
  pasty rather than clear.
  The calcium carbonate adds
  to harvest mass and adds Ca
  as a soil nutrient.
  Calcium is a plant macronutrient
  often deficient in acid soils.
  Horse urine harvest has a calcium
  fraction not present in
  other species at this level.

HORSE URINE HARVEST PROFILE SUMMARY:
  N: high (from urea).
  K: moderate-high.
  P: moderate.
  Ca: high (calcium carbonate).
  Allantoin: present — cosmetic claim possible.
  Hippuric acid: present — adds TDS.
  TDS: 20–60g/L (highest range of
  all common livestock).
  Special value: highest-TDS harvest
  per litre processed of any species.
```

## PART III: THE EXISTING FACILITY ADVANTAGE

### 3.1 — Why Livestock Facilities Are the Optimal Deployment

```
THE FIVE STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGES OF
LIVESTOCK FACILITIES VS HUMAN APPLICATIONS:

ADVANTAGE 1 — URINE IS ALREADY COLLECTED:

  In confined dairy, pig, and poultry
  operations, urine is not scattered
  across a pasture.
  It falls on concrete floors,
  flows to drains, and is collected
  in manure pits, lagoons, or
  slurry tanks.
  The urine is already concentrated
  in one place.
  The collection infrastructure exists.
  It was built for waste management.
  It can be repurposed for value extraction.

  The human application requires
  behaviour change (use a bucket
  instead of a toilet).
  The livestock application requires
  NO behaviour change — the animals
  already urinate where they urinate.
  The change is only in what happens
  to the urine after it is collected.

ADVANTAGE 2 — SCALE IS FIXED AND PREDICTABLE:

  A dairy farm with 500 cows
  produces exactly 500 × 30L = 15,000L
  of urine per day.
  Every day. Reliably. Predictably.
  The supply chain has no variability
  in the feedstock.
  Production planning is straightforward.
  A human-based system has variability
  in participation rate, volume,
  and frequency.
  A livestock system has none of this.

ADVANTAGE 3 — URINE IS ALREADY SEPARATED
FROM FAECES IN MANY OPERATIONS:

  Modern dairy and pig facilities
  use slatted floors or liquid
  manure separation systems that
  partially or fully separate urine
  from solid waste.
  In dairy operations: urine flows
  through slats and is collected separately.
  This is the ideal feedstock —
  a urine-dominant liquid stream
  without the complications of
  mixed solid waste.
  The separation is already done.
  The facility built it to manage
  ammonia emissions and manure
  handling — which is the exact
  same problem P1S-B dry ingredients solve.

ADVANTAGE 4 — THE OPERATOR HAS
EXISTING WASTE MANAGEMENT COSTS:

  CAFO operators pay for:
    Manure lagoon management.
    Nutrient management plans (regulatory).
    Application equipment for spreading
    liquid manure on fields.
    Environmental compliance for
    nitrogen and phosphorus runoff.
    Ammonia emission mitigation.

  These are real costs.
  A P1S-B system that converts the
  liquid urine stream into a dry,
  storable, saleable product:
    Eliminates the lagoon management cost.
    Eliminates the liquid application cost.
    Reduces ammonia emissions
    (regulatory benefit).
    Converts a compliance cost into
    a revenue stream.

  The operator does not just gain
  a revenue stream.
  They also eliminate a cost stream.
  The total economic swing is
  revenue gained + cost avoided.

ADVANTAGE 5 — REGULATORY TAILWIND:

  CAFO operators are under increasing
  regulatory pressure for:
    Nitrogen and phosphorus runoff
    into waterways (Clean Water Act).
    Ammonia emissions (Clean Air Act).
    Nutrient management planning.

  Capturing urine as a dry product
  directly addresses all three:
    Nitrogen is harvested, not discharged.
    Phosphorus is harvested, not discharged.
    Ammonia is suppressed at source by borax.

  A P1S-B system at a CAFO is not
  just an economic opportunity.
  It is a regulatory compliance tool.
  Regulators want what the system produces.
  Operators want to avoid what the
  system eliminates.
  The alignment is structural.
```

### 3.2 — The Slurry Tank Application

```
THE KEY PRACTICAL QUESTION:

  In most livestock operations, urine
  is not collected in individual
  reservoir buckets — it flows as
  a liquid and pools in collection
  pits or slurry tanks that hold
  days or weeks of accumulated waste.

  Can P1S-B dry ingredients be applied
  to a slurry tank or lagoon rather
  than a bucket?

THE ANSWER IS YES — WITH SCALING.

  The mechanism does not require
  a bucket. The mechanism requires
  the dry ingredients to be present
  in the urine at the correct ratio.

  APPLICATION METHOD FOR SLURRY TANKS:

    METHOD A — IN-FLOW TREATMENT:
      Dry ingredients are mixed into
      the urine as it enters the
      collection system.
      A hopper at the drain point
      dispenses the correct ratio
      of dry mix per litre of urine flow.
      The SAP absorbs the liquid
      immediately at the point of entry.
      Borax inhibition begins immediately.
      The resulting gel accumulates
      in the collection area.
      Periodically removed and sent
      to the drying yard.

      ADVANTAGE: Inhibition is active
      from the moment of collection.
      Maximum nitrogen retention.
      No nitrogen lost in storage.

    METHOD B — BATCH TREATMENT OF
    EXISTING SLURRY:
      Existing liquid slurry in a
      collection pit has already lost
      some nitrogen to ammonia
      (borax was not present).
      Add dry ingredients to the
      liquid slurry in the correct ratio.
      SAP absorbs the liquid.
      Borax inhibits further decomposition.
      Prevents further nitrogen loss
      from this point forward.
      Allows conversion to dry product.

      LIMITATION: Nitrogen already
      lost before treatment cannot
      be recovered. The batch treatment
      captures remaining nitrogen only.
      In fresh or short-hold slurry
      (under 24 hours): nitrogen loss
      is 10–20%. Most nitrogen still present.
      In aged slurry (weeks in lagoon):
      nitrogen loss up to 40–60%.
      Batch treatment of aged slurry
      recovers only what remains.
      For maximum nitrogen retention:
      Method A (in-flow treatment)
      is strongly preferred.

  DRY MIX QUANTITY FOR SLURRY TANK:

    The ratio is the same: 106.7g dry mix
    per litre of urine processed.
    For a 10,000L slurry tank:
    10,000 × 106.7g = 1,067kg dry mix.
    Cost (industrial): 1,067kg × $0.385/kg
    = $410.78.
    Harvest yield: 10,000L × 32g/L TDS
    + 1,067kg dry mix
    = 320kg urine solids + 1,067kg
    = 1,387kg harvest.
    Revenue at $10/kg: $13,870.
    Net: $13,870 - $411 = $13,459
    from one 10,000L tank fill.

    OPERATING COST OF LAGOON VS P1S-B:
      Typical lagoon management cost:
      $0.01–$0.05 per litre of
      liquid waste managed annually.
      10,000L lagoon: $100–$500/year.
      P1S-B: costs $411 but generates
      $13,459 in product.
      The cost is not a cost.
      It is a $13,048 net gain on
      material that would have cost
      $100–$500 to dispose of.
```

```

THIS IS THE INDUSTRIAL OPERATOR MODEL.
IT IS STRUCTURALLY SOUND.

THE MODEL:

  A P1S-B processing operator

  They operate a mobile or fixed
  processing facility.
  They PURCHASE liquid urine or
  urine-dominant slurry from
  livestock operators at a low price
  (below the operator's current
  disposal cost).
  They apply dry ingredients.
  They evaporate the water fraction.
  They sell the harvest product.

THE ECONOMICS:

  OPERATOR BUYS:
    Liquid urine slurry from dairy.
    Current market: dairy operators
    PAY to dispose of this material
    ($0.005–$0.02/L for lagoon management,
    spreading, and compliance).
    Operator offers to TAKE IT FOR FREE
    or pay a small price ($0.001–$0.003/L).
    The dairy operator benefits:
    disposal cost eliminated.
    The processing operator benefits:
    free or near-free feedstock.

    Feedstock cost to processor:
    $0.001–$0.003/L or FREE.

  OPERATOR APPLIES:
    106.7g dry mix per litre.

    Harvest product.

    = 82% net margin per litre.

  AT SCALE:

    A processing facility handling
    1 million litres per day
    (approximately 33,000 dairy cows'

    Daily revenue: 1,000,000L × $1.39 = $1,390,000.
