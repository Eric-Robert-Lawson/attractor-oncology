## PART I: THE GAME-THEORETIC FRAMEWORK

### 1.1 — Understanding the Partner's Decision

```
A potential partner's decision to work with
you is NOT primarily a financial decision.

It's a TRUST AND OPTIONALITY decision.

Let me explain through game theory:

STANDARD BUSINESS DECISION (what you might
expect):

Partner thinks:
  "This person wants me to:
   1. Risk capital.
   2. Risk operational disruption.
   3. Risk my reputation.

   In exchange for:
   1. A promise of profit.

   I have no evidence this will work.
   They have no track record.
   They can't even fully explain the technology.

   The rational decision is: NO."

But that's not the decision you need them to
make.

You need them to make a DIFFERENT decision.

THE DECISION YOU NEED THEM TO MAKE:

Partner thinks:
  "This is a rare opportunity.

   The profit opportunity is enormous
   ($40M-$100M/year from one facility).

   The risk, while real, is CALCULABLE
   and MANAGEABLE.

   If this works (even at 20% confidence),
   my business transforms.

   If this doesn't work (80% confidence),
   my losses are bounded and manageable.

   The optionality value (possibility of
   transformational upside) exceeds the
   downside risk.

   More importantly: if I don't do this,
   someone else will.

   And I'll have missed the opportunity
   forever.

   The decision is: YES."

This is NOT an irrational decision.
It's a RATIONAL decision under UNCERTAINTY
with HIGH UPSIDE ASYMMETRY.

The key insight: Your job is not to convince
them it's certain.
Your job is to convince them it's WORTH THE
ASYMMETRY.

HOW THE ASYMMETRY WORKS:

Their downside:
  Capital at risk: $500K-$2M (retrofit,
  trial).
  Operational downside: 2-6 months
  of disruption while deploying system.
  If trial fails: sunk costs, learn lessons,
  back to baseline.
  Total maximum loss: $2M.

Their upside:
  If trial succeeds (and scales):
  $40M-$100M/year additional profit.
  If they get 30% of that: $12-30M/year
  additional profit.
  Five-year value: $60-150M.

The asymmetry:
  Downside risk: $2M.
  Upside potential: $60-150M.
  Ratio: 30:1 to 75:1 upside/downside.

At even 10% probability of success, the
expected value is positive:
  EV = (10% × $60M upside) - (90% × $2M downside)
     = $6M - $1.8M
     = $4.2M positive expected value.

At 20% probability of success:
  EV = (20% × $100M upside) - (80% × $2M downside)
     = $20M - $1.6M
     = $18.4M positive expected value.

This is not gambling.
This is portfolio management under uncertainty.

A rational decision-maker should say YES
if you can demonstrate:
  1. The upside is real (not fabricated).
  2. The downside is bounded (not unlimited).
  3. The probability of success is >10%
     (not 1%).
  4. The asymmetry is clear (upside >>
     downside).

Your job is to demonstrate all four.

THE PARTNER'S REAL CONSTRAINT (Not rational
analysis):

The partner's actual constraint is NOT the
financial decision.

It's THREE psychological constraints:

CONSTRAINT 1: TRUST
  "Can I trust this person?"
  - Are they competent?
  - Are they honest about limitations?
  - Do they have skin in the game?
  - Will they follow through?

CONSTRAINT 2: VISION
  "Do I believe in the vision?"
  - Do I understand what this enables?
  - Is it aligned with my business direction?
  - Is it something I want to own?

CONSTRAINT 3: DECISION-MAKER AUTHORITY
  "Can I make this decision alone, or do
   I need board/investor approval?"
  - If alone: one conversation.
  - If needs approval: they are already
    skeptical (they know approval is hard).
  - Your job is to make them confident
    enough to fight for approval.

These three constraints are the REAL barriers.

The financial analysis is secondary.

Your strategy must address all three.
```

## PART II: THE DISCOVERY AND OUTREACH STRATEGY

### 2.1 — Finding the Right Partner

```
You cannot cold-call facilities and say
"I have a primitive technology that will
transform your business."

You will get hung up on.

You need a WARM PATH to the decision-maker.

THE DISCOVERY PROCESS:

STEP 1: IDENTIFY YOUR GEOGRAPHIC TARGET

You need to be within 50-100 miles of the
partner facility.
This is for:
  - Demo visits (you need to show up in person).
  - Trial management (you need regular on-site).
  - Relationship building (trust is built
    in-person).
  - Scaling support (implementation requires
    your presence).

Pick a region with high concentration of
livestock facilities:
  - Iowa (pig and cattle concentration).
  - California Central Valley (dairy).
  - Texas (beef and dairy).
  - Wisconsin (dairy).
  - North Carolina (pork).
  - Missouri (cattle and pork).
  - Minnesota (dairy and pork).
  - Colorado/Wyoming (beef and dairy).

Pick the region where you have the BEST
existing relationships (even loose ones).

STEP 2: IDENTIFY YOUR TARGET FACILITY TYPE

First partnership should NOT be:
  - Mega-CAFO (1000+ animals, corporate-owned,
    slow decision-making).
  - Very small operation (<500 animals,
    marginal economics).
  - Commodity-focused only (no upside vision).
  - Recently purchased/under stress
    (distracted owner).

First partnership SHOULD be:
  - Mid-scale (5,000-20,000 animals).
  - Owner/operator (single decision-maker).
  - Mixed revenue streams (milk + meat + now
    byproducts).
  - Stable for 2-5 years (not in crisis, not
    hypergrowth).
  - Open to innovation (has made changes
    before).

STEP 3: RESEARCH THE OPERATOR

Once you've identified a target region and
facility type, identify SPECIFIC facilities
that match.

Sources:
  - USDA farm census data (publicly available,
    searchable).
  - State agricultural department registries.
  - Industry publications (dairy industry
    newsletters, cattle association magazines).
  - Agricultural lender relationships
    (bankers know who's successful).
  - Ag extension services (they know the
    operators).

For each candidate facility:
  - Learn about the operator/owner
    (age, tenure, background, family).
  - Learn about their facility (size, type,
    recent investments, current focus).
  - Learn about their business (profitable?
    growing? stable?).
  - Learn about their values
    (sustainability? innovation? efficiency?).

Research goal: Understand if they are
  a. Financially able to take on risk.
  b. Intellectually open to new ideas.
  c. Operationally capable of supporting
     deployment.

STEP 4: IDENTIFY YOUR WARM CONNECTION

Cold outreach to a facility has <5% success
rate.
Warm introduction has 50%+ success rate.

Identify a warm connection:
  - Do you know anyone in agriculture?
  - Do you know a banker who lends to farmers?
  - Do you know an ag extension agent?
  - Do you know a feed supplier?
  - Do you know a veterinarian?
  - Do you know another farmer who could
    introduce you?
  - Do you know a professor in an ag school?
  - Do you know someone who consults to
    farmers?

The warm connection needs to:
  - Know the target operator personally.
  - Be willing to introduce you.
  - Understand roughly what you're doing
    (not all the details, but the gist).
  - Vouch for you (credibility transfer).

THE WARM INTRODUCTION SCRIPT:

Your connector reaches out to the target
operator:

"Hey [Name], I have someone I'd like to
introduce you to. They've developed a new
technology that could potentially add a
significant new revenue stream to your
operation. It's unconventional, but the
economics are compelling. The person is
serious and thoughtful. Would you be open
to a 30-minute conversation to hear about it?"

This does three things:
  1. Primes the operator (expectation set:
     something new is coming).
  2. Positions you as serious (vouched for
     by someone they trust).
  3. Low commitment (30 minutes, not a
     binding decision).

If the operator says yes, you get a meeting.

If they say no, you move to next target.

(With a 50% warm introduction success rate,
you only need 2-3 targets to get your first
meeting.)
```

### 2.2 — The Pre-Meeting Research and Positioning

```
Once you have a meeting scheduled, you have
1-2 weeks to prepare.

This is the most important preparation you
will do.

THE GOAL OF PREPARATION:

By the time you walk into the meeting, you
know:
  - What their pain points are.
  - What their incentives are.
  - What their constraints are.
  - How to position P1S-B to address
    THEIR specific situation (not a generic
    pitch).
  - How to build TRUST (by showing you
    understand their world).

RESEARCH AREAS:

RESEARCH AREA 1: THEIR FACILITY AND OPERATIONS

Questions to answer:
  - What animals do they raise?
    (This determines urine composition,
     volumes, handling challenges.)
  - How many animals?
    (This determines scale of opportunity.)
  - What's their facility layout?
    (Slatted floor? Open pasture? Confined?
     This determines collection feasibility.)
  - What's their feed type?
    (This affects urine concentration.)
  - What's their current waste management?
    (Lagoon? Spreading? Composting?
     This is a cost you can reduce.)
  - What are their operational metrics?
    (Milk yield per cow? Pork yield per pig?
     This is baseline for measuring improvement.)

Sources:
  - USDA farm census data.
  - State dairy/livestock boards.
  - Industry publications.
  - Agricultural lenders (if you can get
    them to talk).
  - Inspection records (some are public).

RESEARCH AREA 2: THEIR FINANCIALS AND
CONSTRAINTS

Questions to answer:
  - Are they profitable?
    (If not, they're Type 2 distressed
     operator.)
  - What's their approximate revenue?
    (Number of animals × product value.)
  - What are their major costs?
    (Feed, labor, utilities, manure management,
     compliance.)
  - Are they growing or stable?
    (Type 3 growth-focused or Type 1 incumbent.)
  - What's their debt situation?
    (Heavy debt = more conservative, or
     more desperate depending on severity.)
  - What regulatory pressures do they face?
    (Nitrogen discharge? Ammonia emissions?
     This is leverage for you.)

Sources:
  - Public records (if corporate/public).
  - Industry benchmarks (published by
    agricultural schools).
  - Conversations with agricultural lenders.
  - State regulatory filings.

RESEARCH AREA 3: THEIR OWNER/OPERATOR

Questions to answer:
  - How old are they?
    (Younger = more open to innovation.
     Older = more skeptical.)
  - How long have they owned the business?
    (Longer tenure = more confident to take
     risks.)
  - What's their background?
    (Career farmer? Family business?
     Corporate ag?)
  - Are they active in industry associations?
    (Indicates sophistication and openness.)
  - What are their stated values?
    (Sustainability? Efficiency? Profit?)
  - Do they have a succession plan?
    (If kids are involved, might be more
     forward-thinking.)

Sources:
  - Industry association websites.
  - LinkedIn.
  - Local news articles.
  - Agricultural school alumni networks.
  - Conversations with people who know them.

RESEARCH AREA 4: THEIR RECENT DECISIONS AND
INVESTMENTS

Questions to answer:
  - What improvements have they made recently?
    (Shows they invest in operational
     improvement.)
  - Have they adopted new technologies?
    (Shows they're open to innovation.)
  - What was their rationale?
    (Understand their decision-making process.)
  - Did those investments work out?
    (Understand if they're risk-savvy or
     risk-averse.)
  - What is their stated next priority?
    (Is it growth? Cost reduction? Compliance?
     This tells you where P1S-B fits.)

Sources:
  - Industry publications.
  - Conversations with suppliers, consultants,
    or neighbors.
  - Observation during farm visits.

THE POSITIONING DOCUMENT (For yourself)

Create a 2-3 page document (not for them,
for you) that summarizes:

SECTION 1: THEIR SITUATION
  "Based on my research, [Name] operates a
  [size] [type] facility. Their primary
  incentive is [growth/cost reduction/risk
  mitigation]. Their pain point is
  [specific pain]. Their constraint is
  [specific constraint]."

SECTION 2: HOW P1S-B ADDRESSES THEM
  "P1S-B directly addresses their pain point
  by [how]. It aligns with their incentive
  by [how]. It respects their constraint by
  [how]."

SECTION 3: THEIR LIKELY OBJECTIONS
  "They will probably worry about [X], [Y],
  [Z]. I will address these by [approach]."

SECTION 4: YOUR POSITIONING
  "I am positioning myself as: [trustworthy
  technologist who understands the primitive,
  not as an ag expert]. I will establish
  credibility by: [method]. I will handle
  credentials gap by: [approach]."

SECTION 5: THE MEETING OBJECTIVE
  "My goal is not to close the partnership
  in this meeting. My goal is to: [specific,
  achievable outcome: get a demo scheduled,
  agree to trial scope, etc]."

This document is YOUR thinking. They never
see it. But it guides your every word in
the meeting.

## PART III: THE FIRST MEETING

### 3.1 — The Demo Meeting: Positioning, Demonstration, Trust Building

```
The first meeting is not a PITCH.
It's a DEMONSTRATION and CONVERSATION.

Your goal is NOT to convince them.
Your goal is to:
  1. Build trust.
  2. Show the primitive works.
  3. Position yourself as credible in your
     domain.
  4. Understand their constraints and
     incentives.
  5. Leave them wanting to continue the
     conversation.

THE MEETING STRUCTURE (90 minutes total):

SECTION 1: CONTEXT SETTING (10 minutes)

You begin by showing that you understand
THEIR world.

"Before I show you what I've developed,
I want to acknowledge what you've built
here. You operate [size] head of [animal],
you manage [specific challenges], your
business is [description]. You're doing
this in an environment where [regulatory
constraint], which is creating pressure
on [area]. You're looking to [their stated
goal]. That's the context I'm approaching
this from."

This does THREE things:
  1. Proves you've done homework
     (trust building).
  2. Shows you understand THEIR situation,
     not just your tech (credibility).
  3. Demonstrates you have respect for
     their expertise (humility).

If you get this wrong, they stop listening.
If you get this right, they're now engaged
because you clearly understand their world.

SECTION 2: YOUR POSITIONING (10 minutes)

"I'm not a livestock expert. I'm not a
dairy expert. I'm not an ag expert. What
I am an expert in is a new primitive
technology that I've developed. I don't
have a track record in your industry,
but I do have something that might be
valuable to you. Before I show you, I
want to be clear about my positioning:

I'm here to show you what's possible
based on a new technology. You're the
expert in [their industry]. If this works
for you, it works because YOU understand
how to deploy it, manage it, and scale it.
My role is to provide the technology and
to support the technical aspects of
deployment. Your expertise remains
critical. I'm not trying to replace your
knowledge. I'm trying to add to it.

Does that make sense?"

This does THREE things:
  1. Explicitly acknowledges the credential
     gap (honesty).
  2. Positions you as COMPLEMENTARY, not
     competitive (removes threat).
  3. Frames their expertise as critical
     (respect).

Most operators will nod. Some will test you
("OK, so what makes you think you can do
this without industry experience?"). If they
test, respond:

"Fair question. I've been building this
technology for [X years], and I've done
[Y demonstrations]. The technology is
principle-based, not industry-specific.
The [specific principle] is the same
whether we're talking about [animal 1] or
[animal 2]. What's different is how we
implement it in YOUR specific facility
and YOUR specific constraints. That's
where your expertise is critical, and
that's where the partnership works."

They will respect this answer because it's
honest and defers to them appropriately.

SECTION 3: THE DEMONSTRATION (30-40 minutes)

This is the core of the meeting.

You demonstrate the primitive.

Setup:
  - Bring sample urine (if possible) or
    simulate with saline solution.
  - Bring the dry ingredients (weighed,
    measured, labeled).
  - Bring a scale.
  - Bring containers for the test.
  - Bring documentation of the results
    (charts showing the transformation).

The demonstration:

"Here's how this works. I'm going to take
[amount] of urine, I'm going to add
[dry mix], and I'm going to show you
what happens."

Step 1: Weigh the urine (show them the weight).

Step 2: Add the dry mix (show them the
dry mix, the amount, explain what each
component does - but NOT the specific
ratios or the trade-secret formulation).

"This component [SAP] absorbs the liquid.
This component [bentonite] adsorbs
solutes. This component [borax] inhibits
decomposition. This component [baking
soda] buffers pH. Together, they transform
a liquid into a gel. The process is
driven by [principle], not by some
complex chemistry that only I understand."

Step 3: Show the transformation (the gel
forming).

"In about [X minutes], the liquid is
immobilized. Let me show you..."
(Wait if needed, or show with a prepared
sample).

Step 4: Let it partially dry (or show
results from a sample that's already dried).

"After [X days] of drying, we get a dry
solid. Let me show you the yield..."

Step 5: Weigh the final product.

"We started with [X] liters of urine.
We added [Y] grams of dry mix. We end
with [Z] grams of dry product. That
[Z] grams contains the nutrients from
the urine - the nitrogen, the phosphorus,
the potassium - all preserved and
concentrated."

Step 6: Show the analysis.

"I've had this product tested. Here are
the results: [show NPK analysis, show
that it's fertiliser-grade]."

Step 7: The financial implication.

"That [Z] grams of product, at
current market prices for specialty
organic fertiliser, is worth [$ amount].
Your facility produces this every day.
Do you see where this is going?"

The demonstration is MATHEMATICAL and
REPRODUCIBLE.

It's not magic. It's not hand-waving.
It's: add A, get B, measure B, quantify
the value of B.

A rational person watching this cannot
deny that it works at the lab scale.

SECTION 4: ADDRESSING CONCERNS (15-20 minutes)

After the demo, they will have concerns.
Let them voice them.

Common concerns:

CONCERN 1: "This works at small scale. Will
it work at my facility scale?"

Response:
"That's a fair question. And the answer is:
we need to test it. That's why I'm
proposing [trial approach]. We'll run a
small-scale trial in [location], measure
the results, and see if it scales. If it
doesn't, we've learned something for
[cost]. If it does, we know how to deploy
across your facility. The trial is the
validation."

CONCERN 2: "What about regulatory approval?
Can I even sell this?"

Response:
"Another fair question. The regulatory
pathway depends on [what you're claiming].
If this is a fertiliser, it follows
[pathway]. I've already begun the
research on [regulatory requirements].
Here's [document] showing [regulator
approval, or regulatory pathway]. I'm
prepared to support the regulatory
process with [specific support you'll
provide]."

(Note: Have actual regulatory research
done before the meeting. This is critical.)

CONCERN 3: "What about my existing
operations? Will this disrupt things?"

Response:
"The trial is designed to be isolated
from your main operation. We're setting
up [trial location] that's separate from
[main facility]. Your existing operations
continue unchanged. If the trial shows
promise, then we scale gradually. Your
milk production, your herd health, none
of that is at risk."

CONCERN 4: "What's in it for you? What are
you getting out of this?"

Response:
"Great question. Here's my interest:
I've developed a primitive technology
with potential applications across
agriculture. Your facility is the ideal
test case - you have [specific advantages].
If this works here, I can scale this to
other facilities and other applications.
I'm proposing [specific structure]:
[equity share/fee/royalty]. This aligns
our incentives. If you succeed, I succeed.
If this doesn't work, I learn valuable
lessons before approaching other
operators. So I have skin in the game
just like you do."

CONCERN 5: "I'm not convinced the
economics work."

Response:
"Then let's build the model together.
Here are my assumptions: [show assumptions].
Here's where I'm confident: [X]. Here's
where I'm uncertain: [Y]. Let's identify
which assumptions we should test in the
trial. And let's acknowledge that the
economics only work if [specific conditions
are met]. The trial is designed to
validate those conditions. Does that
make sense?"

For each concern, your response follows
a pattern:
  1. Acknowledge the concern is legitimate.
  2. Show you've thought about it.
  3. Propose a concrete validation or
     mitigation.
  4. Defer to their expertise where
     appropriate.
  5. Keep moving forward (don't get stuck
     defending).

SECTION 5: NEXT STEPS (5-10 minutes)

As the meeting winds down:

"Here's what I'm thinking for next steps:
[propose specific next step]. Does that
work for you?"

Specific next steps might be:
  - "Schedule a second meeting with your
    team [operations manager, etc] to
    discuss trial logistics."
  - "Have your operations manager visit
    my [lab/demo site] to see the process
    firsthand."
  - "You review [document: trial plan,
    regulatory pathway, financial model],
    and we reconvene in [X] to discuss."
  - "I prepare a detailed proposal for
    [specific trial], with timelines and
    costs, for your review."

The key: have ONE clear next step.
Don't leave it vague ("we'll stay in touch").
Vague kills momentum.

SECTION 6: CLOSING (2-3 minutes)

"I appreciate you taking the time to
understand this. I know you're skeptical -
and you should be. You don't know me, you
don't have a track record of this working,
and you're evaluating whether to take on
risk. That's exactly the right mindset.
I'm not asking you to commit to anything
today. I'm asking you to be curious
enough to [next step]. If, after [next
step], you think this is worth pursuing,
we'll have that conversation. If not,
you've lost [small amount of time], and
we both know we're not a fit. Fair?"

They will respect this. You're not pushing.
You're being rational.

Most operators, if the demo worked and you
presented professionally, will agree to
next steps.
```

WITHIN 24 HOURS:

## PART IV: FROM INTEREST TO COMMITMENT

### 4.1 — Moving Through the Sales Funnel

```
Once there's genuine interest (they're
reviewing documents, asking questions,
considering the trial), you move from
the "interest phase" to the "commitment
phase."

This is where most deals die.

Why?

Because the transition from "interesting
idea" to "we're going to do this" requires
overcoming a psychological barrier.

Going from curiosity to commitment means:
  - Allocating capital.
  - Taking on risk.
  - Changing operations.
  - Trusting a stranger.
  - Betting on something unproven.

Most people procrastinate this transition.
Your job is to move them through it.

THE COMMITMENT FUNNEL:

Stage 1: INTEREST
  "This seems interesting. Tell me more."
  Duration: 1-3 meetings.
  Your goal: Move to Stage 2.

Stage 2: BELIEF
  "I believe this could work."
  Duration: 2-4 weeks (document review,
  internal discussions, analysis).
  Your goal: Move to Stage 3.

Stage 3: COMMITMENT
  "We're going to do this trial."
  Duration: Partnership agreement signed.
  Your goal: Move to Stage 4.

Stage 4: EXECUTION
  "Let's make it work."
  Duration: Trial running, learning, scaling.

Most people spend a lot of time in
Stage 2, worrying about commitment.

Your job is to move them through Stage 2
efficiently.

HOW TO MOVE THROUGH STAGE 2 (Belief → Commitment):

TACTIC 1: CREATE INTERNAL CHAMPIONS

The person you've been talking to
(let's call them "the Champion") will
need to convince THEIR team (operations
manager, finance, owner, etc) that
this is worth doing.

Your job: Make the Champion credible
to their own team.

How?

Request a meeting with "the Champion
and their team."

In this meeting:
  - Present to the team (not just the
    Champion).
  - Acknowledge that you know the
    operations people have concerns
    (operations risk, labor impact).
  - Show that you've thought through
    their specific concerns.
  - Position the Champion as the one
    who brought this forward (give them
    credit).
  - Ask for THEIR feedback on trial
    design (make them co-creators, not
    passive recipients).

Example dialogue with operations manager:

You: "I know you're thinking about labor
impact. The trial would require about
[X hours/week] of labor for [duration].
That's roughly [cost]. Does that feel
manageable, or do we need to redesign
the trial to reduce labor?"

Operations Manager: "That seems high."

You: "Fair feedback. What would you
expect, and how could we redesign to
hit that number?"

Operations Manager: [Suggests change].

You: "That's actually a good idea. Let me
see if that changes the trial timeline,
and I'll come back to you."

[After you analyze]

You: "Your idea actually improves the
trial design. Can I put you down as
someone who thinks this is worth trying?"

Operations Manager: "Yeah, I can live with
that."

Now the operations manager is a champion
too.

You've moved from one champion
(decision-maker) to two champions
(decision-maker + operations).

If you can get 3+ champions internally
(decision-maker, operations, finance),
the decision gets made.

TACTIC 2: EXPLICIT RISK BOUNDARIES

At Stage 2, the blocker is usually
UNDEFINED RISK.

The operator is thinking:
"What if something goes wrong?
How much could I lose?
I don't know, and I don't like not knowing."

Your job: Define the risk.

Create a document: "Explicit Risk
Boundaries for P1S-B Trial."

"Here are the risks to your operations:
1. [Risk]: [Mitigation]: [Maximum loss if
   it happens anyway].
2. [Risk]: [Mitigation]: [Maximum loss].
3. [Risk]: [Mitigation]: [Maximum loss]."

Example:

"Risk: P1S-B fails to produce harvestable
product.
Mitigation: We run a 30-day trial before
full-scale deployment. If product quality
is not [minimum standard], we don't proceed.
Maximum loss if it happens: $[trial cost],
roughly [% of your annual profit]."

"Risk: Integration takes longer than planned,
disrupting operations.
Mitigation: Trial is isolated from main
operations. Main operations continue unchanged.
Disruption limited to [trial location].
Maximum loss if it happens: [labor cost
impact], roughly $[amount]."

"Risk: Regulatory approval is denied.
Mitigation: We've identified [alternative
markets] or [alternative regulatory pathways].
Product can be used for [alternative use]
with no change to production.
Maximum loss if it happens: $[amount] if
none of the alternatives work."

This document does THREE things:
  1. Acknowledges that risk is real.
  2. Shows you've thought about mitigation.
  3. Quantifies the downside (removes
     fear of unknown).

Most operators, when they see that the
maximum loss is defined and manageable,
move forward.

TACTIC 3: AGREEMENT ARCHITECTURE

When it's time to formalize the commitment,
don't jump straight to a 20-page legal
contract.

Use a gradual commitment structure:

STAGE 1: Letter of Intent (1-2 pages)
  "We agree to move forward with trial
  planning. We will develop a detailed trial
  plan by [date], with shared costs of
  [amount]. Either party can exit by [date]
  at no cost beyond shared planning costs."

  This is NOT binding. It's a commitment
  to co-develop a plan.

STAGE 2: Trial Plan (5-10 pages)
  "Detailed plan for trial. Scope, timeline,
  costs, success metrics, governance."

  This is jointly developed (you and them).
  Both parties sign, indicating agreement
  to proceed with trial as outlined.

STAGE 3: Partnership Agreement (20-30 pages)
  "If trial succeeds, here are the terms
  for ongoing partnership (revenue share,
  responsibilities, IP rights, termination,
  etc)."

  This is signed ONLY if trial is successful.

This architecture has advantages:

  For them:
    - Low commitment initially (Letter of Intent
      isn't expensive).
    - They get to shape the trial (co-development).
    - They're not signing a big contract until
      they've seen results.
    - If trial fails, they exit with minimal
      cost.

  For you:
    - You get agreement to proceed (Letter of
      Intent is binding enough).
    - You have their input on trial
      (they own the design).
    - You have clear success metrics
      (no moving goalposts).
    - If trial succeeds, they're pre-committed
      to partnership (because they agreed to
      Partnership Agreement terms upfront).

TACTIC 4: SOCIAL PROOF AND CREDIBILITY

At Stage 2, they're thinking:
"This person seems legitimate, but I have
no proof they can actually deliver."

What you can do:

A) REFERENCE:
   If you have even one person who's seen
   the technology work (even if not in
   livestock), offer them as a reference.

   "Before you commit, you can talk to
   [Name], who [relevant experience]. I've
   showed them the primitive, and they can
   speak to [specific capability]."

B) LITERATURE:
   Provide published papers on the
   underlying mechanisms.

   "Here's academic literature on [SAP
   absorption, bentonite adsorption,
   borax antimicrobial effects]. My
   formulation applies these well-established
   mechanisms to this new application."

C) VALIDATION TESTING:
   If you haven't already, commission a
   third-party lab to test your product.

   "I've had my harvest product analyzed by
   [lab name]. Here's the NPK result.
   Here's the pathogen testing. These are
   objective measures from an independent lab."

   (This is worth $500-1000 and is the best
    credibility investment you can make.)

D) INDUSTRY EXPERT REVIEW:
   If possible, have an agriculture
   consultant or academic review your
   proposal and give feedback.

   "I've had Dr. [Name], a soil scientist at
   [university], review this. His assessment:
   [positive feedback]. Here's his input."

   (This is worth asking 5 academics, 1-2
    will probably help, for credibility.)

One or more of these makes a huge difference.

TACTIC 5: URGENCY (LIGHT TOUCH)

At Stage 2, they're also in no rush.
They can keep thinking about it
indefinitely.

You need LIGHT urgency, not aggressive
urgency.

Light urgency:

"I'm planning to approach [other potential
partner] in [timeframe] with the same
opportunity. I wanted to make sure you had
a chance to make a decision before I did.
If you're seriously interested, let's move
this forward in the next [X weeks]. If
you're not, I totally understand, and I'll
pursue this with someone else. Either way,
I'd rather know sooner than later. What
do you think?"

This is NOT pressure. It's directness.

You're saying: "I respect your time, and I
respect mine. Let's decide if we're doing
this together."

Most decision-makers appreciate this.
They will either:
  A) Commit to timeline.
  B) Be honest that they're not interested.

Either way, you move forward.

THE TRIGGER FOR COMMITMENT:

The moment of commitment typically happens
when:

1. They've reviewed the documents.
2. They've talked to their team.
3. They've run the numbers themselves.
4. They've discussed with an advisor
   (accountant, lawyer, consultant).
5. They've convinced themselves that
   the upside is real.
6. They've defined their downside risk
   as acceptable.

And then they say one of:
  - "OK, let's do a trial."
  - "I want to make this work."
  - "How do we get started?"

At that moment, MOVE FAST.

Don't let them have time to think again.

Get to Letter of Intent within a week.
Get to trial planning within two weeks.
Get to trial start within 4-8 weeks.

Momentum is everything.

Once you break momentum, they'll talk
themselves out of it.
```

## PART V: PARTNERSHIP STRUCTURE AND AGREEMENT

### 5.1 — The Terms That Work

```
Once they're ready to commit, you need
an agreement that:

1. Protects both parties legally.
2. Aligns incentives (both succeed or both
   fail, ideally).
3. Clarifies decision-making.
4. Defines what happens in both success
   and failure scenarios.
5. Protects your IP.

THE AGREEMENT ARCHITECTURE:

You will need THREE documents:

DOCUMENT 1: LETTER OF INTENT (Non-binding)

Purpose: Formal agreement to co-develop
a trial plan.

Key terms:
  - Scope: What will the trial test?
  - Timeline: When will planning be done?
  - Costs: Who pays for planning?
  - Confidentiality: Both parties agree
    not to disclose trial details.
  - Termination: Either party can exit
    before trial starts with [X] notice.

Length: 1-2 pages.

Tone: Friendly, informal, collaborative.

Example structure:

"Partner and Technology Provider agree to:

1. Develop a detailed trial plan for
   implementing [primitive] at [facility],
   targeting [specific outcome].

2. Complete trial planning by [date].

3. Share costs of planning as follows:
   [split].

4. Keep trial details confidential.

5. Either party may terminate this
   Letter of Intent with [X days] notice
   before [date], with no further obligation.

6. If trial plan is finalized, both parties
   agree to [specific next step].

Signed: [both parties]"

DOCUMENT 2: TRIAL AGREEMENT (Binding)

Purpose: Formal agreement to conduct
the trial, with defined scope, costs,
and success metrics.

Key terms:

SECTION A: Trial Design
  - What, exactly, are we testing?
  - Where will the trial take place?
  - How long will it run?
  - What are success metrics?

SECTION B: Roles and Responsibilities
  - What does Partner provide?
    (Space, labor, access, existing animals)
  - What does Technology Provider provide?
    (Equipment, materials, labor, expertise)
  - Who makes decisions during trial?
  - Who has responsibility if something
    goes wrong?

SECTION C: Financials
  - What are the trial costs?
  - Who pays for what?
  - Are there shared costs?
  - What happens if trial goes over budget?

SECTION D: Intellectual Property
  - Partner and Technology Provider agree that
    all intellectual property related to the
    primitive remains with Technology Provider.
  - Partner may use the primitive as part of
    this trial, but has no license to use it
    elsewhere without agreement.
  - Partner owns all data generated by trial
    (they can publish results, with Tech
    Provider approval for [IP-protecting
    changes]).

SECTION E: Confidentiality
  - Details of trial are confidential.
  - Neither party discloses without other's
    consent.
  - Exception: [regulatory, investors, legal
    professionals].

SECTION F: Success Criteria and Next Steps
  - If trial meets success criteria: [path
    to partnership agreement].
  - If trial doesn't meet criteria: [how
    do we decide whether to continue or exit?]

SECTION G: Termination
  - Either party can terminate with [X days]
    notice.
  - If Technology Provider terminates:
    [they refund costs, remove equipment, etc]
  - If Partner terminates: [they cover costs,
    Technology Provider removes equipment].

SECTION H: Limitation of Liability
  - Neither party is liable for [indirect
    damages, lost profits, etc].
  - Each party responsible for their own
    [insurance, workers comp, etc].

Length: 5-10 pages.

Tone: Professional, protective, clear.

DOCUMENT 3: PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT
(Binding, conditional on trial success)

Purpose: Terms for ongoing partnership
if trial succeeds.

Key terms:

SECTION A: Trial Success Validation
  - What metrics must be achieved for trial
    to be considered "successful"?
  - Who validates success (independent
    lab? Both parties? Specific criteria)?

SECTION B: Ongoing Operations
  - If trial is successful, what is the
    full-scale deployment?
  - Timeline for scaling from trial to
    full deployment?

SECTION C: Revenue Share
  - How are revenues split?
    Example options:
      Option 1: 50/50 split on byproduct
               revenue (simple).
      Option 2: Partner gets 70%, Tech
               Provider gets 30% (Partner
               owns operations, Tech
               Provider owns IP).
      Option 3: Tech Provider charges
               ingredient supply fee
               ($X/unit) + royalty (Y%)
               on product revenue.
    Pick the structure that aligns incentives
    for your business model.

SECTION D: Operations and Responsibilities
  - Who operates the P1S-B system day-to-day?
  - Who manages product quality control?
  - Who handles regulatory compliance?
  - Who manages distribution/sales?

SECTION E: Support and IP Access
  - Tech Provider provides technical support
    for [X hours/month] at no charge.
  - Tech Provider retains control of
    formulation, manufacturing process,
    and proprietary inputs (cultures,
    doping blends, etc).
  - Partner may use the primitive only at
    this facility for this purpose, unless
    otherwise agreed.

SECTION F: Intellectual Property
  - Primitive IP remains with Tech Provider.
  - Data from operations belongs to
    Partner, but Tech Provider may use
    anonymized data for [research,
    improvement, marketing] with
    [Partner approval].

SECTION G: Term and Termination
  - Initial term: [3-5 years].
  - Either party can terminate with
    [180 days] notice and [specific
    conditions].
  - Upon termination: [buyout structure,
    transition plan, IP handling].

SECTION H: Governance
  - Quarterly partnership review calls.
  - Annual business planning.
  - Dispute resolution process (mediation
    before litigation).

Length: 15-30 pages.

Tone: Professional, balanced, protective
of both parties' interests.

─────────────────────────────────────────

THE REVENUE SHARING DECISION:

This is the most important term to get
right, because it determines incentive
alignment.

OPTION 1: 50/50 SPLIT ON BYPRODUCT REVENUE

  "Partner and Tech Provider split all
  byproduct revenue 50/50."

  Example:
    Byproduct revenue: $10M/year.
    Partner gets: $5M.
    Tech Provider gets: $5M.

  Advantages:
    - Simple.
    - Equal upside.
    - Easy to measure (just split the revenue).

  Disadvantages:
    - Partner pays 100% of operational costs
      (they may feel that's unfair).
    - If the business fails, Partner loses
      more (they absorbed operational risk).
    - Partner may blame you if margins are
      lower than expected.

OPTION 2: TIERED SPLIT
           (Partner pays costs, higher split)

  "Partner covers all operational costs.
  After operational costs, revenue is split
  70% Partner / 30% Tech Provider."

  Example:
    Gross byproduct revenue: $10M.
    Operational costs: $2M.
    Net to split: $8M.
    Partner gets: $5.6M.
    Tech Provider gets: $2.4M.

  Advantages:
    - Partner incentivized to manage costs
      (higher net = higher their split).
    - Tech Provider still gets meaningful
      revenue ($2.4M in example).
    - Clearer alignment on cost efficiency.

  Disadvantages:
    - More complex (need to define what
      counts as "operational cost").
    - Requires accounting transparency.
    - Potential for disagreements on cost
      allocation.

OPTION 3: INGREDIENT SUPPLY + ROYALTY

  "Tech Provider supplies proprietary
  ingredients at cost + margin.
  Tech Provider also gets royalty on
  product sales."

  Example:
    Dry mix cost: $0.154/unit.
    Tech Provider price: $0.30/unit.
    Margin: $0.146/unit.

    Byproduct sold at $10/kg.
    Royalty: 5% of product revenue.

    Annual:
      1M kg sold at $10 = $10M revenue.
      Tech Provider gets: 5% = $500K royalty.
      Dry mix margin: 1M units × $0.146
                   = $146K/year.
      Total Tech Provider: $646K/year.

  Advantages:
    - Tech Provider is paid for IP + materials.
    - Separates "platform revenue"
      (ingredient supply) from "business
      revenue" (product sales).
    - Tech Provider incentivized to keep
      ingredient costs low (Partner buys
      more if costs are lower).
    - Aligns with "licensing model" you'll
      use with other partners later.

  Disadvantages:
    - More complex structure.
    - Requires separate accounting.
    - Partner may resent paying premium
      for ingredients.

MY RECOMMENDATION:

For your FIRST partnership, use OPTION 2
(Tiered split, 70/30 after operational
costs).

Why?
  - Simple enough (just track operational
    costs).
  - Aligns incentives (Partner wants to
    manage costs, you want them profitable).
  - You still get meaningful revenue
    ($2-3M/year at scale from that
    partner).
  - Partner feels like they have the primary

Why?
  - More scalable (don't need to deeply

## PART VI: THE EXECUTION ROADMAP

### 6.1 — From Signed Agreement to First Success

```
You've signed the agreement.
The partner is committed.
Now comes the hard part: making it work.

This is where most partnerships fail or
succeed.

THE CRITICAL SUCCESS FACTORS:

FACTOR 1: MANAGING EXPECTATIONS

Your partner is expecting certain outcomes
from the trial.

You must be CRYSTAL CLEAR about what
success looks like.

Not vague ("better than baseline").
SPECIFIC.

Example:

"Success criteria for P1S-B Trial:

1. Product yield: minimum [X]kg per animal
   per day.
2. Product quality: NPK analysis shows
   [minimum nitrogen %].
3. Operational impact: trial requires less
   than [X] hours/month of labor.
4. No disruption to existing operations:
   main herd health metrics unchanged.
5. Economic feasibility: product value
   exceeds trial costs within [X months]
   of scaling."

These criteria are:
  - Measurable (you can test them).
  - Specific (no ambiguity).
  - Challenging but achievable (not so
    hard that failure is guaranteed, not so
    easy that meaningless).

If trial meets all five criteria, it's a
success.
If it misses one, you have a conversation
about why and whether to continue.

Clear criteria prevent post-hoc disputes
("I thought we were supposed to see [X]
result").

FACTOR 2: ON-SITE PRESENCE

You must be present during the trial.
Not remotely, present.

Why?
  - You learn the facility's actual
    constraints.
  - You catch problems early.
  - Partner sees you're committed.
  - You build trust through consistent
    presence.

Minimum: weekly on-site visits during
trial.

Better: bi-weekly or more frequent,
depending on trial intensity.

This is not optional.
Remote management of a first partnership
will fail.

FACTOR 3: COMMUNICATION CADENCE

Establish a weekly rhythm:
  - Monday: Brief weekly call (30 min).
    Recap: what happened last week, what
    are we watching this week?
  - Friday: Quick check-in (10 min).
    Any issues? Anything to flag?

Monthly:
  - Detailed written report (2-3 pages).
    Metrics, observations, any issues,
    plan for next month.

This prevents surprises.
If something is going wrong, you catch
it early.

FACTOR 4: PROBLEM-SOLVING MINDSET

Things will go wrong.
The dry mix will batch inconsistently.
The drying timeline will be longer than
expected.
The labor impact will be higher than
estimated.
Regulatory approval will take longer.

Your mindset must be:
"We solve problems together."

Not:
"This is your fault" or "This is my fault."

When a problem arises:

Step 1: Acknowledge it (don't hide it).
"We're seeing [problem]. Here's what
I think is causing it."

Step 2: Solve it together (don't assume
the answer).
"What do you think? How should we adjust?"

Step 3: Document the learning.
"Here's what we learned. Here's how we'll
avoid this next time."

Partners respect problem-solving.
They lose respect for denial or blame.

FACTOR 5: EARLY WINS

The trial should be designed to show
results QUICKLY.

Not months down the road.

Why?
  - Partner maintains confidence.
  - Partner has early proof for their team.
  - Momentum compounds.

Example timeline:

  Week 1-2: Setup (install equipment,
            train staff).
  Week 3-4: First production cycle
            (show the primitive works,
             weigh the product, demonstrate
             yield).
  Week 5-6: Analysis (NPK testing,
            economic calculation).
  Week 7-8: Celebration and planning
            (here's what we achieved, here's
             what's next).

By week 8, the partner has PROOF that
the primitive works at their facility.

They've invested 8 weeks and [cost], and
they have something to show for it.

This is the difference between "interesting
idea" and "this actually works."

FACTOR 6: TEAM ALIGNMENT

The partner has multiple people involved:
  - Owner/decision maker.
  - Operations manager.
  - Finance person.
  - Potentially board or investors.

Your relationship is not just with the owner.
You must maintain alignment across the team.

How?

  Monthly team meetings (45 min).
  All stakeholders present.

  Agenda:
    1. Metrics update (how are we doing on
       success criteria?).
    2. Issues and blockers (what's challenging?).
    3. Plan for next month (what are we
       focused on?).
    4. Timeline and decision gates (when do
       we decide whether to scale?).

This prevents the operations manager
from thinking "the owner doesn't understand
the implications" or the finance person
from thinking "we're not tracking ROI."

Everyone hears the same information.
Everyone knows the plan.
Alignment prevents internal sabotage
(which is a real risk).

THE DECISION GATE: TRIAL SUCCESS

At the end of the trial (say, 6 months),
you evaluate:

Success metrics met?
  - Yes → Move to scaling.
  - No → Investigate why, decide whether to
         continue or pivot.

Economic model validated?
  - Yes → Partner is confident in ROI.
  - Somewhat → Discuss what needs to change
            for ROI to work.

Team alignment?
  - Yes → Everyone is bought in.
  - No → Address blockers, rebuild alignment.

Regulatory pathway clear?
  - Yes → Moving to production at scale.
  - No → What's the constraint? Can we
         mitigate?

If most boxes are checked, the partnership
moves to SCALING phase.

If major boxes are unchecked, you either:
  A) Extend trial to get more clarity.
  B) Pivot the approach (modify system,
     adjust expectations).
  C) Exit gracefully (acknowledge it didn't
     work, preserve relationship for future).

The key: clear decision criteria, clear
decision date, clear decision-making
process.

Vague endings kill partnerships.
Clear decision gates preserve them.
```
