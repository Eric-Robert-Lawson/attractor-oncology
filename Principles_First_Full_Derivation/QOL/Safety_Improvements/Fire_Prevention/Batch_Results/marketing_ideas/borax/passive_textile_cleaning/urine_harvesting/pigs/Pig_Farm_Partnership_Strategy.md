## PART I: THE SIGNAL LANDSCAPE

### 1.1 — What Signals You Are Sending (Intentionally or Not)

```
Every statement you make sends signals
about you.

A potential partner is constantly asking:

"Is this person:
  - Credible?
  - Honest?
  - Confident?
  - Desperate?
  - Hiding something?
  - Smart?
  - Experienced?
  - Manipulative?
  - Trustworthy?
  - Aligned with my interests?"

These judgments are formed from SIGNALS.

SIGNAL CATEGORY 1: CONFIDENCE SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Does this person believe in their own
technology?"

Positive signals:
- You speak about the technology with
  precision ("The borax concentration is
  X, which is 12× the inhibition constant").
- You acknowledge limitations honestly
  ("This works at facility scale if Y
  condition is met").
- You don't oversell ("This solves your
  odor problem, but it doesn't solve your
  feed cost problem").
- You discuss failure modes naturally
  ("If the trial shows X, we'll pivot to
  Y approach").

Negative signals:
- You hedge excessively ("I think... maybe...
  possibly... it might...").
- You oversell ("This will transform your
  entire operation").
- You avoid discussing limitations ("I'm
  not going to talk about challenges").
- You speak vaguely ("It's a revolutionary
  technology that works in mysterious ways").

Signal consequence:
Positive confidence signals → They believe
you (you're credible).
Negative signals → They doubt you (you're
either inexperienced or hiding something).

SIGNAL CATEGORY 2: HONESTY SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Is this person being straight with me,
or are they hiding something?"

Positive signals:
- You acknowledge tradeoffs ("You get
  higher revenue, but you also have
  operational responsibility").
- You admit what you don't know ("I don't
  know exactly how your specific soil will
  respond, which is why we have a trial").
- You correct yourself ("I said 6 months,
  but thinking about it more, 8 months is
  more realistic").
- You share downsides alongside upsides
  ("The upside is $200K/year, the downside
  risk is $2M capital").

Negative signals:
- You never admit limitations.
- You never say "I don't know."
- You dodge difficult questions.
- You only discuss upsides.
- You change your story based on who's
  listening.

Signal consequence:
Positive honesty signals → They trust you.
Negative signals → They suspect you're
manipulating them.

SIGNAL CATEGORY 3: ALIGNMENT SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Does this person benefit only if I benefit?
Or do they benefit regardless?"

Positive signals:
- You propose shared risk ("We split the
  trial cost 50/50").
- You tie your revenue to their success
  ("I get 30% of revenue only if you succeed").
- You explicitly acknowledge their concerns
  ("You're worried about X, which is
  legitimate").
- You propose mechanisms that protect them
  ("If the trial doesn't meet criteria Y,
  we exit and you get refunded").

Negative signals:
- You want payment upfront regardless of
  outcome.
- You propose terms that benefit you even
  if they fail.
- You dismiss their concerns ("That's not
  a real problem").
- You propose terms that lock them in
  ("You can't exit even if it doesn't work").

Signal consequence:
Positive alignment signals → They believe
you're partnering, not extracting.
Negative signals → They believe you're
trying to trap them.

SIGNAL CATEGORY 4: EXPERTISE SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Does this person actually know what
they're talking about?"

Positive signals:
- You speak with technical precision (not
  excessive jargon, but precise language).
- You understand their industry (you know
  what confinement looks like, what lagoon
  management is, what regulations exist).
- You can connect dots (you see how your
  primitive solves their specific problem).
- You ask informed questions (you're not
  discovering their industry as you go).

Negative signals:
- You use jargon that doesn't apply to
  their context.
- You misunderstand their operation
  ("So you have a septic system?" when
  they clearly said lagoon).
- You ask basic questions ("What's a
  slatted floor?").
- You make recommendations that don't fit
  their reality.

Signal consequence:
Positive expertise signals → They respect
you in your domain.
Negative signals → They think you're
inexperienced or didn't do homework.

SIGNAL CATEGORY 5: RISK AWARENESS SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Does this person understand the real
risks I'm taking? Or are they naive?"

Positive signals:
- You name specific risks upfront ("If
  regulatory approval takes longer than
  expected, timeline shifts").
- You propose mitigations ("Here's how we
  manage that risk").
- You acknowledge their constraints ("I
  know you can't disrupt your main operation
  for 6 months").
- You frame the trial as a validation
  tool ("The trial de-risks the decision
  before full scaling").

Negative signals:
- You say "There's no risk" (naive or
  lying).
- You dismiss risks they raise ("That's
  not really a concern").
- You propose solutions without
  understanding their constraints.
- You act like the decision is obvious
  ("Why wouldn't you do this?").

Signal consequence:
Positive risk awareness → They feel you
understand their world.
Negative signals → They feel you're
disconnected from reality.

SIGNAL CATEGORY 6: BOUNDARY SIGNALS

What they're evaluating:
"Does this person respect my autonomy?
Or are they trying to control me?"

Positive signals:
- You propose, don't demand ("Would you
  be interested in exploring...?").
- You defer decisions to them ("You'll
  decide how to implement this in your
  operation").
- You acknowledge their expertise ("You
  know your facility better than I do").
- You offer optionality ("We could do it
  this way or that way, what works for
  you?").

Negative signals:
- You tell them what they should do.
- You demand commitments quickly.
- You act like you know better than them.
- You give them no choices.

Signal consequence:
Positive boundary signals → They feel
respected and in control.
Negative signals → They feel pressured
or manipulated.

THE META-SIGNAL:

Beyond these six categories, there's a
META-SIGNAL you're sending with your
entire approach:

"Is this person trying to UNDERSTAND me,
or are they trying to CONVINCE me?"

Understanding signal:
- You ask questions and listen more than
  you talk.
- You build your case based on their
  situation, not a pre-built pitch.
- You adjust your approach based on what
  you learn about them.
- You leave room for their input.

Convincing signal:
- You talk more than you listen.
- You deliver a prepared pitch that doesn't
  adapt to their situation.
- You try to overcome objections rather
  than address underlying concerns.
- You're trying to get them to say yes.

THE MOST IMPORTANT SIGNAL:

The meta-signal matters more than
all six categories combined.

If you're operating from "understand"
mode, the individual signals mostly work
in your favor because they're authentic.

If you're operating from "convince" mode,
the signals often work against you because
operators can sense manipulation.

So the first decision is INTERNAL:

Are you genuinely trying to understand
this operator and find if partnership
is a fit?

Or are you trying to convince them to
say yes?

If the answer is the second one, operators
will sense it.

And they will not partner with you.

So reset internally.
You're looking for the RIGHT partner, not
just ANY partner.

This mindset shift changes all your signals
from "convince" to "understand."

And the signals improve dramatically.
```

### 1.2 — The Signals You're Sending About IP and Competition

```
The competitor risk question will come up.

They'll ask something like:
"If I invest in this partnership, and
you develop something valuable, what
stops you from taking the same deal to
my competitor?"

This is a LEGITIMATE question.

The signals they're evaluating:
- Do you think of me as a partner, or an
  opportunity?
- Are you loyal to partners, or just
  chasing deals?
- Do you have integrity around
  exclusivity and agreements?
- Are you planning to scale this by
  licensing to competitors?

Your answer to this question sends massive
signals about your trustworthiness and
your true intentions.

WHAT NOT TO SAY (And why):

WRONG ANSWER 1:
"I have a patent pending on the
formulation. No one else can use it."

Why this is wrong:
- Signal: You're trying to control/extract
  value through legal mechanisms, not
  partnership.
- It's also false (patent on formulation
  won't stop someone from developing
  alternative approach).
- It positions the patent as YOUR asset,
  not something that benefits them.
- It signals you're planning to use the
  patent leverage elsewhere.

WRONG ANSWER 2:
"Don't worry, I only work with one partner
per industry."

Why this is wrong:
- Signal: You're already thinking about
  your next partner (implicit: after
  you're done extracting value from
  this one).
- You're admitting you WILL work with
  competitors, just one per industry
  (so they're not special).
- "Per industry" is vague (are they the
  pig industry partner? What about dairy?
  What about poultry?).
- It sounds like a pre-packaged answer,
  not genuine thinking.

WRONG ANSWER 3:
"I can't tell you my strategy for scaling
across multiple operators because it's
proprietary."

Why this is wrong:
- Signal: You're hiding your strategy,
  which suggests your strategy doesn't
  serve their interests.
- If your strategy involved taking the
  deal to competitors, you definitely
  wouldn't tell them. So NOT telling them
  makes them assume the worst.
- It violates the honesty signal (you're
  being opaque).

WHAT TO SAY INSTEAD:

RIGHT ANSWER:

"That's a good question. Here's how I'm
thinking about it:

The primitive I've developed has multiple
applications. Pig farming is one. There
are applications in dairy, beef, poultry,
municipal wastewater, and several others.

My plan is NOT to compete with you by
taking the same deal to another pig
operation.

My plan IS to apply the primitive to
different animals and different contexts,
where your competitive advantage isn't
threatened.

But I want to be honest: eventually, I
will approach other operators. Not in
pig farming (that's yours to own once we
succeed). But in dairy, poultry, and
elsewhere.

You get first-mover advantage in pig farming.
That's your defensibility—you have a
head start, proven track record, and deep
optimization that competitors won't have
for years.

In exchange for that first-mover advantage,
I ask that you understand my business model
includes scaling the primitive across
industries.

Does that feel fair?"

Why this works:

HONESTY SIGNAL:
You're being upfront about your scaling
plans. No hidden agenda.

ALIGNMENT SIGNAL:
You're acknowledging that FIRST-MOVER
ADVANTAGE is their protection, not legal
mechanisms. You're not trying to use IP
to lock them in.

CLARITY SIGNAL:
You're being specific about scope
(different animals, not competitors
in their space).

RESPECT SIGNAL:
You're asking if it feels fair (you care
about their perspective, not just
extracting value).

CONFIDENCE SIGNAL:
You're confident in the model because
it doesn't depend on keeping them
ignorant—it depends on them having
genuine advantage through first-mover
position.

THE DEEPER GAME THEORY:

The competitor risk question is actually
a test.

They're not just asking about competitors.
They're asking: "Will this person be
honest with me about their true intentions?"

If you're evasive: They assume you have
something to hide.

If you're honest: They believe you're
trustworthy.

The honest answer is actually BETTER for
your business than any legal lock-in.

Because it signals that your confidence
is built on the QUALITY of your offer,
not on contractual entrapment.

And operators respect that.

WHAT TO DO IF THEY PUSH BACK:

If they say: "But how do I know you won't
take the same deal to someone else right
away?"

Response: "You don't. And you shouldn't
just trust me on that. That's why we have
the partnership agreement. Here's what it
specifies: [specific terms about exclusivity
and timeline]. But honestly, I wouldn't
do that because it would be stupid. If I
take the same deal to a competitor next
quarter, you lose trust in me, you tell
other operators not to work with me, and
my scaling strategy fails. My incentive
is to make you successful first. Your
success proves the model works. Then I
can approach dairy operators and say 'Here's
what we did in pig farming.' Your success
is my most valuable marketing asset. So
self-interest aligns with partnership
interest."

Why this works:

- You're being honest (you acknowledge
  you CAN'T be bound by trust alone—they
  need contractual protection).
- You're being rational (you're explaining
  why breaking trust would hurt you more
  than it would help).
- You're being strategic (you're showing
  how their success serves your interests).

This is a PARTNERSHIP answer, not a
vendor answer.

Operators respect this.
```

## PART II: THE QUESTION TAXONOMY

### 2.1 — The Predictable Questions and What They Really Mean

```
An operator will ask you questions.

Most will be surface questions about
specific technical or financial details.

But beneath every surface question is a
DEEPER question about trust, credibility,
alignment, and risk.

Your job is to understand both levels
and answer in a way that addresses the
deeper question while providing the
surface information.

QUESTION CATEGORY 1: TECHNICAL QUESTIONS

Surface: "How does the technology actually
work?"

Deeper: "Does this person understand the
mechanism well enough to troubleshoot
when things go wrong? Am I going to be
stuck without support?"

How to answer:

WRONG: You give a 15-minute technical
explanation of polymer chemistry, enzyme
kinetics, and clay mineralogy.

Why wrong: You're answering the surface
question, not the deeper question. And
you're probably overwhelming them with
details they don't need. This signals
that you either don't understand your
audience or you're hiding complexity in
jargon.

RIGHT: You give a 2-minute mechanical
explanation, then pause and ask:
"Does this level of detail make sense,
or do you want me to go deeper? I can
explain either how it works fundamentally,
or how it works in your facility. What's
more useful?"

Why right: You've answered the deeper
question (you understand the mechanism
well enough to explain it at their level).
You're asking what they actually need
to know (you respect their time). You're
showing flexibility (you adapt to them,
not forcing your approach).

─────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION CATEGORY 2: PERFORMANCE QUESTIONS

Surface: "What kind of yields can I expect?
What's the upside financially?"

Deeper: "Is this person being realistic,
or are they over-promising? What if the
yield is half of what they're saying?"

How to answer:

WRONG: "You'll see $200K-$300K/year in
additional revenue. These numbers are
conservative estimates."

Why wrong: (1) You're not being specific
about assumptions. (2) You're saying
"conservative" but the operator doesn't
know what that means. (3) You haven't
addressed the deeper worry (what if it's
less?).

RIGHT: "Based on your facility size and
urine concentration, I'm projecting
$200K-$300K/year in revenue. But let me
walk you through the assumptions:

We're assuming:
- 30g/L urine concentration (yours might
  be 25-35).
- 93% nitrogen retention (lab data shows
  87-96% range).
- $10/kg product price (specialty market
  range is $8-14).
- Full facility deployment (trial might
  teach us to optimize further).

If any of these assumptions are off:
- If concentration is lower: revenue is
  proportionally lower.
- If retention is lower: revenue is lower.
- If market price is lower: revenue is
  lower.
- If we only deploy partial facility:
  revenue is proportionally lower.

In the trial, we're validating each of
these assumptions. Once we know your
actual numbers, we can calculate your
actual upside.

So in the trial phase: $200K-$300K is the
range, but your actual upside might be
higher or lower. We'll know after 3 months."

Why right: (1) You're being transparent
about assumptions. (2) You're showing
you've thought about downside scenarios.
(3) You're using the trial as a validation
tool, not a lock-in. (4) You're answering
the deeper question (you're realistic,
not over-promising).

─────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION CATEGORY 3: RISK QUESTIONS

Surface: "What could go wrong?"

Deeper: "Is this person aware of real
risks? Or are they naive? Have they
thought through failure scenarios?"

How to answer:

WRONG: "Nothing should go wrong if you
implement correctly. The chemistry is
sound."

Why wrong: You're dismissing the question.
You're implying failure is operator error,
not a real possibility. This signals
naivety or defensiveness. It signals you
haven't thought deeply about risk.

RIGHT: "Several things could go wrong.
Here's what I've identified:

RISK 1: Product quality is lower than
expected.
Cause: Urine concentration is lower than
assumed.
Impact: Revenue is $50K instead of $200K.
Mitigation: Trial measures actual
concentration. If too low, we optimize
with feed additives (separate approach).

RISK 2: Integration takes longer than
planned.
Cause: Your facility layout is more complex
than I understood.
Impact: Trial timeline extends 4-8 weeks.
Mitigation: We do a detailed facility tour
before trial starts. We plan integration
together.

RISK 3: Regulatory approval is delayed.
Cause: State requires additional testing
or documentation.
Impact: You can't market the product
immediately.
Mitigation: We've researched pathways
for [state]. Approval typically takes
[timeline]. If delayed, we have [plan B:
direct farm use, no label required].

RISK 4: Market price for fertiliser drops.
Cause: Feed costs drop, agricultural
commodity prices drop broadly.
Impact: Revenue per kg drops (less margin).
Mitigation: This is real market risk I
can't control. But upside is still positive
even at 50% of projected price.

RISK 5: You find the operational changes
too disruptive.
Cause: Trial reveals issues we didn't
anticipate.
Impact: You decide not to scale.
Mitigation: That's why we limit the trial
to [portion of facility]. Main operation
is unaffected.

Of these, which concerns you most?"

Why right: (1) You've clearly thought about
failure modes. (2) You've proposed
mitigations for each. (3) You've
acknowledged some risks are inherent
(market risk) and some are manageable
(integration risk). (4) You're inviting
their input on what matters most to them.
(5) You're demonstrating realistic thinking,
which signals competence.

─────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION CATEGORY 4: CREDIBILITY QUESTIONS

Surface: "Why should I trust you? You've
never done this before."

Deeper: "What qualifications do you have?
What's your track record? Are you
competent?"

How to answer:

WRONG: "I've spent 10 years developing
this technology. I'm an expert."

Why wrong: (1) They're not asking about
your background. (2) "10 years" means
nothing if they don't know who you are or
what you've done. (3) Claiming expertise
sounds defensive when they're questioning
credibility.

WRONG: "I don't have industry experience,
but the science is sound."

Why wrong: (1) You're giving them exactly
what they feared (no credibility). (2)
You're using "the science is sound" as a
crutch (which avoids their actual concern).

RIGHT: "You're right—I don't have a track
record in pig farming. I'm not claiming
to. Here's what I have:

I've spent [X] years developing this
primitive. I can show you [specific
validation: lab tests, third-party
analysis, reproducible demonstrations].

I understand the underlying science in
detail [show precision in technical
discussion].

I've thought through implementation in
your specific facility [show you've done
homework on their operation].

But here's what I DON'T have: a track
record of success in pig farming. No one
does—because this hasn't been deployed
at facility scale yet.

So here's how we manage that risk: the
trial.

The trial is designed so that I EARN your
trust through execution, not ask you to
give it to me upfront.

If I execute well during the trial, you'll
know I'm competent. If I don't, you'll
know to move on before you've invested
heavily.

Fair?"

Why right: (1) You're being honest about
what you don't have. (2) You're not
apologizing for inexperience—you're
framing it as normal for new technology.
(3) You're proposing a mechanism (the
trial) that de-risks their decision.
(4) You're showing that your confidence
is built on the trial's ability to prove
things, not on you claiming expertise.

─────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION CATEGORY 5: EXCLUSIVITY QUESTIONS

Surface: "Will you do this with other
operators? How many partnerships are you
pursuing?"

Deeper: "Am I special? Or am I just one
of many? What's my competitive advantage
if I'm not first?"

How to answer:

WRONG: "You'll be my first partner. Once
we succeed, I'll approach others."

Why wrong: (1) You're implying you're
currently approaching others (undermining
their specialness). (2) You're setting up
expectation that they'll have first-
mover advantage without explicitly
framing the VALUE of that advantage.

RIGHT: "Good question. Here's my strategy:

I'm looking for ONE first partner in pig
farming. That's you if this conversation
goes well.

The reason: your success is my proof point.
Once we've succeeded—trial validated,
revenue generated, system optimized—then
I can approach dairy operators and say
'Here's what we did in pig farming.'

Your success is my marketing.

Second and third pig farm partners will
have an easier time because they can see
your results. But they'll also have later
tech adoption, and they'll be optimizing
around YOUR approach, not leading in
innovation.

In other industries (dairy, poultry,
municipal), I'll apply the same approach:
one first partner to prove the model,
then scale.

So you're first in pig farming. That means
[specific advantage: first-mover in odor
solution, first to optimize urine capture,
first to own the narrative in your market].

That advantage is real and defensible even
if other pig farms eventually adopt this—
because you'll be 1-2 years ahead in
optimization."

Why right: (1) You're being clear about
scope (ONE first pig farm partner, but
eventual scaling across pig farms). (2)
You're framing their specialness honestly
(first, not only—but first is valuable).
(3) You're explaining the REAL value of
first-mover (they get optimization
advantage, proof point, narrative control).
(4) You're being realistic (eventually
others will adopt this, but later). (5)
You're showing your scaling strategy is
transparent, not hidden.

─────────────────────────────────────────

QUESTION CATEGORY 6: FINANCIAL COMMITMENT
QUESTIONS

Surface: "How much will this cost? How
much will I need to invest?"

Deeper: "Can I afford this? Will I get
trapped in a bad financial deal? Is this
a profit opportunity or a money pit?"

How to answer:

WRONG: "The trial cost is $[amount].
That's your investment."

Why wrong: (1) You're framing it as a cost
(expense mindset) rather than investment
(opportunity mindset). (2) You haven't
contextualized whether $[amount] is big
or small relative to their operation. (3)
You haven't explained what they get for
that investment.

RIGHT: "The trial cost will be approximately
$[amount]. Let me put that in context:

Your annual facility revenue is roughly
$[estimate based on size/type].
The trial cost is [X]% of annual revenue.

We split the cost 50/50: you invest
$[X amount], I invest $[X amount].

What you get for that investment:
- Data on whether this works in your facility.
- 3 months of my time and expertise (roughly
  $[X value]).
- A fully operational system by the end of
  trial.

If trial is successful: you own the system
going forward, and we split revenue at
[terms].

If trial is unsuccessful: you've paid for
validation that tells you whether to scale
or exit. That's worth the cost—better to
know now than commit $[larger amount] to
full scaling.

So the question is: is $[amount] a
reasonable investment to validate a
$[X]/year opportunity?"

Why right: (1) You're contextualizing cost
relative to their business. (2) You're
explaining what they GET for the cost. (3)
You're framing the cost as VALIDATION
investment, not sunk expense. (4) You're
showing ROI math (cost vs upside). (5)
You're honest that failure has value too
(avoiding worse decisions).

```

### 2.2 — The Meta-Questions Behind the Surface Questions

```
Beneath nearly every question is one of
five meta-questions:

META-QUESTION 1: "CAN YOU DELIVER?"

Surface questions that indicate this:
- "How does the technology work?"
- "What kind of yields?"
- "What if something goes wrong?"
- "Do you have a track record?"

What they're really asking:
"Is this person competent enough to
actually deliver on their promises?"

How to signal yes:
- Show precision and depth in technical
  knowledge.
- Acknowledge limitations honestly.
- Propose realistic timelines, not
  optimistic ones.
- Demonstrate that you've thought through
  failure modes.
- Offer validation and third-party proof.

How to signal no:
- Wave off technical questions with vague
  answers.
- Oversell and over-promise.
- Avoid discussing what could go wrong.
- Act like you're an expert in domains
  where you're not.

─────────────────────────────────────────

META-QUESTION 2: "ARE YOU HONEST?"

Surface questions that indicate this:
- "What are the risks?"
- "Will you do this with competitors?"
- "What if it doesn't work?"
- "What are you not telling me?"

What they're really asking:
"Can I trust this person to tell me the
truth, or are they manipulating me?"

How to signal yes:
- Acknowledge tradeoffs and downsides
  proactively (don't wait to be asked).
- Say "I don't know" when you don't know.
- Correct yourself when you realize
  you've said something inaccurate.
- Don't hide your scaling strategy.
- Be transparent about your incentives.

How to signal no:
- Only discuss upsides.
- Answer every question with confidence,
  even when uncertain.
- Avoid difficult subjects.
- Discover your scaling strategy as you
  go (shows you haven't thought it through).
- Hide your incentives (use jargon or
  complexity to obscure).

─────────────────────────────────────────

META-QUESTION 3: "ARE WE ALIGNED?"

Surface questions that indicate this:
- "How much of the revenue do I get?"
- "What if your technology fails?"
- "Will you invest in the trial?"
- "What happens if the partnership doesn't
  work out?"

What they're really asking:
"Will this person benefit even if I fail?
Or do we only both succeed if I succeed?"

How to signal yes:
- Propose shared risk in the trial (50/50
  or equivalent).
- Tie your revenue to their success
  (commission, royalty, upside share).
- Acknowledge their constraints and build
  around them.
- Propose exit ramps if things go wrong
  (they can leave).
- Show that your long-term vision depends
  on their success.

How to signal no:
- Ask for payment upfront regardless of
  outcomes.
- Propose terms that benefit you even if
  they fail.
- Dismiss or minimize their concerns.
- Create lock-in mechanisms that trap them.
- Show that you benefit from failure
  (e.g., you charge them for "learning"
  if trial fails).

─────────────────────────────────────────

META-QUESTION 4: "ARE YOU CONFIDENT?"

Surface questions that indicate this:
- "Why should I believe this will work?"
- "What's your evidence?"
- "Has anyone else done this?"
- "What's your backup plan?"

What they're really asking:
"Does this person actually believe in
their technology? Or are they uncertain?"

How to signal yes:
- Speak about the technology with certainty
  (not arrogance, but clarity).
- Share evidence without being asked
  (proactively show data).
- Acknowledge that you're early (no
  precedent yet) but that's not a weakness,
  it's normal for new tech.
- Propose concrete mechanisms that de-risk
  the decision (the trial).

How to signal no:
- Qualify every statement ("I think...
  maybe... possibly...").
- Treat evidence as optional ("We could
  test if you want, but I'm sure it works").
- Act defensive when questioned.
- Propose risk mitigation that protects
  you, not them.

─────────────────────────────────────────

META-QUESTION 5: "DO YOU RESPECT ME?"

Surface questions that indicate this:
- "How much of my operation do you need
  to change?"
- "Will this disrupt my current business?"
- "Who gets to make decisions about how
  this is implemented?"
- "Do I have to trust you on everything?"

What they're really asking:
"Will this person respect my autonomy?
Or will they try to control me?"

How to signal yes:
- Acknowledge their expertise (they know
  their facility better than you do).
- Propose, don't demand (give them choices).
- Defer decisions to them (let them decide
  implementation details).
- Show respect for their constraints
  (don't ask them to change what's working).
- Invite their input (ask them how they'd
  approach problems).

How to signal no:
- Act like you know their business better
  than they do.
- Tell them what they should do.
- Demand quick decisions.
- Treat their constraints as obstacles
  instead of parameters to work with.
- Provide only one solution (take it or
  leave it).

## PART III: THE DISCLOSURE STRATEGY

### 3.1 — What to Disclose and What to Withhold

```
Not all information is equally valuable
to disclose.

Some information moves them toward
partnership.
Some information moves them away.
Some information is irrelevant.

Your job is to understand which is which.

The framework:

DISCLOSE IF:
1. The information directly addresses
   one of their pain points.
2. The information builds credibility
   (shows you've thought deeply).
3. The information demonstrates alignment
   (shows your interests match theirs).
4. The information is factual and
   defensible.
5. The information answers a question
   they're likely to ask anyway (better to
   control the narrative).

WITHHOLD IF:
1. The information is about your scaling
   strategy in ways that threaten them
   (e.g., "I'm already talking to three
   other pig farm operators").
2. The information is uncertain and
   could undermine confidence (e.g.,
   "I'm not sure if regulatory approval
   will take 3 months or 8 months").
3. The information is about your IP in
   ways that suggest you're using IP to
   protect you, not to benefit them
   (e.g., "I have a non-compete clause
   that prevents you from doing X").
4. The information is about your
   incentives in ways that suggest
   misalignment (e.g., "I make 50% of
   revenue regardless of your success").
5. The information is irrelevant to
   their decision (e.g., "I spent
   10 years developing this").

PROACTIVELY DISCLOSE
(Even if not asked):

1. YOUR SCALING TIMELINE
   What you're withholding: "I'm eventually
   going to approach other operators."
   Why disclose: They'll assume you will
   anyway. Better to be transparent.
   How to frame: "My long-term vision
   includes [scaling to dairy, poultry,
   etc]. I'm not planning to compete
   with you in pig farming [if that's
   true]."

2. YOUR CONSTRAINT
   What you're disclosing: "I can only
   support one trial simultaneously
   because I'm doing this solo."
   Why disclose: Demonstrates honesty
   about your limitations. Shows you're
   not overpromising on support.
   How to frame: "This means you get
   my full attention during our trial.
   Once we complete it, I'll move to
   the next partner."

3. YOUR REGULATORY RESEARCH
   What you're disclosing: "I've researched
   fertiliser approval in [state]. Here's
   the pathway [specific details]."
   Why disclose: Demonstrates preparation
   and de-risks the approval process.
   How to frame: "I've already done the
   homework on what approval requires.
   Here's what we need to do."

4. YOUR IP STRATEGY
   What you're disclosing: "I'm protecting
   the formulation as a trade secret [not
   patent] for these reasons [specific
   reasons]."
   Why disclose: Shows you've thought
   about protection strategy. Trade
   secret is actually more protective
   than patent in this case.
   How to frame: "This means the IP can't
   be designed around easily. It's a real
   defensibility advantage for you as
   first partner."

5. YOUR BOTTOM LINE EXPECTATIONS
   What you're disclosing: "For ongoing
   partnership, I need [specific
   percentage/structure]. Below that, it
   doesn't make sense for me to scale."
   Why disclose: Clarity prevents later
   disputes. Shows you have clear
   thinking about value.
   How to frame: "I'm transparent about
   what I need financially so we can
   agree upfront whether partnership
   makes sense for both of us."

PROACTIVELY WITHHOLD
(Don't volunteer):

1. EARLY FAILURES OR SETBACKS
   What you're withholding: "The first
   three formulations didn't work well.
   I almost gave up on this approach."
   Why withhold: Demonstrates trial-and-
   error, which is normal, but signals
   uncertainty to operator. They don't
   need to know this.
   When to disclose: Only if they ask
   specifically about your development
   process.

2. ALTERNATIVE APPLICATIONS NOT
   RELEVANT TO THEM
   What you're withholding: "I'm also
   exploring this for municipal wastewater,
   textile processing, and industrial
   fermentation."
   Why withhold: Distracts from their
   focus (pig farming). Makes it seem
   like you're not fully committed to
   their application.
   When to disclose: Only if they ask
   about your broader vision, or when
   discussing your scaling strategy
   post-partnership.

3. UNCERTAINTY ABOUT TIMELINE
   What you're withholding: "Honestly,
   I'm not sure if full deployment takes
   3 months or 8 months. It depends on
   factors I can't predict."
   Why withhold: Undermines confidence
   in execution. They don't need to know
   your internal uncertainty about timelines.
   When to disclose: You can be honest
   about "potential delays" and "we'll
   have better timeline clarity after
   trial starts," but don't share raw
   uncertainty.

4. YOUR DESPERATION OR FINANCIAL
   PRESSURE
   What you're withholding: "I really need
   this partnership to work out. I've been
   working on this for years with no
   revenue."
   Why withhold: Creates perception of
   desperation, which undermines
   negotiating position. Makes them think
   you'll accept bad terms to close deal.
   When to disclose: Never, unless
   they ask directly about your financial
   situation. And even then, frame as
   "I'm committed to this long-term"
   not "I'm desperate."

5. CRITICISM OF COMPETITORS OR
   EXISTING APPROACHES
   What you're withholding: "Your current
   odor management approach is outdated
   and inefficient."
   Why withhold: Implies criticism of
   THEM (they chose that approach).
   Triggers defensiveness.
   When to disclose: Never phrase it as
   criticism. Instead: "The current
   approach is appropriate given the
   constraints. I'm proposing a way to
   remove the constraints."

6. YOUR IP CONCERNS ABOUT THEM
   What you're withholding: "I'm worried
   you'll copy my approach and keep 100%
   of the value."
   Why withhold: Signals distrust,
   creates friction, shows you're
   defensive.
   When to disclose: Never unilaterally.
   But if they ask about IP protection,
   you can discuss (in partnership
   agreement context): "We both want to
   protect the partnership value. That's
   why we have [specific contractual
   terms]."

THE HEURISTIC:

Simple rule of thumb:

DISCLOSE information that shows:
- Preparation (you've done homework).
- Honesty (you acknowledge limitations).
- Alignment (your interests match theirs).

WITHHOLD information that shows:
- Uncertainty (undermines confidence).
- Desperation (undermines negotiating
  position).
- Distrust (creates friction).
- Irrelevance (distracts from focus).

Following this heuristic, most of your
disclosure strategy works itself out
naturally.

## PART IV: THE BOUNDARY FRAMEWORK

### 4.1 — The Limits of Withholding: When Transparency Becomes Necessary

```
There's a limit to what you can withhold
without damaging trust.

Beyond that limit, withholding becomes
DECEPTION.

Deception destroys partnerships.

Your job is to understand where the line
is.

THE BOUNDARY:

You can withhold information IF:
1. They don't have a right to know it
   (it's about your internal operations,
   not their decision).
2. Disclosure would undermine your
   negotiating position unfairly
   (e.g., revealing your lowest acceptable
    percentage before you've discussed
    terms).
3. Disclosure would create problems
   without solving them (e.g., detailing
   all your fears about failure).

You CANNOT withhold information IF:
1. It directly affects their decision
   about whether to partner with you.
2. It's about facts they're entitled to
   know (e.g., whether you have IP
   protection, whether regulatory approval
   is feasible).
3. It's about your incentives in the
   partnership (how you're compensated,
   what happens if things go wrong).
4. They've asked you directly and you
   respond with a deflection.

EXAMPLES AT THE BOUNDARY:

EXAMPLE 1: The Patent Question

They ask: "Do you have patent protection
on this technology?"

BOUNDARY CASE:
You have a patent application filed, but
you're not sure if it will be granted.

What you CANNOT say:
"Yes, I have a patent." (This is
deception if the patent isn't granted.)

What you CAN say:
"I have a patent application filed. The
approval process typically takes 2-3
years. I'm fairly confident it will be
granted based on [specifics], but nothing
is certain until it's granted. In the
meantime, I'm protecting the formulation
as a trade secret, which in some ways is
stronger protection."

Why this works:
(1) You're being honest about status.
(2) You're managing expectations
(approval isn't guaranteed).
(3) You're showing you have a backup
strategy (trade secret).

────────────────────────────────────────

EXAMPLE 2: The Regulatory Approval Question

They ask: "How long will it take to get
regulatory approval?"

BOUNDARY CASE:
You've researched that approval typically
takes 2-3 months, but you've never actually
gone through the process. You don't have
real data.

What you CANNOT say:
"It takes 2-3 months. It's straightforward."
(This implies certainty you don't have.)

What you CAN say:
"Based on my research of regulatory
pathways, approval typically takes 2-3
months. That's the timeline I've seen for
other organic amendments in [state].
However, I haven't done this specific
process yet, so the actual timeline could
be shorter or longer. If timeline becomes
critical, we can work with a regulatory
consultant to expedite. But I want to be
clear: I haven't done this exact process,
so my estimate is based on industry
benchmarks, not personal experience."

Why this works:
(1) You're being honest about source of
information (research, not experience).
(2) You're setting realistic expectations
(it could be longer).
(3) You're proposing a mitigation if
timeline is important (regulatory consultant).

────────────────────────────────────────

EXAMPLE 3: The IP Strategy Question

They ask: "How are you protecting the
technology? If I invest in this, how do I
know another operator won't copy it?"

BOUNDARY CASE:
You want to keep the specific formulation
secret (trade secret), but you're not
sure trade secret is stronger than patent
in this industry.

What you CANNOT say:
"Don't worry, it's totally protected.
Nobody can copy it." (This is false
confidence.)

What you CAN say:
"I'm using trade secret protection, which
means:
(1) The exact formulation stays secret
(only known to us).
(2) There's no public record of how it
works (like there is with a patent).
(3) Someone would have to reverse-engineer
the product, which is hard for a novel
formulation.
(4) If they do reverse-engineer and copy it,
we have legal recourse for misappropriation.

Trade secret has advantages and
disadvantages compared to patent:
Advantages: longer protection (indefinite
if kept secret), harder to design around,
no public disclosure.
Disadvantages: requires we keep it secret
(can't publicly claim the innovation).

For this application, trade secret is
stronger protection than patent would be.
The value to you comes from being first
in market with proven results—that's more
defensible than any patent."

Why this works:
(1) You're being realistic about what
protection means.
(2) You're explaining the tradeoff
(longer protection vs can't claim innovation).
(3) You're showing you've thought about
this strategically.
(4) You're not overselling the protection
(you acknowledge legal recourse assumes
you'd enforce, which costs money).

────────────────────────────────────────

EXAMPLE 4: The Competitor Strategy Question

They ask: "Are you going to take this to
other pig farms?"

BOUNDARY CASE:
You are planning to eventually approach
other pig farmers, but you want them to
feel special.

What you CANNOT say:
"No, you're my only pig farm partner."
(If you're planning to approach others, this
is deception.)

What you CAN say:
"Yes, eventually. Here's the timeline:
Year 1: Validate with you (first partner
advantage is yours).
Year 2-3: Optimize based on your results.
Year 3+: Approach other operators.

Your advantage is 2-3 years of head start.
That's real defensibility—you'll have
optimization, proven results, narrative
control. Operators coming in year 3 will
be copying your approach, not pioneering
it."

Why this works:
(1) You're being honest (you will approach
others).
(2) You're explaining their actual
advantage (first-mover, not exclusivity).
(3) You're showing the value of that
advantage is real, even if not exclusive.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE TEST:

If you're unsure whether you can withhold
information, ask yourself:

"If they found out later that I withheld
this, would they feel deceived?"

If yes: you need to disclose.
If no: you can withhold.

Examples:

Q: Can I withhold that I'm also approaching
dairy operators?
Test: "Would they feel deceived if they
found out?"
Answer: YES (they'd think I promised
exclusivity I didn't actually promise).
Result: DISCLOSE.

Q: Can I withhold my internal timeline
uncertainty?
Test: "Would they feel deceived if they
found out I wasn't sure?"
Answer: MAYBE NOT (you did disclose a
reasonable timeline based on research).
Result: CAN WITHHOLD, but not indefinitely
(once the trial starts and you have more
information, you update them).

Q: Can I withhold that I'm also exploring
applications in municipal wastewater?
Test: "Would they feel deceived if they
found out?"
Answer: NO (you're not competing with
them, and it shows the primitive has
broader potential).
Result: CAN WITHHOLD unless they ask.

The Test doesn't always give clear answers,
but it's a guide.

When in doubt: disclose.

Operators would rather know more than
fewer uncomfortable truths.
```

## PART V: THE NAVIGATION RULES

### 5.1 — Real-Time Decision Making in Conversation

```
You're in a conversation with an operator.
They ask you a question you didn't
anticipate.

What do you do?

You can't refer to this document.
You need a quick decision rule.

Here are the NAVIGATION RULES:

RULE 1: THE PAUSE

When asked a question, pause for 2-3
seconds before answering.

This serves three purposes:
(1) Signals you're taking the question
seriously (not reflexive).
(2) Gives you time to think through the
implications.
(3) Shows confidence (desperate people
answer immediately, thoughtful people
pause).

What to say during the pause:
Nothing. Just pause. Look like you're
thinking.

If they fill the silence, let them. Often
they'll clarify or expand on the question,
which gives you more information.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 2: THE SURFACE + DEEPER STRUCTURE

Structure your answer in three parts:

PART 1: Answer the surface question
(2-3 sentences).

PART 2: Address the deeper question
(2-3 sentences).

PART 3: Invite their input
(1 sentence).

Example:

Q: "How much will this cost?"

SURFACE: "The trial cost will be
approximately $[amount]."

DEEPER: "Let me put that in context:
your annual revenue is roughly $[estimate],
so this is [X]% of annual revenue. For
that investment, you get proof of whether
a $[Y]/year opportunity is viable for
your facility."

DEEPER: "If successful, we scale. If
unsuccessful, you've validated that
decision. Either way, the cost is worth
the learning."

INPUT: "Does that framing make sense, or
are you thinking about cost differently?"

This structure ensures you answer what
they're really asking, not just the
surface question.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 3: THE HONESTY OVERRIDE

If you're tempted to tell a half-truth or
avoid a question, OVERRIDE it.

Tell the honest answer instead.

Why? Because the half-truth will be
detected. Operators are skilled at reading
evasion.

And once they sense evasion, partnership
is dead.

So:

Q: "Do you have patent protection?"
[You're tempted to say yes, because you
 have an application filed.]

OVERRIDE: "I have an application filed,
but not granted yet. I'm fairly confident
it will be granted, but I'm not certain."

Q: "Will regulatory approval be a problem?"
[You're tempted to say no, but you're not
 sure.]

OVERRIDE: "I've researched the pathway
and it looks straightforward, but I
haven't done it myself. There could be
surprises."

Q: "Are you going to approach other pig
farms?"
[You're tempted to avoid the question.]

OVERRIDE: "Yes, eventually. Here's when
and how..."

The honesty override feels risky in the
moment.

But it's the safest move long-term.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 4: THE INVITATION

After any answer, invite their perspective:

"Does that make sense?"
"Is that addressing your concern?"
"What do you think?"
"What matters most to you about this?"

This serves three purposes:
(1) Shows you care about their reaction,
not just your message.
(2) Gives you information about whether
your answer landed.
(3) Keeps the conversation collaborative,
not broadcast.

If they say "That makes sense," you've
won the point.

If they say "Not really," you get to
understand what they were actually asking.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 5: THE BOUNDARY CHECK

If a question pushes into territory that's
not appropriate for this stage, you can
gently set a boundary.

Q: "What exactly is in the formulation?"

BOUNDARY: "I can't share the exact
composition right now—that's the IP I'm
protecting. But I can tell you the
functional components [list them] and
why each one is there. Does that give
you enough information to evaluate
whether this works?"

This is not evasion. It's appropriately
protecting IP.

But you're offering transparency in a
different way (function instead of
composition).

Q: "How much do you make off of each
facility?"

BOUNDARY: "I'm happy to discuss my
compensation structure for the partnership
when we get to terms negotiation. Right
now, what matters is understanding
whether this opportunity is attractive
to you. Once you decide you're interested,
we'll discuss financial terms. Fair?"

This is not evasion. It's redirecting to
the appropriate stage of conversation.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 6: THE REVERSE QUESTION

If they ask a question you don't have a
good answer for, reverse it back to them:

Q: "What if the product quality is worse
than you're predicting?"

REVERSE: "That's a great question. What
would 'worse than predicted' look like to
you? What's the minimum quality threshold
where you'd still be willing to scale?"

This accomplishes three things:
(1) Buys you time to think.
(2) Shows you care about THEIR
definition of success, not your prediction.
(3) Often reveals that you don't actually
have a bad answer—they just needed to
specify what matters to them.

The reverse question is not deflection.
It's collaborative problem-solving.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 7: THE DATA HEDGE

When discussing numbers or projections,
hedge with data:

WRONG: "You'll see $200K/year revenue."

RIGHT: "Based on [data source: your
facility size + industry benchmarks],
I'm projecting $200K/year. But that
assumes [list assumptions]. Your actual
number could be higher or lower
depending on [variables]. The trial
measures your actual numbers."

The hedge is not weakness. It's
precision.

It signals you know the difference between
projection and reality.

Operators respect that.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 8: THE COMMITMENT CLARITY

When discussing timelines or next steps,
be crystal clear:

WRONG: "We'll probably schedule a meeting
in a few weeks."

RIGHT: "I'm going to propose a meeting
with your operations manager on [specific
date]. I'll send you a calendar invite
by [specific date]. If that date doesn't
work, let me know and we'll find
another. But I want to make sure we have
a specific commitment, not just
'sometime soon.'"

Clarity is your friend.

Vagueness is your enemy.

Operators respond to specificity.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 9: THE SILENCE COMFORT

If you've answered a question and there's
silence, don't fill it.

Let them think.

Let them process.

When they're ready, they'll respond.

If you fill the silence with more talking,
you often undermine your own answer.

The silence is a sign they're taking you
seriously.

Let it be.

─────────────────────────────────────────

RULE 10: THE RESET

If you realize you've made a mistake—said
something inaccurate, or gave a bad answer—
correct it immediately:

"Actually, I want to correct what I just
said. [Correct statement]."

Don't try to defend the wrong answer.

Don't hope they didn't notice.

Just correct it and move on.

This signals integrity.

Operators respect it.
```

## PART VI: THE META-LEVEL STRATEGY

### 6.1 — The Master Framework: What You're Really Doing

```
All of this—signal management, question
navigation, disclosure strategy, boundary
setting—is operating at the level of
TACTICS.

But there's a META-LEVEL strategy that
encompasses everything:

You are not trying to CONVINCE them.
You are trying to BUILD TRUST through
TRANSPARENCY.

This is the master framework.

If you internalize this, all the tactics
work automatically because they'll be
authentic.

If you use the tactics without this
underlying framework, they'll feel
manipulative and you'll fail.

THE MASTER FRAMEWORK IN PRACTICE:

When they ask a question, you don't ask:
"How should I answer to maximize my
chances of partnership?"

You ask:
"What's the honest answer that addresses
their legitimate concern?"

Then you give that honest answer.

The partnership outcome follows from
honest, transparent communication.

It's not guaranteed (sometimes the honest
answer is "this won't work for you").

But it's the only strategy that builds
lasting partnership.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE INTELLIGENCE OF THE FRAMEWORK:

Why does transparency win over persuasion?

Because operators have been pitched to
before.

They know what sales persuasion looks
like.

They're defensive against it.

But they're not defensive against someone
who's genuinely transparent.

Transparency is disarming.

It signals confidence.

"I don't need to hide anything because
what I'm offering is solid."

Persuasion signals doubt.

"I need to convince you because you
might rationally say no."

Operators pick up on this signal.

They respond to confidence.

Transparency builds confidence.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE SURPRISING DISCOVERY:

Here's the weird part:

When you commit to transparency as your
strategy, you actually become MORE
persuasive, not less.

Because you're not trying to persuade them.

You're giving them the information they
need to make a smart decision.

And smart operators make smart decisions
in their favor.

If partnership is good for them, they'll
say yes.

If it's not, they'll say no.

And you'll respect that decision either way.

That confidence—that you can walk away if
it's not a fit—is the most persuasive
signal you can send.

Because it shows you're serious about
partnership, not desperate for deals.

Operators partner with people who don't
need their deal.

Not people who do.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE PARADOX:

The master framework is:

"I'm going to be 100% transparent and
honest, regardless of how it affects
partnership outcome."

And yet, this approach produces BETTER
partnership outcomes than any strategy
built on selective persuasion.

Because it builds the right kind of trust.

The trust that survives trials.
The trust that survives setbacks.
The trust that sustains partnership for
years.

If you're using strategies to manipulate
someone into partnership, the partnership
is built on manipulation.

And it collapses when reality diverges
from the pitch.

If you're using transparency to build
partnership, the partnership is built on
reality.

And it survives divergence from the pitch
(because both parties understand reality
is complex).

────────────────────────────────────────

THE DECISION:

So the REAL question is not:

"What should I say to maximize partnership
probability?"

The REAL question is:

"Am I committed to transparency as the
foundation, even if it means losing some
potential deals?"

If yes: use the framework, and success
is likely.

If no: use the tactics without the
framework, and success is unlikely (and
any partnerships you do get will be fragile).

You have to choose which game you're
playing.

The game of manipulation (high failure
rate, fragile partnerships).

Or the game of transparency (lower initial
success rate, but more durable partnerships).

I'm recommending the second game.

Because the first game fails with operators
who are experienced and thoughtful.

And those are the operators you want
anyway.

## PART VII: FINAL SYNTHESIS AND NAVIGATION

### 7.1 — The Decision Tree for Any Conversation

```
You're in a conversation with a potential
partner.

They ask a question.

You use this decision tree:

STEP 1: IDENTIFY THE QUESTION TYPE

Is this a:
- Technical question? (Understanding mechanism)
- Performance question? (Understanding upside)
- Risk question? (Understanding downside)
- Credibility question? (Understanding you)
- Exclusivity question? (Understanding scope)

- Other?

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 2: IDENTIFY THE META-QUESTION

Beneath the surface question, what are
they REALLY asking?

Is it:
- "Can you deliver?"
- "Are you honest?"
- "Are we aligned?"
- "Are you confident?"
- "Do you respect me?"

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 3: PAUSE AND THINK

Take 2-3 seconds.

Think about:
(1) What's the honest answer?
(2) What assumption in their question
am I allowed to challenge?
(3) Is there a deeper concern I should
address?

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 4: ANSWER THE META-QUESTION

Give an answer that addresses the deeper
question, not just the surface question.

Structure:
(1) Answer the surface question
(2-3 sentences).
(2) Address the deeper question
(2-3 sentences).
(3) Invite their input (1 sentence).

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 5: CHECK THE HONESTY OVERRIDE

Is there any temptation to:
- Hedge the answer?
- Avoid the answer?
- Tell a half-truth?

If yes: override and give the honest answer
instead.

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 6: DELIVER WITH CONFIDENCE

Say your answer with clarity and conviction.

Don't qualify it excessively.
Don't apologize for it.
Don't seek their permission to have said it.

Conviction signals confidence.

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 7: INVITE RESPONSE

"Does that make sense?"
"Is that addressing your concern?"
"What do you think?"

Let them respond.

Listen to their response without defending.

────────────────────────────────────────

STEP 8: ADJUST OR CONTINUE

If their response signals they didn't
understand:
Clarify and re-explain.

If their response signals they have a
follow-up concern:
Address the follow-up.

If their response signals they're satisfied:
Continue to next topic or next step.

────────────────────────────────────────

This decision tree is not complicated.

It's basically:

1. Understand what they're asking.
2. Be honest.
3. Address the deeper concern.
4. Let them respond.
5. Adjust if needed.

But executing it consistently requires
practice.

Practice now, in low-stakes conversations.

So when the high-stakes conversation
comes (the real potential partner), you're
natural with it.
