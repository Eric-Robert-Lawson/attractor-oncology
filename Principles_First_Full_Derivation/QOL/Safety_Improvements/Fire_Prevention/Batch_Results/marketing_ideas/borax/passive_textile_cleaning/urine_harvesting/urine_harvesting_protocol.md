## PART I: WHAT IS ACTUALLY IN URINE

### 1.1 — The Composition of Human Urine

```
URINE IS NOT WASTE IN A CHEMICAL SENSE.

It is a concentrated solution of
metabolic products — compounds the
body has processed and is eliminating.
Many of these compounds have
significant economic value.

URINE COMPOSITION PER LITRE:

  WATER: 950–960g (the fraction
    to be evaporated away).

  UREA: 9–23g/L (average ~15g/L
    at normal hydration).
    Urea is the primary nitrogen
    carrier. It is a fully formed,
    chemically stable molecule:
    (NH2)2CO. It is the active
    ingredient in most nitrogen
    fertilizers. It is used in
    cosmetics (skin moisturiser —
    urea cream is a pharmaceutical
    product). It is used in
    industrial chemistry.
    Urea is the most valuable
    single compound in urine.

  CREATININE: 0.6–2g/L.
    Metabolite of muscle creatine.
    No significant agricultural or
    commercial value at this scale.
    Contributes to dry mass.

  SODIUM: 1.5–3.5g/L.
  CHLORIDE: 3–8g/L.
    NaCl from dietary salt.
    Low value but contributes to
    dry mass.

  POTASSIUM: 0.5–2g/L.
    K — a primary plant macronutrient.
    Valuable as fertilizer (K component
    of NPK).

  PHOSPHATE: 0.4–1.3g/L (as P).
    Phosphorus — the scarcest
    primary plant macronutrient.
    Global phosphorus reserves are
    finite and depleting.
    Phosphate from urine is genuinely
    strategic in a 30–50 year
    agricultural context.
    Value as fertilizer: high and rising.

  SULFATE: 1–2g/L.
    Minor nutrient value.

  URIC ACID: 0.08–0.3g/L.
    Purine metabolite.
    Limited commercial value at
    this concentration.

  OTHER ORGANICS: 2–5g/L combined.
    Hormones, vitamins, amino acids,
    minor metabolites. Trace value.

  TOTAL DISSOLVED SOLIDS: ~20–50g/L.
    Average at normal hydration: ~35g/L.
    Average at high concentration
    (low water intake): ~50g/L.
    Average at low concentration
    (high water intake): ~20g/L.

THE NPK PROFILE OF URINE
(per litre):

  N (nitrogen): 10–14g/L.
    ~80% from urea, ~20% from
    other nitrogen compounds.
    This is the highest-value component.

  P (phosphorus): 0.4–1.3g/L.
    All from phosphate compounds.

  K (potassium): 0.5–2g/L.
    All from dietary potassium.

  COMPARISON TO COMMERCIAL FERTILIZER:
    Urea fertilizer (46-0-0):
    46% N by weight.
    Dried urine is approximately
    25–40% N by dry weight.
    Dried urine is a nitrogen
    fertilizer comparable in
    concentration to commercial
    urea-based products.
    But it ALSO contains P and K.
    It is a complete NPK fertilizer
    from a single waste stream.

WHAT NORMALLY HAPPENS TO URINE:

  Standard urine evaporation without
  any treatment:

    PROBLEM 1 — UREASE DECOMPOSITION:
      Bacteria in the environment
      produce urease enzyme.
      Urease rapidly converts:
        (NH2)2CO + H2O →
        2NH3↑ + CO2↑
      Urea → ammonia gas + CO2.
      The nitrogen — the most
      valuable component — volatilises
      as ammonia into the atmosphere.
      Up to 50–80% of urine nitrogen
      is lost as ammonia within
      hours to days of deposition
      in an open, uncontrolled environment.

    PROBLEM 2 — ODOR:
      Ammonia and other volatile
      amines are the source of the
      characteristic urine odor.
      The odor is the signal of
      nitrogen being lost.

    PROBLEM 3 — MICROBIAL GROWTH:
      Urine is a rich microbial
      growth medium.
      Within hours, populations of
      bacteria and fungi colonise
      and begin decomposing the
      nitrogen compounds to volatile
      forms.

    PROBLEM 4 — UNCONTROLLED EVAPORATION:
      Without a matrix to hold the
      liquid, urine evaporates
      unevenly — pooling, spreading,
      contaminating surfaces.
      The residue is distributed,
      diluted, and lost.

  THE RESULT OF UNCONTROLLED
  EVAPORATION:
    A small amount of salt-rich,
    nitrogen-depleted residue.
    Most of the value has been
    lost to the atmosphere.
    What remains smells bad and
    has limited agricultural utility.

  THIS IS WHY URINE HARVESTING
  HAS HISTORICALLY BEEN IMPRACTICAL
  AT SMALL SCALE:
    The product degrades faster
    than it can be harvested.
    The nitrogen is lost before
    the water evaporates.
    The resulting product is
    low value and high volume
    of water to manage.

  P1S-B DRY INGREDIENTS SOLVE ALL
  FOUR PROBLEMS SIMULTANEOUSLY.
  This is the geometric fit.

## PART II: WHY THE DRY INGREDIENT

### 2.1 — The Mechanism, Component by Component

```
THE DRY INGREDIENT RATIO:
(from P1S-B formula for 750mL water)

  SAP powder: 8g
  Bentonite: 14g
  Borax: 9g
  Baking soda: 5g
  Total dry: 36g

  Normalised ratio:
  SAP : Baking soda : Borax : Bentonite
  = 8 : 5 : 9 : 14
  = 1 : 0.625 : 1.125 : 1.75

  Each component addresses a specific
  failure mode of standard urine evaporation.

─────────────────────────────────────

COMPONENT 1 — SAP POWDER
(SODIUM POLYACRYLATE):

  PROBLEM ADDRESSED:
  Uncontrolled liquid pooling and spread.

  MECHANISM:
    SAP absorbs liquid on contact —
    up to 300–500× its weight
    in distilled water.
    In urine (ionic solution —
    dissolved salts reduce SAP
    absorption capacity):
    approximately 30–80× its weight.
    Conservative estimate: 50×.

    At 50× absorption:
    8g SAP absorbs 400mL urine.

    This is the primary structural
    mechanism: the liquid is
    immediately immobilised in the
    polymer matrix. It cannot pool,
    spread, or run off.
    The urine is converted from
    free liquid to a gel matrix
    the moment it contacts the SAP.

  SECONDARY MECHANISM:
    The gel matrix slows evaporation
    compared to free liquid.
    Slower evaporation = more
    controlled process = more
    complete nitrogen retention
    before evaporation removes
    the water fraction.
    It also slows microbial access
    to the absorbed liquid —
    the polymer matrix physically
    impedes bacterial colonisation
    of the urine.

  TERTIARY MECHANISM — THE SAP
  REUSE AND DEGRADATION GEOMETRY:
    SAP absorbs and releases
    water repeatedly.
    Each absorption/drying cycle
    in urine deposits water-soluble
    urine solids within the
    polymer matrix.
    These deposited solids partially
    occupy the SAP absorption sites —
    reducing absorption capacity
    in subsequent cycles.
    After N cycles, the SAP absorption
    capacity falls below the threshold
    needed to immobilise a full
    urination event.
    At this threshold: the SAP is
    saturated with urine solids,
    the polymer matrix is
    loaded with the extracted material,
    and the reservoir is ready for
    final harvest.
    The degradation is the signal
    that maximum extraction has occurred.
    This is not a failure mode.
    It is the harvest indicator.

─────────────────────────────────────

COMPONENT 2 — BORAX
(SODIUM TETRABORATE DECAHYDRATE):

  THIS IS THE MOST CRITICAL COMPONENT
  FOR URINE HARVESTING.
  THIS IS WHY THE APPLICATION
  WAS NOT PREVIOUSLY PRACTICAL.

  PROBLEM ADDRESSED:
  Urease-catalysed decomposition of urea
  to ammonia — the primary nitrogen loss mechanism.

  MECHANISM:
    Borax dissociates in water to
    produce borate ions (B(OH)4⁻).
    Borate ions are competitive
    inhibitors of urease enzyme.
    Urease is the enzyme that
    catalyses:
      Urea → 2NH3 + CO2.
    Borate binds to the active site
    of urease and blocks it.

    THE CONSEQUENCE:
      Urease is inhibited.
      Urea decomposition is suppressed.
      The nitrogen remains as urea
      rather than volatilising as ammonia.
      The most valuable component
      of urine is preserved.

    UREASE INHIBITION AT BORAX CONCENTRATIONS
    IN P1S-B:
      Borax content per urination event
      (see calculation below): 2–5g borax
      dissolved in 250–350mL urine.
      Borax concentration: ~7–14g/L.
      Effective borate ion concentration:
      sufficient for near-complete
      urease inhibition.
      (Literature: borate inhibits urease
      at concentrations as low as 1–5 mM.
      7–14g/L borax = 18–36 mM borate.
      This is 4–36× the minimum
      inhibitory concentration.)

    THE NITROGEN RETENTION DIFFERENCE:

      WITHOUT BORAX:
        50–80% of urine nitrogen
        lost as ammonia within
        24–72 hours.
        Product is nitrogen-depleted.
        Severe odor.

      WITH BORAX IN P1S-B DRY MIX:
        Urease inhibited.
        Urea preserved as urea
        in the dry residue.
        Nitrogen retention: 80–95%.
        Odor dramatically suppressed
        (less ammonia produced).

    THIS SINGLE MECHANISM IS WHY
    THE APPLICATION WAS NOT PRACTICAL
    BEFORE THIS COMBINATION.
    Standard urine evaporation loses
    the nitrogen before the water leaves.
    Borax prevents that loss.
    The product that remains
    after evaporation is genuinely
    nutrient-dense because the
    primary nutrient was not lost.

─────────────────────────────────────

COMPONENT 3 — BENTONITE CLAY:

  PROBLEMS ADDRESSED:
  Secondary nitrogen retention,
  residual ammonia adsorption,
  product structure and soil performance.

  MECHANISM 1 — AMMONIUM ADSORPTION:
    The small fraction of urea that
    does decompose (borax inhibits,
    does not eliminate decomposition)
    produces ammonium ions (NH4⁺).
    Bentonite clay has very high
    cation exchange capacity (CEC):
    80–150 meq/100g.
    Bentonite preferentially adsorbs
    NH4⁺ ions onto its clay platelets.
    Adsorbed ammonium is held in
    the clay structure and does not
    volatilise as ammonia gas.
    This is a secondary nitrogen
    retention mechanism — catching
    the nitrogen that the borax
    inhibition did not preserve as urea.

  MECHANISM 2 — PHOSPHATE RETENTION:
    Bentonite adsorbs phosphate ions.
    Phosphate is the second most
    valuable component of urine.
    Without bentonite, phosphate
    remains in solution until
    evaporation — then crystallises
    as whatever salt form it takes.
    With bentonite, phosphate is
    adsorbed to the clay surface
    in a mineral-bound form that
    is more stable and more
    available to plants (slow-release)
    than free crystallised phosphate salt.

  MECHANISM 3 — PRODUCT STRUCTURE:
    The bentonite fraction gives
    the dried harvest product a
    clay-mineral structure that:
      Improves soil incorporation
      (clay particles integrate well
      with soil).
      Improves water retention when
      applied to soil (the same
      property that makes bentonite
      valuable as a soil amendment).
      Provides a physical matrix
      that slows release of bound
      nutrients — slow-release
      fertilizer geometry.

  MECHANISM 4 — ODOR ADSORPTION:
    Bentonite adsorbs volatile
    organic compounds and residual
    ammonia molecules from the
    headspace above the reservoir.
    This is a secondary odor control
    mechanism operating alongside
    the primary borax urease inhibition.

─────────────────────────────────────

COMPONENT 4 — BAKING SODA
(SODIUM BICARBONATE):

  PROBLEMS ADDRESSED:
  pH optimisation for borax
  inhibition efficiency and
  residual odor buffering.

  MECHANISM 1 — pH BUFFERING:
    Borax urease inhibition is most
    effective at slightly alkaline pH
    (7.5–8.5).
    Fresh urine pH: 5.5–7.5.
    Aged urine: more alkaline
    (ammonia raises pH).
    Baking soda buffers the mixture
    toward pH 7.5–8.0 immediately.
    This optimises the borax
    inhibition from the first
    moment of urine contact —
    before the pH would naturally
    rise from the partial decomposition.

  MECHANISM 2 — ODOR NEUTRALISATION:
    Sodium bicarbonate reacts with
    acidic odor compounds.
    It also reacts with the small
    amounts of ammonia produced:
      NH3 + NaHCO3 → NaHCO3·NH3
      (ammonium bicarbonate)
    Trapping residual ammonia in
    a non-volatile salt form.

─────────────────────────────────────

THE COMBINED MECHANISM SUMMARY:

  Without P1S-B dry ingredients:
    Urine → pools → bacterial colonisation
    → urease activity → ammonia volatilisation
    → nitrogen-depleted residue + odor.
    Practical extraction: inefficient.
    Product quality: poor.
    Odor: severe.

  With P1S-B dry ingredients:
    Urine → immediately gelled by SAP
    → movement stopped, evaporation controlled
    → borax inhibits urease (primary N retention)
    → bentonite adsorbs NH4⁺ (secondary N retention)
    → bentonite adsorbs phosphate (P retention)
    → baking soda buffers pH (optimises all above)
    → slow, controlled evaporation of water fraction
    → urine solids accumulate in SAP/bentonite matrix
    → N, P, K all retained
    → odor suppressed throughout
    → multiple cycles compound the accumulation
    → harvest at SAP saturation threshold.
    Practical extraction: efficient and scalable.
    Product quality: high-N, complete NPK.
    Odor: managed.

  THE FOUR COMPONENTS EACH ADDRESS
  A SPECIFIC FAILURE MODE OF STANDARD
  URINE EVAPORATION.
  THEY DO NOT OVERLAP — THEY STACK.
  THE SYSTEM IS COMPLETE BECAUSE
  EACH FAILURE MODE HAS ITS OWN SOLUTION.
  THIS IS THE GEOMETRIC FIT.

### 4.3 — Absorption Capacity and Cycle Count

```
MAXIMUM LIQUID CAPACITY PER RESERVOIR:

  For 100g dry mix:
  SAP content: 22.2g.
  At Ks = 55mL/g: 22.2 × 55 = 1,221mL total capacity.
  At Ks = 40mL/g: 22.2 × 40 = 888mL total capacity.
  At Ks = 80mL/g: 22.2 × 80 = 1,776mL total capacity.

  IMPORTANT NUANCE:
    The SAP capacity degrades with
    each urine cycle as dissolved
    solids occupy absorption sites.
    The degradation is not linear —
    the first few cycles degrade
    faster than later cycles as
    the accessible surface area fills.
    Estimated capacity per cycle:

    Cycle 1: 100% capacity (Ks = 55mL/g).
    Cycle 2: ~85% capacity.
    Cycle 3: ~72% capacity.
    Cycle 4: ~60% capacity.
    Cycle 5: ~50% capacity.
    Cycle 6: ~40% capacity.
    Cycle 7: ~32% capacity.
    Cycle 8: ~25% capacity.
    (Approximating a geometric degradation
    with factor ~0.85 per cycle.)

  TOTAL CUMULATIVE ABSORPTION ACROSS
  ALL CYCLES BEFORE THRESHOLD:

    Threshold: when per-cycle capacity
    drops below one full urination
    event volume (Vu = 250mL).

    For 100g dry mix (SAP = 22.2g):
    Cycle 1: 22.2 × 55 × 1.00 = 1,221mL.
    Cycle 2: 22.2 × 55 × 0.85 = 1,038mL.
    ...
    But we are not filling to max
    capacity each cycle — we add
    one urination event (250mL) per cycle.

    PER-CYCLE ABSORPTION CHECK:
    Does the remaining capacity
    exceed Vu = 250mL?

    Cycle 1 residual capacity: 1,221mL
    → absorbs 250mL. Residual: 971mL.
    Cycle 2 residual cap. (degraded):
    971 × 0.85 = 825mL remaining.
    → absorbs 250mL. Residual: 575mL.
    Cycle 3: 575 × 0.85 = 489mL.
    → absorbs 250mL. Residual: 239mL.
    Cycle 4: 239mL < 250mL threshold.
    HARVEST TRIGGERED.

    THEREFORE:
    A 100g dry mix reservoir handles
    approximately 3–4 urination events
    before the SAP is saturated
    with urine solids and the
    absorption threshold is passed.

    FOR LARGER RESERVOIRS:
    250g mix: 8–10 events.
    500g mix: 15–20 events.
    1,000g mix: 30–40 events.

  THIS IS THE DESIGN VARIABLE.
  Reservoir size is selected based on:
    Expected event volume (festival

```

  made synthetic nitrogen fertilizer
  cheap enough that urine recycling
  became economically uninteresting.

  PREVENTED PRACTICAL SMALL-SCALE
  URINE HARVESTING AT MODERN STANDARDS:

  BARRIER 1 — NITROGEN LOSS:
    Without urease inhibition, 50–80%
    of nitrogen volatilises as ammonia.
    The product was nitrogen-depleted
    before it could be harvested.
    This made dried urine a poor
    fertilizer compared to commercial urea.

    P1S-B SOLUTION: Borax as integrated
    urease inhibitor. First time a
    cheap, stable, self-contained
    urease inhibitor has been built
    into a collection medium.

  BARRIER 2 — ODOR:
    Ammonia from urease activity
    made open urine collection
    socially and practically unacceptable
    in most contexts.

    P1S-B SOLUTION: Borax inhibits
    the source of odor (ammonia production),
    bentonite adsorbs residual volatiles,
    baking soda neutralises remaining
    odors. The odor problem is solved
    at source, not masked.

  BARRIER 3 — LIQUID MANAGEMENT:
    Free urine requires containers,
    pumping, transport of liquid
    (heavy, bulk, contaminating).
    Processing liquid urine at scale
    requires infrastructure.

    P1S-B SOLUTION: SAP immediately
    immobilises the liquid.
    No pooling, no liquid transport.
    The harvest is a dry or
    semi-dry solid.
    Transport and handling of
    dry material vs. liquid:
    dramatically simpler and cheaper.

  BARRIER 4 — PRODUCT FORM:
    Evaporated urine without a

    P1S-B SOLUTION: The bentonite

    suitable for bagging, spreading,
