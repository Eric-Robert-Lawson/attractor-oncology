## PART I: THE OPTIMAL DEPLOYMENT GEOMETRY

### 1.1 — Why Simple Is Structurally Correct

```
THE ENGINEERING PRINCIPLE:

  Every additional component in
  a system is a failure point,
  a cost, a complexity, and a
  barrier to adoption.

  The question is not:
    What is the most sophisticated
    system that could work?

  The question is:
    What is the minimum system
    that achieves the result?

  THE MINIMUM SYSTEM IS:

    A container (bucket).
    Dry ingredients at the bottom.
    A person urinating into it.
    A designated drying area.

  That is the complete system.
  Nothing else is required.
  Everything else is optional
  elaboration that adds cost
  and complexity without
  improving the fundamental
  mechanism.

THE URINATION STREAM AS A
MIXING MECHANISM:

  A standard urination stream
  delivers:
    Volume: 250–400mL.
    Duration: 20–35 seconds.
    Flow rate: ~10–15mL/second.
    Velocity at impact: moderate —
    sufficient to disturb and
    partially suspend fine powder.
    Temperature: 35–37°C.
    The stream arrives at body
    temperature, which is optimal
    for SAP hydration kinetics.

  WHAT THE STREAM DOES TO THE
  DRY INGREDIENTS:

    Impact disturbs the powder bed.
    Fine SAP particles become
    airborne briefly and then
    contact the incoming liquid —
    this is equivalent to the
    "at the liquid surface" addition
    protocol in the blending methodology.
    The stream provides continuous
    mixing as it falls — the turbulence
    distributes the liquid through
    the powder bed rather than
    channelling through a single path.
    The temperature (37°C) is higher
    than room temperature water,
    which marginally accelerates
    SAP hydration kinetics.
    The acidic pH of fresh urine
    (5.5–6.5) activates the baking
    soda buffer immediately on contact —
    the fizzing reaction is visible
    and confirms the buffer is operating.

  THE STREAM IS NOT AN INFERIOR
  MIXING METHOD.
  For this application it is the
  correct mixing method because:
    It delivers liquid gradually
    (20–35 seconds) — not as a
    single large bolus that would
    overwhelm the SAP before it
    can hydrate.
    It delivers turbulent energy
    that distributes the liquid.
    It maintains temperature above
    ambient.
    It is free — no energy, no
    equipment, no step added.

  ENGINEERING VERDICT:
    The urination stream provides
    adequate and appropriate mixing
    for this application.
    Mechanical agitation would not
    improve the outcome and would
    add cost and complexity.
    The body is the blender.
    The bucket is the vessel.
    The system is complete.
```

### 1.2 — The Operational Protocol — Full Simplicity

```
THE COMPLETE OPERATING PROCEDURE:

  SETUP (once per reservoir):
    Pour dry ingredient mix into
    bucket to a depth of approximately
    2–3cm (visual indicator).
    This is the full setup.
    No measuring required in practice —
    a standard scoop or cup measure
    works for household use.
    The ratio is pre-mixed in bulk
    so the user pours a single mix,
    not four separate ingredients.

  USE (each event):
    Urinate into bucket.
    The stream provides the mixing.
    Observe: the liquid is absorbed
    within 30–120 seconds as SAP
    hydrates.
    The surface returns to a
    semi-solid or solid appearance.
    A faint fizzing (baking soda
    reacting) may be visible or audible.
    No odor should develop if
    borax loading is sufficient.
    Walk away. Nothing else required.

  FULL INDICATOR:
    The bucket is ready for rotation
    when one of two things occurs:
      VISUAL: The gel surface no longer
      flattens and re-solidifies after
      a urination event — it remains
      visibly liquid or pooled,
      indicating SAP absorption
      capacity is exhausted.
      VOLUME: The bucket is approaching
      physical capacity (gel expanding
      to near the rim).
    Either indicator is unambiguous
    and requires no measurement.
    A glance confirms readiness.

  ROTATION:
    Move the full bucket to the
    drying area.
    Place a fresh bucket with dry
    ingredients in the use position.
    The two-bucket rotation is the
    complete logistics system:
    one in use, one drying.
    At low use frequency (household):
    one bucket is sufficient —
    the gel dries between events.
    At high use frequency (public,
    event): two or more in rotation.

  DRYING AREA PROTOCOL:
    The full bucket is placed in
    a location with:
      Ventilation (not enclosed —
      the evaporating water should
      be able to leave).
      Ideally some warmth (sunlight,
      near a heat source) to
      accelerate evaporation.
      Protection from rain (which
      would rehydrate the gel and
      delay drying).
    No mechanical drying is required.
    Sun and air are the drying system.
    Timeline: 1–7 days depending
    on ambient temperature, humidity,
    and gel volume.

  HARVEST:
    When the bucket contents are
    fully dry — visually confirmed
    as a dry solid or powder that
    does not return to liquid on
    pressing — the contents are
    the harvest product.
    Scoop or pour the dry contents
    into a collection bag.
    The bucket is returned to service
    with a fresh dry ingredient charge.

  THE ENTIRE PROTOCOL REQUIRES:
    No power.
    No water.
    No mechanical equipment.
    No chemicals beyond the initial
    dry ingredient mix.
    No training beyond reading
    two paragraphs.
    No infrastructure beyond
    a bucket and outdoor drying space.

  THIS IS THE CORRECT LEVEL
  OF COMPLEXITY FOR THIS APPLICATION.
  Any additional complexity reduces
  the addressable market without
  improving the fundamental outcome.
```

### 3.2 — The Boron Consideration

```
THE BORON ISSUE — STATED PRECISELY:

  The harvest contains approximately
  2.24% boron by weight from the
  borax component.

  Agricultural boron requirements:
    Optimal soil boron: 0.2–0.5 mg/kg soil.
    Phytotoxicity threshold: 2–5 mg/kg
    for sensitive crops.
    Tolerant crops: up to 10 mg/kg.

  AT TYPICAL FERTILIZER APPLICATION RATES
  (100–500g/m²):
    Boron added at 100g harvest/m²:
    = 2.24g B / m².
    Soil depth 20cm, density 1.3kg/L:
    = 260kg soil/m².
    Boron concentration: 2,240mg / 260,000g
    = 8.6 mg B/kg soil.
    This is above the phytotoxicity
    threshold for sensitive crops.
    Below the threshold for tolerant crops.

  IMPLICATION:
    The harvest is NOT a general-purpose
    broadcast fertilizer at standard rates.
    It is suitable for:
      Boron-tolerant crops (sunflowers,
      sugar beet, asparagus, stone fruits):
      directly applicable.
      Diluted application: mix with
      4–8 parts compost or soil before
      applying — reduces effective boron
      concentration to safe levels.
      Boron-deficient soils: some soils
      (particularly sandy, acidic, or
      high-rainfall leached soils) are
      genuinely boron-deficient. The
      harvest is ideal for these soils.
      Hydroponic/controlled media:
      where inputs are precisely managed,
      the boron content is a calculable
      variable, not a problem.

  THE NO-BORAX FORMULA (P1S) FOR
  HARVESTING:

    This is where the formulation choice
    becomes operationally significant.

    P1S (no-borax) harvest boron: near zero.
    General fertilizer application: unrestricted.
    Broader crop and soil applicability.
    BUT: lower nitrogen retention
    (urease inhibition absent).
    Product has lower N content.
    More nitrogen lost to atmosphere.
    More odor during collection.

  THE TRADE-OFF IS REAL AND MUST BE NAMED:

    P1S-B HARVEST:
      Higher N content (borax urease inhibition).
      Better odor control.
      Boron content restricts direct application.
      Best for: boron-tolerant crops, diluted use,
      boron-deficient soils, controlled media.

    P1S HARVEST (no borax):
      Lower N content (~60–70% of P1S-B N).
      More odor during collection.
      No boron restriction.
      Best for: general broadcast fertilizer,
      boron-sensitive crops, unrestricted use.

  THE MARKET POSITIONS ARE DIFFERENT.
  Both are real markets.
  The choice of formula is a product
  positioning decision, not a chemistry failure.

MODIFIED FORMULA OPTION —
REDUCED BORAX FOR BALANCED HARVEST:

  If boron in the harvest is to be
  reduced while maintaining urease
  inhibition:
    The minimum effective borax for
    urease inhibition: ~1–2g/L urine.
    Per 250mL event: 0.25–0.5g borax needed.
    Original formula borax per event:
    9g borax per 36g total / 100g mix.
    At 100g mix per 3 events: 25g borax,
    ~8.3g per event.
    This is 16–33× the minimum effective dose.

  A REDUCED BORAX FORMULA:
    Reduce borax from 9g to 2g per
    equivalent batch unit.
    Urease inhibition maintained
    (still 4–8× minimum inhibitory
    concentration).
    Boron in harvest reduced by ~78%.
    Harvest boron content: ~0.5% B.
    Application rate: 4× higher before
    phytotoxicity threshold reached.
    Now suitable for broader crop range.

  FORMULA ADJUSTMENT (per 750mL water
  equivalent ratio):
    SAP: 8g (unchanged).
    Bentonite: 14g (unchanged).
    Borax: 2g (reduced from 9g).
    Baking soda: 5g (unchanged).
    Total dry: 29g.

  COST IMPACT: Minimal.
    Borax is cheap — reducing from
    9g to 2g saves ~$0.014 per batch.
    But allows a "general use fertilizer"
    product claim without boron restriction.

  RECOMMENDATION:
    For harvest-optimised deployment:
    use the reduced-borax formula.
    For fire/antimicrobial applications:
    use the full-borax P1S-B formula.
    Two formulas. Two applications.
    Same base ingredients.
```
