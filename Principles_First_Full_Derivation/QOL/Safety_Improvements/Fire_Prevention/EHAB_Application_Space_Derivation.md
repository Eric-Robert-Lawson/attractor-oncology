## Application 1.1 — Wood Modification Under Encapsulation
### (The Compressed Wood Experiment You Proposed)

**The Structural Question:**
If wood is completely encapsulated in EHAB gel, placed under compression,
and heat is applied externally — what happens to the wood?

**The Derivation:**

Normal wood modification under heat (torrefaction, thermal modification)
works by driving moisture and volatile organic compounds out of the wood
structure and partially degrading the hemicellulose matrix. This changes:
- Dimensional stability (thermally modified wood swells and shrinks less)
- Density (slightly reduced)
- Hardness (can increase or decrease depending on degree)
- Color (darkens uniformly)
- Biological resistance (reduced food source for fungi and insects)

The problem with standard thermal modification: the process is difficult
to control at the surface. The outer layers of the wood overheat relative
to the interior, creating a thermal gradient that produces uneven modification
— over-modified outer layers, under-modified interior.

EHAB gel changes the geometry of this process fundamentally.

The gel coating absorbs incoming heat and distributes it via evaporation
across the entire surface simultaneously. The gel acts as a thermal
equalizer. The wood surface never exceeds the temperature of the
evaporating water (100°C at atmospheric pressure) until the gel is
fully depleted in any given zone.

**What this potentially produces:**

A wood modification process where:
- The outer surface is thermally buffered to approximately 100°C while
  the interior heats gradually via conduction
- Modification begins from the inside out rather than outside in
- When the gel finally depletes in any zone, the wood beneath has already
  been pre-conditioned by internal heating
- The result is a more uniformly modified wood with less surface char
  and more consistent interior modification

Under compression simultaneously:
- The gel fills the wood's surface pores under pressure
- As the gel dries and the silica aerogel forms, silica is forced into
  the wood surface pore structure
- The aerogel residue may partially impregnate the wood surface layer
- After cooling and gel removal, the wood surface may have a silica-
  impregnated outer layer — increasing fire resistance, hardness, and
  potentially dimensional stability

**What to test:**
```
Test setup:
  - Two matched wood samples (same species, same cut, same moisture content)
  - Sample A: heated without gel (control — standard thermal modification)
  - Sample B: fully coated in EHAB gel, compressed, same heat applied
  
Heat source: oven at 200-220°C for 60 minutes
Compression: clamps applying moderate pressure (not enough to crush,
             enough to maintain gel contact with all surfaces)

Measurements after cooling and gel removal:
  1. Surface color uniformity (photograph, compare)
  2. Surface hardness (scratch test with known materials as reference)
  3. Water repellency (drop of water on surface — contact angle)
  4. Weight loss % (directly measures degree of thermal modification)
  5. Any silica impregnation visible at surface (white sheen or film)
```

**Feasibility with current materials:** HIGH. You have everything needed.
This test can be run in a kitchen oven.

**Potential value:** MODERATE-HIGH. If the uniformity improvement is real,
this is a novel wood modification technique accessible at extremely low cost.
Commercial thermal modification equipment costs tens of thousands of dollars.
An EHAB-mediated modification process could be done in any oven.

## Application 1.2 — Controlled Firing of Ceramics and Clay
### (The Kiln Buffer Concept)

**The Derivation:**

Ceramics crack during firing because of thermal gradients — the outside
heats faster than the inside. Potters and ceramic engineers spend enormous
effort managing firing ramps (very slow temperature increases) to minimize
these gradients.

EHAB gel applied to a green (unfired) ceramic piece before firing would
buffer the surface temperature to approximately 100°C during gel depletion,
buying time for the interior to catch up thermally. This could allow
faster firing ramps without cracking.

Additionally, the silica aerogel residue left by the gel is already a
precursor ceramic material — compatible with ceramic surfaces. Rather than
contaminating the ceramic, the silica residue may integrate into the
surface layer during high-temperature firing.

**Feasibility:** MODERATE. Requires access to a kiln or high-temperature
oven. Not testable at kitchen scale above 220°C.

**Potential value:** LOW-MODERATE. The ceramic field has well-developed
methods for controlled firing. The marginal improvement from gel buffering
would need to be substantial to be practically valuable.

## Application 1.3 — Battery Thermal Runaway Containment
### (The Lithium Battery Fire Problem)

**The Problem:**

Lithium-ion battery thermal runaway is one of the most difficult fire
problems in modern industry. When a Li-ion cell enters thermal runaway,
it produces temperatures of 500-900°C, ejects flaming electrolyte,
and can propagate to adjacent cells. The fire is self-fueling because
the battery contains its own oxidizer (lithium oxide compounds).
Standard fire suppression (water, CO₂, dry chemical) is largely
ineffective at preventing propagation because it cannot simultaneously:
- Cool the surface fast enough
- Exclude oxygen
- Persist long enough for the internal reaction to exhaust itself

**The EHAB Geometry:**

A battery pack pre-coated or encased in EHAB gel presents a fundamentally
different geometry to thermal runaway propagation:
- The gel absorbs thermal energy from the runaway cell before it reaches
  adjacent cells
- The oxygen exclusion layer prevents the ejected electrolyte from
  igniting on adjacent surfaces
- The aerogel residue persists after water depletion, continuing to
  insulate adjacent cells from conducted heat
- The CO₂ foaming (Phase 2) directly suppresses any ignited electrolyte
  vapor at the surface

The specific property that makes EHAB interesting for this application
is the persistence through Phase D — most cooling agents (water sprays,
CO₂ systems) provide momentary cooling then leave the surface exposed.
The aerogel remains.

**Feasibility as a bench test:** LOW with current equipment.
Li-ion thermal runaway requires a controlled environment and safety
equipment beyond what the bench protocol uses.

**Feasibility as a concept for development:** HIGH. This is a real
and growing industrial problem (EV fires, battery storage fires).
The concept is testable at a professional level.

**Potential value:** VERY HIGH. Battery fire containment is an active
area of research with significant commercial urgency. If EHAB gel can
demonstrably slow propagation between cells in a pack, the application
value is substantial. This is the highest-value long-term application
identified in this analysis.

**What to note:** This application requires Phase D performance to be
strong — the aerogel must persist for the 10-30 minutes it takes a
thermal runaway event to exhaust itself. This is a demanding requirement
and the bench Phase D results will directly predict whether this
application is viable.

## Application 2.1 — Flammability Reduction of Fabrics and Textiles
### (The Clothing Fireproofing Question You Asked)

**The Derivation:**

Textile fire resistance works through one of three mechanisms:
1. Inherently fire-resistant fibers (Nomex, Kevlar, PBI) — expensive,
   specialized
2. Chemical treatment (flame retardant finishes — typically halogenated
   compounds or phosphorus-based chemistry) — raises environmental and
   toxicity concerns
3. Intumescent coatings — swell under heat, protecting the substrate

EHAB gel applied to fabric acts via a fourth mechanism: water-retention
barrier with aerogel transition. This is genuinely different from all
three existing approaches.

**The Threshold Question You Asked:**

Yes, there is a threshold per unit measurement. For a fabric:

The relevant threshold is the minimum gel mass per unit area of fabric
required to:
- Absorb enough heat to prevent the fiber from reaching its ignition
  temperature during a defined exposure
- Form a continuous enough aerogel layer to cover the fabric surface

Both thresholds scale with fiber type (ignition temperature) and heat flux
(severity of fire exposure). Cotton ignites at approximately 255°C. Nylon
at 310-390°C. Polyester at 450-485°C. Lower ignition temperature requires
more gel per unit area for the same protection duration.

**What to test:**
```
Kindling experiment (as you proposed):

Materials:
  - 6 wood kindling sticks, matched (same wood, same size, same moisture)
  - 6 fabric samples (same size — 10cm × 10cm):
    Test A: Cotton
    Test B: Polyester
    Test C: Mixed fabric (if available)
  
Groups:
  - Control kindling: bare, torch applied
  - Coated kindling: 2mm EHAB gel applied, dried 5 min, torch applied
  - Control fabric: bare, torch applied  
  - Coated fabric: 2mm EHAB gel applied, torch applied
  - Half-coated fabric: gel applied to one half only, torch at boundary
    (tests whether gel prevents propagation from uncoated to coated area)

Measurements:
  - Time to first ignition
  - Speed of flame propagation across surface
  - Total mass consumed after 60s exposure (weigh before and after)
  - Whether ignition in uncoated zone propagates to coated zone
```

**The Dry vs. Wet Limitation:**

This is the critical honest caveat for fabric application:

The EHAB gel's fire protection depends on the presence of water.
A wet gel-coated fabric resists fire. A dry gel-coated fabric (gel has
fully dried before fire exposure) relies only on the aerogel residue for
protection — which is less effective on a porous, flexible substrate
than on a rigid surface.

This means:
- **Wet application (gel freshly applied):** Strong protection. Works.
- **Dried application (applied then dried before fire exposure):**
  Weaker protection. The aerogel residue on fabric is fragile and
  will flake off with flexing.
- **Permanent textile treatment:** NOT viable with this formulation
  without further development. The gel is a temporary coating.

**What this means for practical applications:**

Short-duration protective use (emergency coating before a known
fire exposure event): VIABLE. A gel-coated garment worn into a
brief high-heat environment — think a firefighter entering a structure
for a short duration — could have meaningfully extended thermal protection.

Long-duration permanent treatment: NOT VIABLE with current formulation.
The water evaporates. The aerogel flakes off flexible fabric.

**Potential value for fabric:** MODERATE for temporary/emergency use.
LOW for permanent textile treatment without further development.

## Application 3.1 — Flammable Liquid Transport Encapsulation
### (The Question You Asked About Transport)

**The Derivation:**

You asked specifically: can this substance be useful in transporting
flammable materials through encapsulation?

This is one of the most structurally interesting applications because
it exploits oxygen exclusion rather than thermal buffering.

**The Geometry:**

Flammable liquids (gasoline, ethanol, acetone, petroleum products)
require three things to ignite: fuel vapor, oxygen, ignition source.
The fuel is present by definition. The ignition source is a hazard
to be managed separately. Oxygen exclusion eliminates the third
requirement.

A container or pipe interior coated with EHAB gel excludes oxygen
from the inner surface, preventing vapor ignition at the surface even
if a spark is present.

More specifically: if a flammable liquid container is coated on the
interior with EHAB gel, and the liquid is in contact with the gel:
- The gel excludes oxygen at the liquid surface
- The gel's water phase partially dissolves into the flammable liquid
  surface layer, diluting any ignitable vapor
- If a leak occurs and the outside of the container is also coated,
  the leaked liquid contacts gel rather than air at the leak point,
  suppressing immediate ignition

**The Wax Encapsulation Transport Concept:**

Your specific question about wax encapsulation for transport is
geometrically elegant. Consider:

A flammable solid (say, a reactive metal powder, or a pyrophoric
material) individually encapsulated in paraffin wax (the same wax
in your Phase 2 protocol) creates a material that:
- Cannot ignite at room temperature (wax excludes oxygen and moisture)
- Is inert to shipping and handling
- Releases its reactive content when heat is applied (controlled process)
  OR when the wax is mechanically breached at the point of use

This is not a new concept — it exists commercially for some reactive
metals (sodium metal is sometimes shipped in paraffin oil). But the
EHAB framework provides a derivation path to extending this concept
using the EHAB gel as the outer encapsulation layer around wax-coated
reactive materials, providing a double barrier:
- Wax inner layer: excludes moisture and oxygen from reactive material
- EHAB gel outer layer: thermal buffer preventing accidental heat-triggered
  wax melting during transport in hot environments

**Feasibility with current materials:** MODERATE-HIGH for concept testing.
You can test the wax encapsulation of small flammable solid samples using
your current paraffin wax supply.

**Potential value:** MODERATE. Specialized transport of reactive materials
is an existing regulated industry. A dual-layer wax-EHAB system would
need regulatory engagement. But the principle is sound.

## Application 3.2 — Welding and Hot Work on Flammable Substrates

**The Derivation:**

Hot work (welding, grinding, cutting) on or near flammable substrates
is a leading cause of industrial fires. Standard practice is to remove
all flammable materials from the area, use fire blankets, and have a
fire watch. Fire blankets are passive and do not absorb heat.

A surface treated with EHAB gel before welding would:
- Absorb heat from sparks and spatter via evaporative cooling
  (sparks landing on wet gel surface are immediately quenched)
- Exclude oxygen from the substrate surface (preventing ignition
  even if a spark penetrates)
- Persist through the work period (the gel takes time to dry, providing
  continuous protection)

**This is a testable application with your current setup:**

```
Test:
  - One panel of plywood, bare (control)
  - One panel of plywood, EHAB gel coated at 3mm
  - Simulate sparks: hold a steel wire brush against a spinning wheel
    (or simply drop burning matches / lit steel wool) onto both panels
  - Record: how many simulated sparks cause ignition on bare wood?
    How many on gel-coated wood?
  - The ratio is the "spark rejection efficiency"
```

**Feasibility with current materials:** HIGH. Directly testable.
This does not require a torch — simulated sparks from matches or
burning steel wool are sufficient to establish the principle.

**Potential value:** HIGH for industrial application. Hot work fire
prevention is a real and ongoing industrial safety problem. A cheap,
conformally-applied gel that can be quickly applied to adjacent surfaces
before hot work begins (and washed off afterward with water) has clear
industrial utility.

**The Derivation:**

The wax encapsulation architecture in Phase 2 is a general-purpose

- Active at the application condition
- Compatible with water (the gel medium)

**What else can be encapsulated in wax and delivered by this mechanism?**

```
CANDIDATE ENCAPSULATED AGENTS:

Agent: Ammonium phosphate powder
Trigger: 65°C wax melt
Effect: Additional fire retardant released at fire surface
        (phosphorus chemistry interrupts combustion chain reaction)
Value: Stronger fire suppression than CO₂ alone
Risk: Ammonium phosphate is slightly toxic — acceptable for industrial,
      not ideal for interior residential

Agent: Potassium bicarbonate (instead of sodium bicarbonate)
Trigger: Same wax melt
Effect: Same CO₂ production but potassium ion byproduct

Value: Potentially stronger suppression than NaHCO₃ version
Risk: Minimal — potassium bicarbonate is food-safe

Agent: Essential oils or fragrance compounds
Trigger: 45°C wax melt (lower melting wax)
Effect: Timed fragrance release in a room when ambient temperature rises
Value: Consumer product concept (home fragrance with heat trigger)
       Obviously not fire-related — demonstrates how far the

Risk: None

Trigger: 60°C wax melt
Effect: Adsorption of combustion byproducts (CO, volatile organics)

## Application 4.2 — Phase Change Material Integration

**The Derivation:**

Phase change materials (PCMs) are used in building thermal management —
they absorb heat as they melt (at a specific temperature) and release
heat as they solidify. They are used in walls, ceilings, and floors to
smooth out temperature fluctuations in buildings.

The EHAB gel is already partially a phase change material (water
evaporation is the phase change). But the wax capsules in Phase 2 are
also a phase change material — the wax melts, absorbing heat.

A reformulated EHAB gel using a higher-latent-heat wax (or a fatty acid
like lauric acid, myristic acid) encapsulated in the gel matrix would be
a building thermal buffer gel — not for fire protection, but for
passive building temperature regulation.

Applied to walls and ceilings, the gel would absorb heat during hot
periods and release it during cool periods, reducing HVAC load.

**Feasibility:** MODERATE. Requires reformulation with different
encapsulants. Not testable with current Phase 2 protocol directly.

**Potential value:** MODERATE. Building thermal management is a large
market but has established PCM solutions. The novelty would be the
conformal gel application format versus existing PCM board products.

**Feasibility:** HIGH. Testable with a pot of soil, EHAB gel applied

## Application 6.1 — Flame Focusing Through a Non-Combustible Gel Substrate

**The Derivation:**

You specifically asked: can the gel be used in manufacturing or scientific
applications as a substrate through which a flame operates — where the
gel is the surrounding material and the flame is focused through it or
contained by it?

This is a structurally interesting concept. Let me derive what is
actually happening:

If you have a wood substrate fully coated in EHAB gel, and you apply
flame to one spot:
- The flame contacts gel, not wood
- The gel is non-combustible (locally)
- The heat from the flame passes through the gel and into the wood beneath
- The wood beneath heats and may eventually pyrolyze
- But the gel at the flame contact point also absorbs and distributes heat

**The net effect:** The gel does NOT prevent heat from reaching the wood.
It slows the rate of heat transfer and homogenizes the spatial distribution
of heat. The flame does not "focus" through the gel — it is attenuated
and distributed.

**The application this enables:**

**Controlled local wood modification without surface charring.**

Normally if you want to locally harden or modify the surface of wood
(as in some traditional Japanese wood treatments like Yakisugi/Shou Sugi Ban),
you apply flame directly, which chars the surface. The char is then
removed, leaving a hardened, modified surface beneath.

With a gel coating, you could apply flame to a gel-coated wood surface.
The gel prevents surface charring while allowing thermal energy to penetrate
and modify the wood beneath. When the gel is removed (washed with water),
the wood surface is thermally modified without char — a potentially novel
surface treatment producing hardened, dimensionally stabilized wood without
the char layer.

**Testable immediately:**
```
Test:

  Sample B: Gel-coated wood, same torch, same duration, gel washed off
  
Measurements:

## Application 6.2 — The Focused Heat Source as a Scientific Instrument

**The Derivation:**

In materials science, there is frequent need to apply a controlled heat
flux to a specific zone of a material without affecting surrounding areas.
Current methods: laser heating, induction heating, focused hot gas jets.
All are expensive and require specialized equipment.

A gel mask — EHAB gel applied to all areas of a material except the
target zone — provides a cheap, conformal, self-removing thermal mask.
Heat applied to the exposed zone penetrates the material. Heat applied
to the gel-covered zones is absorbed and distributed by the gel.

When the experiment is complete, the gel washes off with water.
No residue (or minimal silica residue that can be abraded off).

**Applications within materials science:**
- Selective grain growth in metals (heat one zone to modify crystal structure)
- Selective tempering of steel (apply gel to zones that should not be tempered)
- Selective annealing of glass or ceramics
- Localized surface chemistry (heating to drive a surface reaction in
  a specific zone)

**Feasibility:** HIGH for concept. The gel is a functional thermal mask
at kitchen-lab scale. The specific scientific applications require

## Application 7.1 — Acoustic Damping and Vibration Isolation

**The Derivation:**

Hydrogels in general, and SAP-based gels specifically, are viscoelastic
materials — they have both viscous (liquid-like) and elastic (solid-like)
responses to mechanical stress. This viscoelasticity is highly effective
at converting mechanical vibration energy to heat (damping).

The bentonite clay network reinforces the viscoelastic behavior by adding
a time-dependent structural recovery element.

EHAB gel applied between two surfaces — as a gasket, a fill material,
or an interface layer — would provide:
- Mechanical vibration damping (absorbs vibration energy)
- Acoustic isolation (reduces sound transmission between surfaces)
- Conformal interfacial contact (fills gaps and surface irregularities)

**Where this is immediately testable:**
Apply gel between two wood panels. Place one panel face-down on a table.
Strike the upper panel. Listen to and feel the vibration transmitted
to the lower panel. Compare to two bare wood panels in direct contact.

**Potential applications:**
- Industrial machinery vibration isolation
- Acoustic wall panels (gel-filled cavities)
- Pipe vibration damping (gel wrapped around pipes to reduce noise)
- Musical instrument construction (controlled resonance modification)

**Potential value:** MODERATE. Vibration damping materials are a
real engineering need. The EHAB gel's advantage is conformability
and low cost. The disadvantage is that it is a wet material — long-term
applications would require enclosure to prevent drying.

## Application 7.2 — Radiation Shielding (Neutron Moderation)

**The Derivation:**

Water is the primary neutron moderator and shield in nuclear applications.
Hydrogen atoms in water scatter and absorb neutrons more effectively
than almost any other material. Hydrogels are approximately 95% water
by mass in their fully swollen state.

SAP hydrogel has a higher water retention per unit volume than free water
in a container — because the SAP holds the water even when the hydrogel
is deformed, compressed, or placed in configurations where free water
would flow away.

A conformal SAP hydrogel coating on the interior surfaces of a container
or room provides:
- A hydrogen-rich shielding layer that conforms to any surface geometry
- Retention of the shielding material against gravity (the gel holds
  to vertical and overhead surfaces)
- A self-sealing property (the gel fills surface irregularities and gaps
  that would be penetration paths for a fitted solid shield)

**The EHAB addition:** Bentonite clay contains significant amounts of
boron-containing minerals in some geological sources. Boron-10 is one
of the most effective neutron absorbers known. Boron-doped bentonite
(or simply substituting some bentonite with borax in the formulation)
would produce a gel that both moderates neutrons (hydrogen from water)
and absorbs thermal neutrons (boron).

**Feasibility with current materials:** The concept is derivable but
not testable at kitchen scale. Radiation measurements require specialized
detectors.

**Potential value:** MODERATE-HIGH in specialized nuclear and medical
radiation applications (medical imaging rooms, nuclear facility walls,
radiation therapy shielding). The conformability is the differentiating
property — current neutron shielding is done with fixed panels that
cannot conform to complex geometries.

## Application 7.3 — Emergency Medical: Burn Wound Cooling and Covering

**The Derivation:**

The standard first aid for burns is: cool running water for 10-20 minutes,
then cover with a non-stick sterile dressing. The cooling is provided
by water. The covering is provided by a separate dressing.

EHAB gel (reformulated without bentonite clay — which could contaminate
wounds — and without CO₂ foaming) would simultaneously:
- Cool the burn surface through evaporative cooling
- Cover the wound, excluding contaminants and air
- Reduce pain (burns exposed to air are extremely painful;
  the gel provides an immediate barrier and the cooling reduces
  nerve stimulation)
- Not adhere to wound tissue (SAP gel is non-adherent to biological tissue
  at appropriate concentrations)

The existing commercial product in this space is hydrogel burn dressings —
already SAP-based in many cases. The EHAB contribution would be:
- Lower cost than current hydrogel dressings
- Conformable to any wound geometry including large surface area burns
- Deliverable by spray or pour rather than requiring placement of a
  pre-formed sheet

**IMPORTANT ETHICAL AND SAFETY NOTE:**

and sterility that are entirely beyond the scope of this bench protocol.
This application is listed as a long-term derivation, not a bench test.
Do NOT test any formulation of this gel on human skin at this stage.

**Potential value:** HIGH if validated through proper clinical pathway.
Burn wound management is a significant medical market. The cost
differential between EHAB gel and commercial hydrogel wound dressings
is very large, which would be particularly valuable in resource-limited
settings.

## Application 7.4 — Food Preservation: Controlled Atmosphere Packaging

**The Derivation:**

Fresh food spoils primarily through two mechanisms: oxidation (oxygen
reacts with food components) and microbial growth (bacteria and fungi
consume oxygen and produce spoilage compounds). Both are driven by
oxygen access.

The EHAB gel's oxygen exclusion property, applied to food storage,
would create a conformal oxygen barrier around any food surface.

More specifically: a gel applied to the cut surface of a fruit or
vegetable (the highest spoilage risk surface) would:
- Exclude oxygen from the cut surface
- Retain moisture (preventing desiccation of the cut surface)
- Be food-safe (all EHAB components are food-grade — SAP is used in
  food packaging, silica is a food additive E551, bentonite is used
  as a wine clarifying agent, baking soda and citric acid are food ingredients)

**Testable immediately:**
```
Test:
  - Cut an apple in half
  - Apply EHAB gel to the cut surface of one half (test)
  - Leave the other half bare (control)
  - Photograph both every 30 minutes for 4 hours
  - Measure browning (oxidation) progression
  
Expected result: gel-coated half browns significantly more slowly
because oxygen is excluded from the polyphenol oxidase reaction
at the cut surface.
```

This is immediately testable with your current materials. It is a
compelling visual demonstration of the oxygen-exclusion property
in a non-fire context. It could be performed the same day you make
your first gel batch, before any fire testing.

**Potential value for food:** LOW for commercial development
(existing solutions like modified atmosphere packaging are well
established). HIGH for demonstration purposes — it is a vivid,
accessible, photogenic demonstration of what the gel does physically.

## Application 7.5 — Architectural Passive Fire Compartmentalization

**The Derivation:**

Building codes require fire-rated walls and floors — structural elements
that resist fire passage for defined time periods (30 min, 60 min, 120 min).
Achieving these ratings requires specific construction methods, materials,
and thicknesses that significantly increase construction cost and limit
architectural flexibility.

A building designed with EHAB gel delivery systems built into wall
cavities — where the gel is pre-positioned in wall cavities and dispensed
onto interior surfaces in the event of fire — could achieve fire
compartmentalization performance through an active surface encapsulation
system rather than through passive construction material selection.

This inverts the architecture of fire protection:
- Current: build fire-resistant structures → fire cannot penetrate
- EHAB concept: build normal structures + deploy encapsulation at fire onset
  → fire cannot propagate because surfaces are encapsulated

**The enabling property:** EHAB gel is deployable through pipes and
nozzles (the thixotropic property). A building with a gel delivery network
(similar in concept to a sprinkler system but delivering gel rather than water)
could apply EHAB gel to wall and ceiling surfaces in a fire zone, rapidly
converting a standard wood-frame room into an encapsulated fire barrier.

**Potential value:** VERY HIGH if validated. This is a fundamentally
different paradigm for building fire protection with direct implications
for construction cost (cheaper construction + active system vs. expensive
fire-rated construction). It is also the application most aligned with
the original geometric derivation of the substance.
