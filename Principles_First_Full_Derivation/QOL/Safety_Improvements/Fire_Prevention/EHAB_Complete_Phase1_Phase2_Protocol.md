## BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING — READ THIS SECTION COMPLETELY

### The One Rule That Cannot Be Broken

The fumed silica powder is the only genuinely hazardous material in this
entire protocol. Everything else — SAP, bentonite, baking soda, citric acid,
paraffin wax — is either food-grade or cosmetic-grade and poses minimal risk.

Fumed silica is different. It is an ultra-fine powder with particle sizes
measured in nanometers — far smaller than anything you can see. A single
gram contains trillions of particles. When disturbed, these particles float
invisibly in the air for minutes. If inhaled, they lodge permanently in lung
tissue and cause a condition called silicosis, which is irreversible. There
is no treatment. There is no antidote. Prevention is the only option.

**The rule: Your N95 respirator goes ON before the fumed silica container
is opened. It does not come off until the powder is fully mixed into water
and no dry powder is visible anywhere. This applies every single time you
open that container, no exceptions.**

A butane torch also produces an open flame. All fire tests are conducted
outdoors, or in a space with a door and windows fully open. You have a
metal sample holder — the burning test sample never sits on anything that
can catch fire. A bowl of water large enough to submerge a burning sample
is placed within arm's reach before any torch is lit.

### What to Expect Before You Start

You are making a material that does not currently exist as a commercial
product for interior fire protection. You are following a protocol derived
from first principles and triangulated against research, not a recipe
copied from a manufacturer's sheet. This means:

- The first batch will not be perfect. This is expected and designed for.
- You will observe things that are not in this document. Write them down.
- The failure modes listed at the end are the most likely things to go wrong.
  Read them before you start so you recognize them when they happen.
- The goal of Phase 1 is not a finished product. The goal is a validated
  baseline formulation that passes five simple criteria. Getting there
  takes 2-4 mixing sessions of 1-2 hours each.

## WORKSPACE SETUP — DO THIS BEFORE OPENING ANYTHING

**Time required: 10 minutes**

1. Clear a flat, stable work surface. A kitchen counter or workbench is ideal.
   The surface should be easy to wipe clean — you will be working with powders
   and gels that will spill.

2. Lay down a sheet of newspaper, a garbage bag opened flat, or paper towels
   across the work area. This makes cleanup much easier and protects the surface
   from staining (bentonite clay can stain light-colored countertops if left wet).

3. Place the following within arm's reach on the work surface before you
   open any containers:
   - Kitchen scale
   - Two large mixing bowls (one for SAP, one for compounding)
   - One medium mixing bowl (for bentonite)
   - One small mixing bowl or glass jar (for fumed silica)
   - Spatula or large spoon (two if you have them)
   - Whisk (a standard kitchen whisk works perfectly)
   - Measuring cups or a separate vessel for measuring water
   - A pen and a notepad or your phone for recording observations
   - Your N95 respirator, within reach but not yet on

4. Put on your nitrile gloves now. You will wear them for the entire session.
   The SAP and bentonite are not harmful, but gloves keep your hands clean
   and prevent skin oils from affecting the gel properties.

5. Have the distilled water jug accessible. Do not use tap water for any
   mixing step. The minerals in tap water, particularly calcium and magnesium,
   interfere with both the SAP's swelling and the bentonite's gelation. The
   distilled water is specifically for this reason.

## PHASE 1 — STEP 1: SAP HYDRATION

**What this step does:**
You are taking 10 grams of dry sodium polyacrylate powder and turning it into
approximately 1 liter of thick, clear gel by adding water. The polymer chains
in the SAP absorb water and swell, creating the sticky, viscous network that
will hold everything else in the final product and adhere to surfaces.

**What you will need for this step:**
- SAP powder: 10g
- Distilled water: 1000mL (approximately 4 standard US cups, or just under 1 quart)
- One large mixing bowl (the largest you have — the gel looks like it will fit
  in a small bowl but having extra space makes mixing much easier)
- Kitchen scale
- Large spoon or spatula
- Your phone or a clock for timing

---

**STEP 1A — WEIGH THE SAP**

1. Place your large mixing bowl on the kitchen scale.

2. Press the "tare" or "zero" button on your scale. The display should read 0.0g
   with the empty bowl on it. This removes the bowl's weight from the reading so
   you are measuring only the powder you add.

3. Open the SAP powder bag. The powder looks like small white crystals or fine
   white granules — similar in appearance to salt or sugar but slightly different
   texture. This is normal.

4. Slowly pour SAP powder from the bag into the bowl while watching the scale
   display. Pour slowly — the powder is light and a small amount adds quickly.

5. Stop adding when the scale reads 10.0g. If you slightly overshoot, use a
   small spoon to remove a tiny amount until you reach 10.0g.
   
   10 grams of SAP looks like a very small amount — roughly a heaping teaspoon.
   This is correct. SAP is extremely effective at low concentrations.

6. Set the bag down, sealed. Remove the bowl from the scale.

**What you should have now:**
A large bowl with approximately 1 heaping teaspoon of white powder in the bottom.
The bowl looks almost empty. This is correct.

---

**STEP 1B — ADD WATER TO THE SAP**

This is the most important part of Step 1. You must add water slowly — not all
at once. If you dump 1000mL of water onto 10g of SAP at once, you get a lumpy,
uneven gel with hard clumps that are extremely difficult to break up. Adding in
increments gives each addition time to absorb and distribute before the next
addition arrives.

1. Measure 1000mL of distilled water. If you do not have a measuring cup that
   holds 1000mL, use a 250mL (1 cup) measure and fill it four times.

2. Pour approximately 100mL (about half a cup) of distilled water into the bowl
   with the SAP powder.

3. Immediately begin stirring with your spoon or spatula. Stir continuously and
   vigorously, scraping the bottom of the bowl to incorporate the powder.

4. You will notice the powder begin to swell immediately. Some areas will form
   small gel lumps. Keep stirring — the lumps will soften and incorporate as
   you mix.

5. After 1 minute of stirring, add another 100mL of water. Keep stirring.

6. Continue this pattern: add 100mL, stir 1 minute, add 100mL, stir 1 minute.
   Repeat until all 1000mL has been added.

   The total process of adding 1000mL in ten 100mL increments with 1 minute
   of stirring between each addition takes approximately 12-15 minutes.
   Do not rush this. The slow addition makes a dramatic difference in the
   smoothness of the final gel.

7. Once all water has been added and you have stirred thoroughly after the
   final addition, set the bowl aside and allow it to rest undisturbed for
   30 minutes.

   During this rest period, the SAP fully hydrates. The gel will thicken
   noticeably during this time. You will see it become more gel-like and
   less liquid as the polymer chains absorb their maximum water.

**What you should have after 30 minutes:**
A bowl containing approximately 1 liter of clear to slightly hazy gel.
It should look somewhat like very thick aloe vera gel, or thick hair
conditioner — not completely clear like water, but not opaque like paint.
It holds a shape briefly when you scoop it with a spoon but flows and
settles within 5-10 seconds.

If it looks like a liquid (pours freely like water with only slight thickness):
The SAP concentration is too low, or the SAP did not absorb fully yet.
Wait an additional 15 minutes. If still too thin, add 2g more SAP
(in 0.5g increments, mixed thoroughly with 15 min wait between each).

If it looks like a solid (does not flow at all, forms a stiff rubbery mass):
The SAP absorbed more water than expected, or your brand of SAP is more
concentrated than the baseline. Add 100mL of distilled water, stir thoroughly,
wait 10 minutes, assess again.

- Time to full hydration (after all water added + rest): ____min
- Consistency description (circle one or write your own):
  watery / slightly thick / thick like conditioner / thick like gel / solid rubbery
- Any observations about the process (lumps, unusual color, smell, etc.):

## PHASE 1 — STEP 2: FUMED SILICA DISPERSION

**STOP. Before you open the fumed silica container, put your N95 respirator on.**

Read that sentence again. Put the N95 on now, before you reach for the container.
Gloves should already be on from workspace setup.

**What this step does:**
You are taking 25 grams of fumed silica powder — an invisible white powder that
floats in air for several minutes when disturbed — and dispersing it into 200mL
of water to create a uniform milky suspension. This suspension, when incorporated
into the final gel and then exposed to heat, will self-assemble into a silica
aerogel layer on the surface. That aerogel layer is the Phase C protection —
the persistent barrier that remains after the water in the gel has evaporated.

**What you will need for this step:**
- Fumed silica powder: 25g
- Distilled water: 200mL
- One small mixing bowl or glass jar (the fumed silica suspension is only
  about 225mL total, so a glass jar works well)
- Kitchen scale
- Whisk (preferred) or spoon
- N95 respirator (ALREADY ON)
- Gloves (already on)

---

**STEP 2A — WEIGH THE FUMED SILICA**

1. N95 respirator must be on. Confirm this before proceeding.

2. Place your small bowl or glass jar on the scale. Tare/zero the scale.

3. Open the fumed silica container slowly and gently. Do not shake or tap it.
   The powder is so fine that simply opening the container disturbs some
   particles into the air.

4. Using a spoon, very slowly scoop small amounts of fumed silica into the
   bowl on the scale. Pour as close to the bowl surface as possible — do not
   pour from height, as this creates a dust cloud.

5. Stop when the scale reads 25.0g.

6. Close the fumed silica container immediately after weighing. Do not leave
   it open while you work.

**What 25g of fumed silica looks like:**
A surprisingly large volume of very fluffy, extremely light white powder.
Fumed silica has a very low bulk density — 25g fills a volume roughly the
size of a large orange. It looks like compressed white smoke or extremely
fine flour.

---

**STEP 2B — DISPERSE THE FUMED SILICA INTO WATER**

Fumed silica is hydrophobic when dry — it naturally repels water and will
float on the surface rather than sink and dissolve. This is normal and expected.
You must force it into the water using mixing energy.

1. Add 200mL of distilled water to a separate container (not the bowl with
   the fumed silica powder — you will add the powder TO the water, not water
   to the powder).

2. You will add the silica to the water in five portions. Divide your 25g
   mentally into five approximately equal portions of 5g each.

3. Slowly sift the first 5g portion of fumed silica powder into the water
   by gently tapping the bowl over the water. The powder will initially
   float on the water surface as a white raft. This is expected.

4. Immediately begin whisking vigorously. The goal is to force the floating
   powder down into the water. Whisk in a circular motion, then a figure-8,
   then straight back and forth — vary the motion to break up the floating
   powder.

5. Whisk for 1 full minute. By the end, most of the powder should be
   incorporated into the water (the water will look cloudy/milky).

6. Add the next 5g portion. Whisk for 1 minute. Repeat until all 25g
   has been added and whisked in.

7. After all silica is added, whisk continuously for a final 3 minutes.
   The suspension should look uniformly milky — like skim milk or very
   diluted white paint. There should be no white powder floating on the
   surface. If there is still floating powder, continue whisking.

8. If you have access to an immersion blender (a hand blender / "stick blender"),
   use it for 2-3 minutes at this point instead of the whisk. It produces much
   finer and more uniform dispersion. If you only have a whisk, the whisk is
   sufficient but requires more effort.

9. Allow the silica suspension to rest for 10 minutes. After resting, look at
   the surface. If dry white powder has re-floated to the surface, it means
   some silica was not fully wetted. Whisk again for 2 minutes.

10. Once no floating powder is visible, you can carefully remove your N95
    respirator — the silica is now locked in water and cannot become airborne
    again. The container is sealed. The risk is eliminated.

    **You may remove your respirator once the silica is fully in water and
    the fumed silica container is sealed. Not before.**

**What you should have:**
A small bowl or jar containing approximately 225mL of milky white liquid.
It should look uniformly cloudy throughout — not clear liquid with white
powder sitting on the bottom. It will feel slightly slippery if you rub
a drop between your fingers. This is normal and expected.

If the liquid is clear with white sediment on the bottom: the silica
settled out because it was not adequately dispersed. Return the N95 to
your face (the act of stirring can re-suspend particles), whisk vigorously
again for 3 minutes, then allow to settle again and check.

**Record in your notes:**
- Fumed silica mass used: ____g
- Water volume used: ____mL
- Appearance after dispersion (circle): clear / slightly cloudy / milky / opaque white
- Floating powder visible after rest? Y / N
- Respirator used throughout silica handling? Y / N (must be Y)
- Any observations:

## PHASE 1 — STEP 3: BENTONITE CLAY DISPERSION

**What this step does:**
You are dispersing 30 grams of sodium bentonite clay into 500mL of water.
Bentonite clay swells when hydrated and the individual clay platelets form
a loosely connected network throughout the water. This network is what gives
the final gel its thixotropic behavior — the property where the gel holds its
shape when still but flows when stirred or squeezed. Without bentonite, the
gel would run off vertical surfaces. The bentonite is what makes it stay.

**What you will need for this step:**
- Sodium bentonite clay powder: 30g
- Distilled water: 500mL
- One medium mixing bowl
- Kitchen scale
- Whisk
- Gloves (already on)
- No respirator required for bentonite (it is not hazardous at these quantities)

---

**STEP 3A — WEIGH THE BENTONITE**

1. Place medium mixing bowl on scale. Tare/zero.

2. Open the bentonite clay bag. The powder should be a grey-tan or off-white
   fine powder. It may have a very slight earthy smell. This is normal.

3. Slowly scoop bentonite into the bowl until scale reads 30.0g. Bentonite
   is heavier than fumed silica — it will not create a dust cloud if handled
   normally. No special precautions are needed beyond your gloves.

4. Close the bentonite bag.

---

**STEP 3B — DISPERSE THE BENTONITE INTO WATER**

1. Add 500mL of distilled water to the medium mixing bowl with the bentonite.
   Unlike fumed silica, bentonite will not float — it will immediately begin
   to sink and absorb water. This means you can add the water more directly.

2. Pour the 500mL of water into the bowl in two additions: pour 250mL,
   whisk for 1 minute, then pour the remaining 250mL and whisk again.

3. Whisk vigorously for 3 minutes after all water is added.

4. You will notice the mixture looks like light tan or grey-tan cloudy water
   immediately after mixing. It is not very thick at this point — that is normal.

5. Set the bowl aside and allow to rest undisturbed for 30-60 minutes.
   During this rest period, the bentonite clay particles fully hydrate
   and the clay platelets stack and network together. The mixture will
   become noticeably thicker during this rest period.

6. After 30-60 minutes, return to the bentonite. Stir it with a spoon.
   Observe the following: does it resist stirring slightly? When you
   stop stirring and pull the spoon out, does the mixture slowly recover
   and stop flowing? This is the thixotropic behavior you are looking for.
   
   To test specifically: stir the bentonite mixture quickly for 30 seconds,
   then remove the spoon and leave the bowl perfectly still. Set a timer.
   Watch the surface. If the surface stops moving and the mixture firms up
   within 2 minutes, the bentonite is working correctly.

**What you should have:**
A medium bowl containing approximately 530mL of grey-tan cloudy liquid
that has thickened to something between water and thin gravy. It stirs
relatively easily but does not flow freely like water — it has some body.
Most importantly, it thickens back up when left alone after stirring.

If it has not thickened at all after 60 minutes: add another 10g of bentonite,
stir thoroughly, wait another 30 minutes.

If it is very thick (paste-like, difficult to stir): add 50mL additional water.

**Record in your notes:**
- Bentonite mass used: ____g
- Water volume used: ____mL
- Resting time: ____min
- Thixotropy observed? (stirs, then firms up) Y / N
- Appearance: ____
- Any observations:

## PHASE 1 — STEP 4: COMPOUNDING — ASSEMBLING THE FINAL GEL

**What this step does:**
You are combining all three prepared components — the SAP gel, the fumed
silica suspension, and the bentonite dispersion — into the final EHAB Phase 1
base gel. The order of addition matters. The bentonite goes in before the
silica because the clay network needs to integrate with the SAP polymer
chains before the silica particles are added.

**You should currently have:**
- Bowl 1 (large): SAP gel (~1000g total mass)
- Bowl 2 (small): Fumed silica suspension (~225g total mass)
- Bowl 3 (medium): Bentonite dispersion (~530g total mass)

**Total combined mass will be approximately 1755g (~1.75 liters)**

---

**STEP 4A — ADD BENTONITE TO SAP**

1. Pour the bentonite dispersion (from Bowl 3) into the large SAP gel bowl
   (Bowl 1). Pour slowly, in a steady stream, while stirring the SAP gel
   with your other hand or with a whisk.

2. Stir continuously as you pour. The bentonite will begin to integrate with
   the SAP polymer network immediately.

3. Once all bentonite has been added, mix vigorously with your whisk for
   3 minutes. You are looking for the mixture to become uniform — no visible
   streaks of tan (bentonite) running through the clearer SAP gel.

4. After 3 minutes of whisking, the mixture should look uniformly slightly
   tan or grey-white. Thicker than the SAP gel alone. This is correct.

---

**STEP 4B — ADD FUMED SILICA SUSPENSION TO SAP+BENTONITE**

This is the most important mixing step. The silica must be added slowly
and in multiple portions to prevent localized clumping.

1. Divide your fumed silica suspension mentally into four equal portions
   (approximately 55mL each).

2. Pour the first portion (~55mL) of the silica suspension slowly into the
   SAP+bentonite bowl while whisking. Pour in a thin stream, moving around
   the bowl rather than pouring into one spot.

3. Whisk for 2 full minutes after the first addition. The mixture will
   become noticeably thicker and more opaque (more white/milky in color).

4. Pour the second portion, whisk 2 minutes. Pour the third, whisk 2 minutes.
   Pour the fourth, whisk 2 minutes.

5. After all silica has been added and after the final 2-minute whisk,
   perform one final extended mixing session: whisk vigorously for 5 minutes
   continuously without stopping. This is the most physically demanding part
   of the process. If you have an immersion blender, use it at medium speed
   for 3 minutes instead — it will produce a better result than hand whisking.

6. The bowl will now contain a visibly thicker, whiter, more substantial
   gel than any of the individual components. It should coat the whisk
   heavily and take several seconds to drip off when you lift the whisk out.

---

**STEP 4C — FINAL REST**

1. Set the large bowl aside undisturbed for 30 minutes.

2. During this rest, the three components fully integrate. The bentonite
   clay network, SAP polymer chains, and silica particles interact and
   form a unified composite gel structure.

3. Do not stir during the rest period. The network needs undisturbed time
   to establish.

---

**STEP 4D — FINAL INSPECTION BEFORE TESTING**

After the 30-minute rest, inspect your gel:

The gel should now exhibit all of the following characteristics:
- Color: White to milky grey-white. More opaque than any single component.
- Texture: Substantially thicker than the SAP gel alone. Like thick yogurt
  or thick glue. Holds peaks when scooped — a spoon dragged through the
  gel leaves a channel that stays open for several seconds before slowly
  closing.
- Flow: Pours very slowly. Definitely not water. Does not flow freely.
- Recovery: Stir vigorously for 30 seconds, leave alone for 1-2 minutes.
  The gel should firm up noticeably after stirring stops.

This last property — flowing when stirred, firming up when left alone —
is called thixotropy and it is the most important physical property of
the gel for its eventual application. It is what allows the gel to be
pumped through pipes (it flows under pump pressure) and then hold on a
vertical wall (it firms up when the pump pressure stops).

Write a detailed description of what your gel looks and behaves like at this
point. This is your baseline record. Every subsequent batch will be compared
to this description.

**Record in your notes:**

- Consistency description (in your own words): ____
- Does it hold a peak when scooped? Y / N
- Does it recover after stirring? Y / N (time to recovery: ____min)
- Total estimated volume of gel produced: ____mL

### Quality Check 1 — Vertical Adhesion Test

**What this checks:**
Will the gel stay on a vertical surface without sliding down?
This is the most fundamental requirement — a gel that runs off before the
fire reaches it is useless.

**Time required:** 5 minutes to set up, 30 minutes of waiting and photographing

**Materials:**
- Your gel from Step 4 (approximately 50g per panel test)
- Three flat vertical surfaces, each approximately 20-30cm tall:
  Panel A: A piece of plywood or any untreated wood (if you have it)
  Panel B: A painted surface — a piece of drywall, or tape a piece of
           cardboard to a wall and use the painted wall behind it
  Panel C: A piece of glass or glazed tile (if available) — optional,
           but glass allows you to see the film from behind
  
  If you only have one type of surface, use it for all three tests
  and note the surface type. Wood is the most important to test.

1. Lean or tape your test panels so they are vertical (90 degrees to the floor).

2. Mark a rectangle on each panel with your marker: approximately 10cm wide
   by 15cm tall. This is your target application zone. Marking it lets you
   see clearly how much the gel moves over time.

3. Using a spoon or spatula, apply gel within the marked rectangle to a
   thickness of approximately 3mm. What does 3mm look like? About the
   thickness of two stacked coins, or the thickness of two credit cards.
   Spread it as evenly as you can across the marked area.

4. The moment you finish applying the gel to a panel, photograph it from
   directly in front. This is your T=0 reference photograph.

5. Note the starting time. Take additional photographs at:
   - 5 minutes
   - 10 minutes
   - 15 minutes
   - 30 minutes

6. For a simple measurement at the 15-minute mark: place a piece of paper
   under the panel to catch any gel that has run off. After 30 minutes,
   weigh the paper with any gel on it (tare the scale with a clean identical
   piece of paper first). Calculate:
   
   Original gel applied (estimate): approximately 15-20g for a 10x15cm area
   at 3mm thickness
   
   Runoff weight: ____g
   
   Retention % = ((original - runoff) / original) × 100

**What to look for and what it means:**

As you observe the panels, you will see one of several behaviors:

GOOD BEHAVIOR: The gel sags very slightly at the bottom of the marked
rectangle (a slight bead forms at the lower edge) but does not drip or
slide. The bulk of the gel holds in place. The top portion of the rectangle
still has visible gel at 15 minutes.

MARGINAL BEHAVIOR: The gel slowly slides downward as a mass, leaving the
top of the rectangle exposed. Some gel accumulates at the bottom. The top
25-30% of the rectangle is bare at 15 minutes but most of the gel is still
on the panel.

FAILURE BEHAVIOR: The gel flows freely, dripping off the panel within
5 minutes. Large drops fall. The panel is mostly bare at 10 minutes.

**Pass threshold:** More than 80% of the gel remains on the panel at 15 minutes.

**What to do if it fails:** See Failure Mode 1 in the appendix at the end of
this document. The correction is to increase the bentonite clay in your
next batch. You do not need to discard the current gel batch — just make
note of the result and adjust for the next batch.

### Quality Check 2 — Shear-Thinning Confirmation

**What this checks:**
Does the gel actually exhibit the critical shear-thinning behavior —
flowing when stirred or squeezed, firming back up when left alone?

**Time required:** 5 minutes

1. Take a large spoonful of gel from your bowl. Place it on a flat surface.

2. Using your spatula, stir the spoonful of gel vigorously for 30 seconds.
   Observe: it should become noticeably more fluid during this stirring.
   It should move more easily, spread more easily. If you tilt the flat
   surface slightly, the gel may begin to flow downhill while being stirred.

3. Stop stirring. Leave the gel completely alone. Set a timer.

4. Observe over the next 2 minutes: the gel should gradually stop moving
   and begin to hold its shape again. If you tilted the surface while
   stirring, the gel should stop flowing and hold its new position when
   you stop stirring.

5. At the 2-minute mark, test the gel's firmness by gently prodding it
   with a spatula. Compare this firmness to how it felt while being stirred.
   It should be noticeably firmer now.

**What you should see:**
Under stirring: more liquid, flows more freely
At rest (2 minutes): firmer, holds position, does not flow on its own

This is the shear-thinning behavior confirming that your gel will flow
through pipes and nozzles when pressure is applied but hold on a surface
when that pressure stops.

### Quality Check 3 — Film Formation Check

**What this checks:**
When spread on a horizontal surface, does the gel form a continuous film
without immediately cracking or disintegrating? This confirms the gel can
coat surfaces as a coherent layer.

**Time required:** 5 minutes to set up, 30 minutes of observation

1. Pour approximately 50-60mL of gel onto a flat horizontal metal surface
   (one of your metal sample holders).

2. Spread the gel evenly across the metal surface using your spatula.
   Aim for approximately 2mm thickness — about the thickness of one credit
   card, or roughly half the thickness you applied in Check 1.

3. Leave undisturbed at room temperature for 30 minutes.

4. Photograph at T=0, T=10min, T=30min.

5. After 30 minutes, observe the film:
   - Is it still continuous (one connected layer)? Or has it cracked into

   - Can you slide the metal sheet without the film breaking off?

**What you should see:**
The gel film should remain as one continuous connected layer for at least
30 minutes at room temperature. Some slight darkening at the edges as
surface water evaporates is normal. Some very fine surface cracks after
30 minutes are acceptable. Complete fragmentation into islands within
10 minutes is a failure.

**Record:**

**You are now working with an open flame. Read this section completely

4. Wear your nitrile gloves. Your torch will be in one hand and a phone

5. Wear close-toed shoes. Keep feet away from the test area.
6. Your butane torch is fine for this test. A butane torch and a propane
   torch behave similarly at this scale.

### Test 1 — Fire Protection Duration (Torch Test)

**What this tests:**
How much longer does a gel-coated wood surface resist ignition compared
to bare wood? This is the core performance test for the entire system.

**Why wood?**
Wood is the primary combustible surface material in residential structures.
Plywood or dimensional lumber represents the fuel gap the gel is designed
to close. If the gel protects wood meaningfully, it closes that fuel gap.

**Materials:**
- 6 pieces of wood, each approximately 10cm × 10cm × 12mm thick
  (roughly 4" × 4" plywood squares cut or broken to size, or any untreated
  wood of similar dimensions)
  If you cannot cut wood precisely, use 6 wood pieces of the same type
  and approximate size. Uniformity matters for comparison.
- Your EHAB gel (from Steps 1-4)
- Butane torch
- Metal sample holders (to hold the wood)
- Stopwatch (phone)
- Camera (phone)
- Bucket of water

**Group the 6 wood pieces:**
Group A (Control — 3 pieces): No gel applied. These are your reference panels.
                               They tell you how fast bare wood ignites.
Group B (Coated — 3 pieces):  Gel applied to the top face at 3mm thickness.

---

**STEP 6A — PREPARE THE COATED PANELS**

1. Take your three Group B panels.

2. Using your spatula, apply gel to the top face of each panel to approximately
   3mm thickness. Spread it to cover the entire top surface, edge to edge.
   Do not apply gel to the sides or bottom — top face only.

3. Apply gel to all three Group B panels.

4. Allow the gel to sit on the panels undisturbed for 5 minutes before testing.
   This simulates the gel having had time to adhere. Do not test immediately
   after application — the gel needs a moment to settle.

5. During this 5 minutes, prepare your test area outdoors.

---

**STEP 6B — ESTABLISH YOUR TEST PARAMETERS**

Standardizing how you hold the torch matters for comparing coated vs.
uncoated results. You must hold the torch at the same distance and angle
for every test.

1. Choose your torch distance: **25mm from the wood surface** (approximately
   1 inch). This is a fairly intense heat flux — comparable to a well-developed
   fire. If your torch has a visible inner cone of flame, measure from the
   tip of that inner cone to the wood surface.

2. Choose your torch angle: directed straight down onto the horizontal surface.
   The wood panel lies flat, gel side facing up, torch aimed straight down.

3. Practice your grip and position with the torch OFF before lighting. You
   want your hand to be comfortable holding this position for up to 5 minutes
   without moving.

---

**STEP 6C — TESTING GROUP A (CONTROL PANELS — NO GEL)**

Testing the uncoated panels first establishes the baseline time-to-ignition
for bare wood with your specific torch at your specific distance.

1. Place an uncoated (Group A) panel flat on the metal sample holder.

2. Hold your torch at 25mm above the wood surface, unlit. Position your
   phone camera to record the test — you want video, not just photos,
   so you can review the timing later.

3. Start your phone timer simultaneously with lighting the torch.

4. Hold the torch steady at 25mm. Watch the wood surface carefully.

5. Record the time at which each of the following events occurs:
   
   EVENT A: First visible smoke rises from the wood surface.
   What to look for: A faint wisp of whitish or grey smoke coming off the
   wood where the flame contacts it. This is the wood beginning to pyrolyze
   (break down thermally). Write down the timestamp.
   
   EVENT B: First visible blackening/charring of the wood surface.
   What to look for: The wood surface changes color from tan/brown to black
   in the flame contact zone. Write down the timestamp.
   
   EVENT C: First ignition — the wood itself catches fire.
   What to look for: A flame appears on the WOOD SURFACE that is separate
   from the torch flame. The wood is now burning independently of the torch.
   This is the critical measurement. Write down the timestamp.
   
   EVENT D: Self-sustained burning test.
   At 2 minutes after EVENT C (or at the 5-minute mark if EVENT C has
   not occurred), briefly pull the torch away for 5 seconds.
   Observe: does the flame on the wood persist without the torch? If yes,
   the wood is independently burning. If the flame goes out, it was not
   self-sustaining yet.

6. Maximum test duration: 5 minutes per control panel.
   If the wood has not ignited in 5 minutes, record ">300 seconds" and move on.
   Extinguish any burning panel immediately by submerging in your water bucket.

7. After the panel cools (wait 5 minutes), measure the maximum char depth
   using your ruler. The char is the black, degraded wood. Measure the
   depth from the original surface to the deepest point of obvious charring.

8. Repeat for all three Group A panels. The average time-to-ignition across
   the three panels is your control baseline.

---

**STEP 6D — TESTING GROUP B (GEL-COATED PANELS)**

1. Place a gel-coated (Group B) panel, gel side up, flat on the metal holder.

2. Hold the torch at the SAME 25mm distance. Start video recording and timer.

3. Light the torch. Hold position fixed.

4. Observe and record the SAME EVENTS as in Group A testing:

   EVENT A (modified): First visible steam rising from the gel surface.
   What to look for: Unlike bare wood, the gel will not produce brown smoke.
   Instead, you will see steam (white, clear, quickly dissipating) rising
   from the gel surface where the flame contacts it. This is the water in the
   gel evaporating. This is the gel working. Record the timestamp.
   
   EVENT B (modified): First visible change in gel appearance.
   What to look for: The gel may begin to turn slightly yellow, brown, or
   grey in the flame contact zone. The gel may start to dry out (lose its
   shiny, wet appearance) in the direct flame zone. Record the timestamp.
   
   EVENT C: First visible charring or blackening at the GEL SURFACE.
   The gel should be protecting the wood surface below it, so charring
   in the direct flame zone may occur on the gel surface rather than the wood.
   Record when any black appears in the contact zone.
   
   EVENT D: First visible ignition — independent flame from wood surface.
   This is the key measurement. Record this timestamp if it occurs.
   
   EVENT E: New observation — aerogel formation.
   Watch carefully as the gel dries in the direct flame zone. After the gel
   appears to have lost much of its water (it will look drier, less shiny,
   may crack slightly), look for a WHITE RESIDUE remaining on the surface
   where the gel was. This white layer is the silica aerogel forming.
   It should appear as a fragile white coating. If you see this, record it
   with timestamp and describe what it looks like.

5. Perform the same self-sustain test (remove torch briefly at 2 min after
   any ignition, or at 5 minutes if no ignition).

6. Maximum test duration: 10 minutes per coated panel.
   If no ignition in 10 minutes, record ">600 seconds protection" — this
   is an excellent result.

7. After cooling, photograph the charring pattern on the coated panels and
   compare to the control panel charring pattern photographs.

---

**WHAT TO EXPECT FROM THE TORCH TESTS**

Here is what you are likely to see, described in sequence for a gel-coated panel:

Seconds 0-30: Steam rises vigorously from the gel surface where the torch
contacts it. The gel looks wet and shiny everywhere except in the direct flame
zone, where it begins to look slightly duller. The wood underneath is completely
protected. If you could see through the gel (you cannot), the wood is still
cool.

Seconds 30-90: The direct flame contact zone starts to dry out. You may see
the gel turn slightly more opaque or yellowish in that zone. Steam continues
from the drying zone. The surrounding gel is still wet and shiny.

Seconds 90-180 (1.5-3 minutes): The drying zone expands. In the core direct
flame zone, you may start to see the first hints of a white layer where the gel
has fully dried — this is the silica aerogel beginning to form. The surrounding
gel continues to provide protection.

Minutes 3-5: The white aerogel residue is more visible in the direct flame zone.
If the silica concentration is adequate, this layer provides continued protection
even after the water is gone. If it is not adequate, the protection decreases
significantly after water depletion.

Beyond 5 minutes: If you are still seeing protection beyond 5 minutes, the
aerogel transition is working. This is an excellent result. If the wood ignites
at around 3-4 minutes (after the gel dries in the contact zone), the silica
concentration may need to be increased in the next batch.

- Control ignites at 45 seconds, coated at 60 seconds → ratio = 1.3 → MARGINAL
- Control ignites at 45 seconds, coated at 50 seconds → ratio = 1.1 → FAIL

### Test 2 — Aerogel Transition Observation (Oven/Heat Test)

**What this tests:**
When the gel is dried by heat, does the silica form a visible solid residue
(the aerogel layer) on the surface? This tests the Phase C protection
mechanism — what happens after the water is gone.

**Materials:**
- 3 metal plates from your sample holders
- Gel: approximately 50g per plate
- Heat source: oven at 200°C, OR heat gun, OR torch at low setting at 15-20cm
  distance (not the close 25mm distance used in Test 1 — you want steady
  heat, not direct impingement flame)
- Kitchen scale
- Camera

1. Apply approximately 50g of gel to each of three metal plates. Spread
   to approximately 2mm thickness. Weigh each plate and record the
   plate-plus-gel weight.

2. Apply heat to each plate:
   - Oven method: place in oven at 200°C for 20 minutes
   - Torch method: hold torch at 15-20cm distance and slowly move it
     across the plate surface for 5-10 minutes until gel appears dry

3. Photograph the plates every 2 minutes.

4. When the gel appears fully dry (no more steam, gel is no longer shiny,
   appears to have hardened), remove from heat and allow to cool for
   5 minutes.

5. Weigh the cooled plate (plate weight alone, weighed before the test).
   The difference is the dried residue mass.

6. Using a dry fingertip (gloved), gently press on the dried residue.

**What to look for — the aerogel transition:**
If the aerogel transition has occurred, you will see a WHITE or OFF-WHITE
layer remaining on the metal plate. It will look light and fluffy — like
a very fine white powder that has compacted slightly, or like the residue
from burning a piece of paper but white instead of black. When you press
on it gently, it should have some structural integrity — it should not
immediately crumble to nothing but should feel slightly firm or powdery
while maintaining a connected structure.

If you carefully peel up a corner with a spatula, it may lift as a thin
white flexible or semi-rigid sheet. This is your aerogel.

**What NOT to look for:**
- A black layer: this is charred SAP polymer, not aerogel. It indicates
  the silica concentration was insufficient or the silica did not disperse.
- Nothing at all (bare metal plate): the silica was insufficient.
- A dense, glassy layer: this would indicate very high silica concentration
  and is not expected at Phase 1 concentrations.

         Description of residue: ____

Plate 3: [same fields]

  Clear aerogel formation: Y / N
  Residue coherent (holds together)? Y / N
  Residue resists being rubbed away? Y / N

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
# PHASE 2 — CO₂ FOAMING ACTIVATION LAYER
# Layer 4: Encapsulated Baking Soda + Citric Acid
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

**What Phase 2 adds to the gel:**
In Phase 1, the gel protects surfaces through three mechanisms: evaporative
cooling (water absorbs heat), oxygen barrier (the gel film excludes air from
the fuel surface), and aerogel formation (silica forms a persistent solid
layer after water depletes).

Phase 2 adds a fourth mechanism: when the gel reaches fire temperature
(approximately 80-100°C at the surface), wax-encapsulated baking soda and
citric acid capsules melt and release their contents. The baking soda and
citric acid react to produce CO₂ gas inside the gel film. This CO₂ does
three things simultaneously:
1. Expands the gel into a foam, tripling or more the effective film thickness
2. Further displaces oxygen at the fuel surface (CO₂ is heavier than air)
3. The reaction is endothermic (absorbs heat), slowing the temperature rise

The encapsulation in wax is critical. Without encapsulation, the baking soda
and citric acid would react immediately when mixed into the water-containing
gel — releasing all the CO₂ at room temperature during storage rather than
at the fire temperature during use. The wax shell holds the two reactants
inert until the temperature rises to the wax melting point, then breaks open
and allows the reaction to proceed.

**Prerequisites for Phase 2:**
Phase 1 MVP criteria must all be passed before beginning Phase 2.
You are adding a new layer to a validated baseline. Adding it to a
baseline that already has problems will make troubleshooting much harder.

**Total time for Phase 2:** 2-3 hours across 2 sessions
(encapsulation session + incorporation and testing session)

## PHASE 2 — STEP 7: UNENCAPSULATED FOAMING TEST (VALIDATION FIRST)

**What this step does:**
Before spending time on wax encapsulation, you confirm that the baking soda
and citric acid actually produce useful foaming behavior when combined in
your gel. This is a proof-of-concept step. The foaming will be uncontrolled
(it starts immediately because there is no wax shell), but you are just
checking that the reaction produces enough CO₂ to foam the gel.

**What you will need:**
- Phase 1 gel: 200g (from your validated Phase 1 batch)
- Baking soda: 4g (about 1 teaspoon — a heaping teaspoon)
- Citric acid: 3g (about a half-teaspoon)
- A medium pot filled with water on a stovetop
  (you will not boil it — just heat to approximately 80-100°C,
  which is just below boiling; you will see steam coming off the
  water but it will not be a vigorous boil)
- A heat-resistant glass jar or metal container that fits inside the pot
  (this is a makeshift double boiler setup — the container with gel sits
  inside the pot of hot water)
- Kitchen thermometer (if you have one — if not, "steaming but not boiling"
  is close enough to 80-90°C)
- Camera/phone

---

**STEP 7A — PREPARE THE TEST GEL**

1. Measure 200g of your Phase 1 gel into the heat-resistant glass jar
   or metal container.

2. Add 4g of baking soda to the gel. Stir to incorporate. Some immediate
   very slow fizzing may occur as the baking soda encounters the slightly
   acidic water environment of the gel — this is minimal and expected.

3. Add 3g of citric acid to the gel. Stir to incorporate.

4. You will see immediate significant fizzing and CO₂ production as the
   baking soda and citric acid combine in the wet gel. This is the reaction
   happening without a wax trigger — it will happen at room temperature
   because there is no encapsulation to delay it.
   
   OBSERVE: Watch the gel foam for 2-3 minutes. Note:
   - How much does the volume increase? (1.5x? 2x? 3x?)
   - Does the resulting foam have any structural integrity, or does it
     immediately collapse back to liquid?
   - Is the foam stable after the initial reaction? (Tilt the jar — does
     the foam hold its position or flow?)

5. After the initial foaming subsides (approximately 2-3 minutes), place
   the jar into the pot of hot water (pre-heated to 80-90°C).

   PURPOSE: At this point, most of the unencapsulated baking soda and
   citric acid have already reacted. The heat is testing whether there
   is any remaining reaction that is triggered by additional temperature.
   In the actual wax-encapsulated version, ALL the reaction will be
   heat-triggered, not room-temperature-triggered.

6. Observe for 5 minutes. Take photographs and video.

**What to look for:**
- After initial room-temperature reaction: the foam should have increased
  the volume of gel by 1.5-3x. The gel-foam should still adhere to the
  jar walls (you can tip the jar slightly and the foam should hold, not
  flow freely like water).
- After heat exposure: any remaining unreacted compounds will contribute
  additional foaming. Describe what you see.

**What this tells you:**
If significant foaming (>1.5x volume increase) is observed and the resulting
foam has structural integrity (holds its position when you tilt the jar), then
the foaming mechanism is valid and worth encapsulating.

If foaming is minimal (less than 1.2x volume increase) or the foam immediately
collapses to liquid: either the gel is too viscous for CO₂ to create stable
bubbles, or the 4g/3g dosage is insufficient. Try increasing to 6g baking soda
+ 4g citric acid in a fresh 200g test portion before proceeding to encapsulation.

Foam appearance: ____

## PHASE 2 — STEP 8: WAX MICROENCAPSULATION

**What this step does:**
You are encasing the baking soda and citric acid mixture inside small
paraffin wax shells. The wax shell holds the reactants completely separated
from the water in the gel, preventing any reaction at room temperature.
When the gel is exposed to fire and the surface temperature reaches the
wax melting point (~58-65°C), the wax shells melt, the reactants contact
the gel's water, and the CO₂ reaction begins — right at the fire boundary
where it is needed.

**What you will need:**
- Baking soda: 15g
- Citric acid: 12g
- Paraffin wax beads: 50g (from your 860g supply)
- Double boiler setup: a medium pot filled with water + a glass bowl or
  metal bowl that rests on top of the pot without touching the water.
  Alternatively, a microwave-safe bowl + microwave works for melting wax.
- Cold plate: a metal baking sheet or cookie sheet placed in the freezer
  for 30 minutes before this step
- Parchment paper or wax paper
- Two teaspoons or a dropper (for handling molten wax)
- Kitchen thermometer (recommended, not mandatory)
- Gloves (already on — molten wax is hot)

---

**STEP 8A — PREPARE YOUR WORKING AREA**

1. Place your metal baking sheet in the freezer 30 minutes before you begin.
   You need a cold surface for the wax drops to solidify quickly. The colder
   the better.

2. Cut a piece of parchment paper to fit your metal sheet. Set both aside.

3. Mix 15g of baking soda and 12g of citric acid in a small dry bowl.
   CRITICAL: Keep this mixture completely dry. Even a single drop of water
   will trigger the reaction immediately. Make sure your bowl is dry
   before adding the powders.
   
   Use a dry spoon to mix the two powders together until they appear
   uniformly combined (no separate white and fine-white regions visible —
   both are white, but mix until they are indistinguishable).

4. Set this mixed powder aside in a dry location. Do not cover it with
   anything wet.

---

**STEP 8B — MELT THE PARAFFIN WAX**

Option A (Double Boiler — recommended):
1. Fill a medium pot with 5cm (2 inches) of water. Heat on the stovetop
   to a gentle simmer (small bubbles, not a rolling boil).
2. Place a heat-resistant glass bowl or metal bowl on top of the pot so
   it rests above the water without touching it. The steam from the simmering
   water will gently heat the bowl.
3. Add 50g of paraffin wax beads to the upper bowl.
4. Wait 5-10 minutes, stirring occasionally with a spoon. The wax beads
   will slowly melt into a clear or slightly cloudy liquid.
5. When fully liquid with no solid beads visible, your wax is ready.
   The temperature should be approximately 70-80°C (around 158-176°F).
   If you have a kitchen thermometer, check it. Do not let it exceed 100°C.

Option B (Microwave):
1. Place 50g of wax beads in a microwave-safe glass bowl.
2. Microwave in 30-second intervals, stirring between each, until fully melted.
3. Be careful — glass gets hot. Use oven mitts.

**What molten paraffin looks like:**
A clear or slightly yellow, slightly oily-looking liquid. Similar in appearance
to cooking oil but with a waxy smell. It moves like thin oil when poured.
This is correct.

---

**STEP 8C — RETRIEVE THE COLD PLATE AND LINE IT**

1. Remove the metal baking sheet from the freezer. Work quickly — it will
   warm up.

2. Lay the parchment paper on the cold sheet.

3. Place the cold sheet, molten wax, and baking soda/citric acid powder
   within arm's reach of each other. You will move quickly between them.

---

**STEP 8D — MAKING THE CAPSULES**

This process is manual and will feel slightly awkward the first few times.
The technique is straightforward once you have done 2-3 capsules.

**The target capsule structure:**
Each capsule is a small disc of solidified wax with a mixture of baking
soda and citric acid powder sealed inside. It is like a tiny wax sandwich —
bottom layer of wax, powder filling, top layer of wax to seal it.

**Making each capsule — repeat this sequence 20-30 times:**

1. Using a teaspoon, scoop up a small amount of molten wax — approximately
   1mL. This is roughly a quarter-teaspoon.

2. Drop (or carefully spoon) this small amount of molten wax onto the cold
   parchment paper. The wax will spread slightly into a disc shape.

3. Wait 5-8 seconds. The outer edge of the wax disc will begin to solidify
   as it contacts the cold surface, but the center will still be slightly
   soft or even liquid. You want it in this in-between state — solid enough
   to hold a shape, but still pliable enough to seal.

   How to judge the state: the wax disc will change from completely shiny
   and liquid-looking to partly matte at the edges. When the edges have
   turned matte (solidified) but the center is still shiny, you are in the
   right window.

4. While the wax disc is in this semi-solid state, use a small spoon to
   place approximately 0.3g of the baking soda/citric acid mixture in the
   center of the wax disc. 0.3g is a tiny amount — a very small pinch.
   It should sit as a small mound in the center of the wax disc.

   Do not use too much powder. A tiny amount per capsule is the goal.
   Better to make more capsules with less powder each than fewer capsules
   with too much powder.

5. Immediately — within 30 seconds — drizzle another approximately 0.5mL
   of fresh molten wax over the top of the powder mound to seal it.
   The molten wax should flow over and around the powder, entombing it.

6. Allow to fully solidify on the cold plate. This takes 2-3 minutes.

7. Proceed to the next capsule while the first one solidifies.

8. Repeat until you have made 20-30 capsules.

9. Once all capsules are fully solidified (solid to the touch, no flexibility):
   For each capsule, perform an optional second coat:
   Pick up the capsule with a spoon, dip it briefly into the molten wax
   (just a 1-second dip), then return it to the cold parchment to resolidify.
   This seals any pinholes in the first coating.

---

**STEP 8E — TESTING YOUR CAPSULES BEFORE USE**

You must test that the capsules actually work before incorporating them
into the gel. Testing one capsule prevents wasting a whole gel batch on
defective capsules.

**Test A — Room Temperature Stability:**
1. Drop one capsule into a cup of room-temperature water.
2. Observe for 5 minutes.
3. No fizzing should occur. If there is immediate vigorous fizzing, the
   capsule has a hole or crack and powder is contacting the water.
   This capsule is defective — make new capsules with a thicker second coat.

**Test B — Heat Activation:**
1. Fill a mug with hot water from the tap, as hot as you can get it —
   approximately 60-70°C (140-158°F). If you cannot get hot enough from the
   tap, heat a small amount on the stove.
2. Drop one capsule into the hot water.
3. OBSERVE: Within 30-60 seconds of the capsule being in hot water, the wax
   should soften and eventually melt. As the wax melts, the powder inside
   contacts the water and reacts.
4. You should see fizzing/CO₂ bubbles rising from the capsule.
5. PASS: No fizzing in room-temperature water + fizzing in hot water.
6. FAIL A (no fizzing in hot water): The capsule is too thick. Shorten the
   melting time or use hotter water. If the capsule truly will not release,
   the wax melting point may be higher than expected — check the product
   specification.
7. FAIL B (fizzing in room-temperature water): The capsule has a hole.
   Remake with thicker wax coating.

## PHASE 2 — STEP 9: INCORPORATING CAPSULES INTO THE GEL

**What this step does:**
You are folding your validated wax capsules into a fresh batch of Phase 1
gel to create the complete Phase 2 gel. The capsules sit inertly in the gel
at room temperature, waiting for the heat trigger.

**Important: Make a fresh Phase 1 batch for Phase 2.**
Do not use old gel that has been sitting for days — the SAP absorbs water
over time and the gel properties may have shifted. Make a fresh batch using
Steps 1-4 with your current validated formulation.

**Materials:**
- Fresh Phase 1 gel: a full batch (~1750g from Steps 1-4)
- Your validated wax capsules: all 20-30 of them
- A large mixing bowl
- Your hands (gloved) for gentle folding
- Camera

---

**STEP 9A — CRUSH THE CAPSULES**

The capsules you made are 1-2cm in diameter — fairly large for a lab-scale
prototype. For the Phase 2 gel, you want them distributed throughout the gel
rather than sitting as large visible lumps.

1. Lay 15-20 of your capsules on the parchment paper.

2. Using your fingertips, gently press and squeeze each capsule to break it
   into 2-4 smaller pieces. You are not trying to break the capsule open —
   you are reducing its size. The wax will crack into fragments. Each fragment
   still has some powder inside, still partially sealed.

   Do this gently. Overly aggressive crushing will break the capsule completely
   and the powder will contact your gloves and any moisture. Work lightly.

3. Your goal: each original capsule becomes 3-4 pieces of roughly 5-10mm size.
   The pieces should not be powder — they should be distinct wax fragments.

---

**STEP 9B — FOLD CAPSULES INTO GEL**

1. Spread your fresh Phase 1 gel in the large bowl.

2. Distribute the capsule pieces across the surface of the gel.

3. Using a spatula, fold the gel over the capsules rather than stirring.
   
   FOLDING vs. STIRRING: Stirring means moving the spatula in circles.
   Folding means scooping from the bottom, bringing the gel up and over
   the top, like folding egg whites into cake batter. Folding is gentle
   and distributes the capsules without crushing them further.

4. Fold 10-12 times. Turn the bowl 90 degrees, fold 10-12 more times.
   Repeat until the capsule pieces are distributed throughout the gel
   without being concentrated in one area.

5. Check visually: are capsule pieces visible throughout the gel (not all
   in one spot)? Are there still wax fragments visible as discrete pieces
   (not dissolved into the gel)? 

   GOOD: Capsule pieces visible but distributed. Gel looks mostly like the
   Phase 1 gel with occasional small white wax fragments throughout.
   
   BAD A: All capsule pieces clumped in one spot. Fold more thoroughly.
   BAD B: No capsule pieces visible at all — the capsules either dissolved
   (they are not supposed to dissolve) or were over-crushed into undetectable
   small pieces. This would be unusual. If this occurs, the wax melted into
   the gel (ambient temperature too warm, or wax melting point too low).

6. Perform a quick room-temperature stability check: leave the Phase 2 gel
   undisturbed for 30 minutes. Do you see any bubbling or CO₂ production?
   Small isolated bubbles around a capsule piece may appear — this is
   a small crack in one capsule. A very small amount is acceptable (minor
   CO₂ at room temperature from a small number of imperfect capsules).
   Vigorous widespread bubbling is not acceptable — it means most capsules
   are compromised.

**Record:**
```
Capsule pieces distributed evenly? Y / N
Any capsule pieces dissolved? Y / N (if Y, note: ____) 
Room-temperature fizzing after 30 min? None / Minor (1-2 spots) / Significant
Notes: ____
```

### Phase 2 Test B — Heat Activation (The Core Phase 2 Test)

**What this tests:**
When the Phase 2 gel is exposed to heat in the fire range, do the capsules
melt, release their contents, and produce the CO₂ foam layer? This is the
direct test of whether Phase 2 works as designed.

**Materials:**
- Phase 2 gel: approximately 150g applied to a horizontal metal plate
- Heat source: torch at LOW, held at 15-20cm distance (not the close
  25mm distance of the fire protection test — you want gradual heating
  to observe the activation, not a direct impingement flame test)
- Camera set up to record video of the entire test

---

**STEP 10B-1 — SETUP**

1. Apply approximately 150g of Phase 2 gel to a horizontal metal plate.
   Spread to approximately 3mm thickness.

2. Set up your camera to record video of the plate from the side, so you
   can see the gel profile (height of the gel layer) clearly.

3. Begin recording.

---

**STEP 10B-2 — HEAT APPLICATION**

1. Light the butane torch.

2. Hold the torch at 15-20cm above the plate, aimed straight down.
   Move it slowly back and forth across the surface (like painting) rather
   than holding it fixed in one spot. You want the entire gel surface to
   heat gradually and evenly, not one spot to get very hot while the rest
   stays cool.

3. Observe the gel surface continuously.

**Sequence of what you should observe (this is what the successful test looks like):**

Minutes 0-1: Steam rises from the gel surface. The gel looks wet and shiny.
Nothing else changes. The capsules are still sealed — the wax has not reached
melting temperature yet.

Approximately 1-2 minutes in: The gel surface starts to lose its shiny wet
appearance as the surface water begins to evaporate. The gel may slightly
change color (slightly more opaque, slightly more matte). The capsule pieces
inside should still be visible as distinct fragments.

Approximately 2-3 minutes in (key observation window): As the gel temperature
rises toward the wax melting point, look carefully at the gel surface and
interior. You should begin to see isolated CO₂ bubbles forming around the
capsule pieces embedded in the gel. These will look like small bubbles rising
through the gel from within — not from the surface, but from the interior
where the capsules are.

This is the activation moment. The wax is melting. The powder is contacting
the water in the gel. The reaction is beginning.

Within 30-60 seconds of the first internal bubbles appearing: the gel should
begin to visibly expand. The gel layer should rise — the profile you can see
on your camera from the side should get taller. This is the CO₂ foam forming
in place, expanding the gel.

The expanded foam will look lighter, more airy, more textured on the surface —
like the gel has been whipped. It will look like a foam rather than a smooth gel.

If the activation goes well, the gel volume will increase by 1.5-3x from
its original thickness.

4. Continue gentle heating for 2-3 minutes after the foam has formed. Observe
   whether the foam maintains its structure (remains elevated) or collapses
   back to liquid level.

5. Turn off the torch. Continue recording for 2 minutes after torch off.
   Observe: does the foam hold its structure after the heat is removed?

---

**WHAT TO WATCH FOR AND WHAT IT MEANS:**

Vigorous foam formation (volume increase >1.5x, foam maintains structure):
The Phase 2 activation is working. Excellent result.

Minimal foam formation (bubbles visible but no significant volume change):
The capsule loading is insufficient, or the capsules are not releasing
efficiently. Increase capsule content in the next batch (add 5-10 more
capsules per 1750g of gel).

No foam formation at all, but internal bubbling visible:
The CO₂ is escaping the gel rather than being captured as foam. This means
the gel may be too thin at the surface, allowing CO₂ to escape instead of
forming stable bubbles. Increase gel thickness (apply 5mm instead of 3mm).

No foam and no bubbles:
The capsules did not activate. Possible causes:
- Torch distance too far (not enough heat reaching the gel)
- Wax melting point higher than expected
- Capsules still intact (use hot water test to confirm capsule activation temperature)

## APPENDIX: FAILURE MODE CORRECTIONS

### Failure Mode 1 — Gel Runs Off Vertical Surface

**You see this when:** Quality Check 1 shows <60% retention at 15 minutes.
Gel drips freely down the panel.

**Root cause:** Insufficient yield stress — the gel cannot hold itself against
gravity.

**Fix, in order:**
1. Add 10g more bentonite per batch. Retest adhesion after each addition.
   Maximum: add up to 50g additional (total 80g bentonite instead of 30g).
2. If still failing at maximum bentonite: increase SAP by 2g (total 12g).
3. If still failing: add 5g xanthan gum (baking aisle of grocery store)
   dissolved in 50mL warm water, then folded into the gel.

---

### Failure Mode 2 — Gel Too Thick to Deploy

**You see this when:** Gel Test 3 (deployability) fails — gel will not flow
through 5mm opening even under firm hand pressure.

**Fix:**
1. Add 100mL distilled water. Mix thoroughly. Test again.
2. Repeat until gel flows under firm hand pressure.
3. After dilution, retest Quality Check 1 (vertical adhesion) to confirm
   it still holds — dilution improves flow but may reduce adhesion.
   The goal is to find the point where it both flows AND holds.

---

### Failure Mode 3 — No Aerogel Transition

**You see this when:** Test 2 (aerogel observation) shows a bare metal plate
or only a black residue after drying. No white coherent layer.

**Fix:**
1. Double the silica: use 50g silica in the dispersion step instead of 25g.
2. Extend the silica dispersion mixing time to 10 minutes continuous.
3. If problem persists: order confirmed Aerosil 200 grade fumed silica from
   a lab supply. Some consumer-grade fumed silica has lower surface area and
   does not form aerogel structure as effectively.

---

### Failure Mode 4 — Gel Ignites During Torch Test

**You see this when:** During Test 1, the gel surface itself catches and
sustains fire.

**Most likely cause:** The gel was applied and allowed to dry out before testing.
A dried SAP film CAN burn.

**Fix:**
1. Always test gel within 2 minutes of application — never test dried gel.
2. Add 5% glycerol (food-grade, available at pharmacy) by weight to the gel.
   This slows water evaporation significantly without combusting.
3. If the SAP powder itself easily ignites in a match test: source a different
   grade of SAP (look specifically for sodium polyacrylate without fragrance
   or additive).

---

### Failure Mode 5 — Wax Capsules Fizz at Room Temperature

**You see this when:** Phase 2 Test A (cold stability) shows bubbling from
the gel within 24 hours at room temperature.

**Fix:**
1. Remake capsules with double wax coat (dip twice after initial solidification).
2. Check wax melting point — if below 55°C the wax may soften at warm ambient
   temperatures (especially in a warm room or summer). Source 60-65°C melt
   point paraffin.
3. Keep Phase 2 gel refrigerated until use.

---

### Failure Mode 6 — Minimal Protection Improvement in Phase 1

**You see this when:** Phase 1 Test 1 fire protection ratio is less than 1.5x.

**Diagnostic sequence:**
1. Was the gel freshly applied (within 2 minutes of test)? If not, apply fresh
   and retest.
2. Is the gel film 3mm thick on the test panel? If not, increase to 3mm.
3. Does Test 2 (aerogel) show aerogel formation? If not, increase silica first.
4. Try testing at longer torch distance (50mm instead of 25mm) — the 25mm
   distance produces heat flux similar to a fully developed fire, which
   exceeds the system's pre-flashover design scope. At 50mm distance,
   the heat flux is more representative of the early growth phase the
   system is designed for, and the protection ratio should be higher.

## WHAT YOUR FINISHED PHASE 2 PROTOTYPE IS

When Phase 1 and Phase 2 are both validated, you have in your hands:

A milky-white viscous gel containing:
- A superabsorbent polymer network that holds water and coats surfaces
- Nanoscale silica particles that transition to an aerogel barrier when heated
- Clay platelets that give the gel its shear-thinning behavior
- Wax-encapsulated CO₂-generating capsules that activate only under fire-range
  heat to expand the gel into a foam and displace oxygen at the fire interface

It is:
- Non-flammable (does not sustain independent combustion when freshly applied)
- Non-toxic (all components are food-grade or cosmetic-grade)
- Deployable (flows under hand pressure through a 5mm opening)
- Adhesive on vertical surfaces (holds >80% at 15 minutes)
- Protective (>2x improvement in time-to-ignition vs. bare wood)
- Thermally transitioning (forms aerogel residue after water depletion)
- Activation-capable (CO₂ foaming triggered by fire temperature)

This is the Encapsulating Hydrogel-Aerogel Barrier.
It was derived from the geometric observation that fire requires fuel and
oxygen at the same surface simultaneously, and that encapsulating surfaces
denies both simultaneously.

The prototype is not an optimized product. It is a validated proof
of concept at bench scale. Every subsequent iteration refines the
formulation parameters toward specific application targets using the
systematic variation protocol defined in the predecessor documents.
