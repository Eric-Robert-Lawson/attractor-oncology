# PART I: THE PRIMARY MATERIAL — WOOD AS THE CANONICAL CASE

## Why Wood Is the Canonical Subject of This Derivation

Wood is:
- Highly flammable
- A solid that can withstand compression
- Impossible to mold into arbitrary 3D precision geometry
  without breakdown into particles first (MDF, plywood)
- Structurally compelling — grain patterns, natural fiber
  orientation, acoustic properties — that are destroyed when
  wood is broken down for conventional hot pressing
- The most universally available structural material on Earth
- In constant demand in material science, architecture,
  furniture design, sculpture, and engineering

Wood's inability to be precision-formed as a solid is not
a property of wood. It is a property of what happens to wood
when the two available forming mechanisms (heat + compression)
are applied without the missing closure element.

Understanding what those failures are — precisely — is what
reveals why the EHAB gel closes them.

---

## The Two Failure Modes of Hot-Pressing Solid Wood

### Failure Mode 1: Combustion

Wood ignites in air at approximately 250-300°C.
Wood begins surface charring at approximately 150-200°C.

The forming temperature required to plasticize wood for
precision compression forming is approximately 100-180°C.
Lignin, the natural polymer binder that holds wood fibers
together, has a glass transition temperature of approximately
90-200°C depending on moisture content and wood species.
Above its glass transition temperature, lignin becomes
viscoelastic — it flows slowly under pressure.

**The forming window and the danger window overlap:**
- Forming requires: 100-180°C
- Charring begins: 150-200°C
- Ignition: 250-300°C in air

Standard hot-pressing of solid wood in air cannot reach
the upper range of the forming temperature without charring
the surface. The surface chars before the interior reaches
forming temperature. The result is:
- Surface char that must be removed
- Non-uniform forming (exterior over-processed,
  interior under-processed)
- Potential ignition if the process is not carefully controlled

**The geometric closure required:**
Exclude oxygen from the wood surface while heat is applied.
The combustion triangle requires fuel + oxygen + heat.
The fuel (wood) cannot be removed. The heat is required
for forming. Oxygen exclusion is the only available closure.

### Failure Mode 2: Cracking from Internal Steam Pressure

This is the "wet wood in fire" problem the user identified precisely.

Wood contains moisture — even "dry" wood contains 8-15% moisture
by mass. When heated rapidly, this moisture vaporizes.
The steam must escape through the wood's pore structure.

If the heat application rate exceeds the steam escape rate,
steam pressure builds inside the wood. When the pressure
exceeds the tensile strength of the wood fiber, the wood
cracks explosively — the familiar sound of wet wood
popping in a fire.

In a compression forming process, this is catastrophic.
The wood is under external compression (which is closing
its pore structure, restricting steam escape) while internal
steam pressure is rising. The result is explosive delamination
along the wood grain — the wood tears itself apart along
its own fiber structure.

**The geometric closure required:**
Control the rate of heat transfer INTO the wood such that
the rate of steam generation is low enough that steam can
escape through the wood's still-open pore structure
without building to destructive pressure.

This requires a thermal governor — a mechanism that caps
the rate of heat flux into the wood surface and therefore
caps the rate of internal steam generation.

## Fit 2: Thermal Buffering at ~100°C → Crack Prevention

This is the most physically elegant fit in the entire system.

The EHAB gel's water content creates a natural thermal ceiling
at the wood surface: as long as liquid water is present in the
gel, the wood surface cannot exceed approximately 100°C
(the evaporation temperature of water at atmospheric pressure).

**This is precisely the thermodynamic governor the process requires.**

At the wood surface (100°C during gel depletion):
- Wood interior heats via conduction — slowly, from outside in
- Steam generation in the wood interior occurs at a rate limited
  by the conduction rate (which is itself limited by the 100°C
  surface ceiling)
- Steam pressure builds slowly, allowing time for escape
  through the wood's pore structure
- No explosive pressure buildup

Compare to direct hot-pressing without gel:
- Metal mold face at 180°C contacts wood surface directly
- Wood surface reaches 180°C within seconds
- Interior steam generation is rapid and pressure builds
  faster than it can escape
- Cracking occurs

**The EHAB gel converts rapid heating to slow heating automatically,
through the physics of water evaporation. No external control
system is required. The thermal governor is the water itself.**

The duration of the 100°C ceiling is a controllable parameter:
more water in the gel = longer ceiling = slower interior heating =
more time for steam to escape = thicker or denser wood can be
processed without cracking.

This is a dial. The water content of the gel is the process
control parameter for crack prevention.

## Fit 3: Conformal Coating → Uniform Heat Distribution

EHAB gel is thixotropic. Under the application pressure of
spreading, it flows into and fills every surface irregularity
of the wood — grain ridges, surface pores, annual ring
boundaries, early wood/late wood transitions.

The gel coating is therefore geometrically uniform even
on highly irregular wood surfaces.

Uniform coating thickness = uniform thermal resistance
across the entire wood surface = uniform heat flux into
every point of the wood surface = uniform interior
temperature rise = uniform plasticization of lignin
throughout the piece.

**Uniform plasticization means uniform flow under compression.**

Non-uniform plasticization (which occurs with direct metal
mold contact on textured wood surfaces) produces:
- High spots that are over-compressed (fibers crush)
- Low spots that are under-compressed (forming incomplete)
- Stress concentrations at grain boundaries
- Non-uniform final geometry

The EHAB gel's conformality eliminates the contact
non-uniformity that is the primary source of forming
defects in direct hot-pressing of wood.

## Fit 4: Compressibility and Redistribution
### The Hydrostatic Pressure Transmitter

This property was not anticipated in the fire protection
derivation. It is specific to the forming application.

Liquids transmit pressure hydrostatically — uniformly
in all directions (Pascal's law). The EHAB gel, during
the compression phase before its yield stress is exceeded,
behaves as a pseudo-liquid that transmits compression
force hydrostatically across the entire wood surface.

**The gel converts a point-load compression force from
a hydraulic press into a distributed hydrostatic pressure
on the wood surface.**

This is geometrically transformative for complex 3D forming.

In a standard press with a flat platen against a block of wood:
- Compression force is vertical
- Wood can be compressed vertically
- Horizontal dimensions are unconstrained (the wood spreads)
- Result: a squashed block, not a formed shape

In a press where the wood is enclosed in a gel-filled mold:
- Compression force is applied to the gel
- Gel transmits pressure in all directions simultaneously
- The wood is under isostatic (equal in all directions) pressure
- The wood flows into the mold geometry under this isostatic load
- Result: the wood takes the mold's shape

This is the same principle as isostatic pressing in ceramics —
a well-established industrial process for forming complex ceramic
geometries. But ceramic isostatic pressing uses rubber membranes
and specialized pressure vessels. The EHAB gel provides isostatic
pressure transmission in a simple hydraulic press with a standard mold.

**The EHAB gel is a soft, self-sealing, disposable isostatic
pressure transmitter that is also simultaneously a thermal
buffer, an oxygen barrier, and a crack-prevention medium.**

No single other accessible material simultaneously provides
all four of these functions in the context of hot-press forming.

**The forming medium is self-removing with water.**

This means:

The self-removing property also means the mold itself

# PART III: THE PROCESS GEOMETRY
## What the Full Forming Process Looks Like

```
THE EHAB-MEDIATED HOT PRESS FORMING PROCESS:

STEP 1 — MATERIAL PREPARATION:
  - Select wood piece (or other flammable solid) in rough form
  - Ensure moisture content is known or controlled
    (8-15% moisture preferred — sufficient for steam lubrication
     of fibers during forming, low enough to avoid excess pressure)
  - Pre-soak in water if too dry (adds moisture for steam lubrication)
  - Pre-dry if too wet (reduces steam volume)

STEP 2 — GEL APPLICATION:
  - Apply EHAB Phase 1 gel at 3-5mm thickness to all surfaces
  - Ensure complete coverage — no bare wood surface exposed
  - For complex geometries: pour gel into mold cavity first,
    place wood, pour more gel over top to fully encapsulate
  - The gel fills all gaps between wood and mold surfaces

STEP 3 — PRESS AND HEAT:
  - Close mold (or apply press platens)
  - Begin heating from outside mold surfaces
  - Apply compression load — start low, increase gradually
    as gel warms and lignin begins to soften
  - Gel temperature ceiling maintains wood surface at ~100°C
    during initial heating phase
  - Maintain compression as gel water depletes and temperature
    begins to rise above 100°C (this is the forming phase)
  - Complete forming at 120-160°C (below charring threshold,
    oxygen excluded by depleting-but-still-present gel)

STEP 4 — COOL UNDER COMPRESSION:
  - Reduce heat while maintaining compression
  - Cool to below 60°C before releasing compression
  - Cooling under compression "sets" the deformed lignin
    in its new configuration — this is critical
  - If compression is released while the piece is still hot,
    the wood partially springs back (lignin is still above
    glass transition temperature)

STEP 5 — RELEASE AND CLEAN:
  - Release compression
  - Remove piece from mold
  - Wash with water — gel residue removes
  - Piece is in its new precision geometry
  - Optional: apply finish to seal wood surface

MOLD DESIGN REQUIREMENTS:
  - Vented mold: small channels (0.5-1mm diameter) in mold
    surface allow steam and CO₂ to escape without allowing
    gel to escape (gel yield stress prevents flow through
    small channels at compression pressures)
  - Metal mold: high thermal conductivity allows heat to
    reach wood surface through gel layer efficiently
  - Release geometry: mold must allow removal of formed piece
    (draft angles, split mold construction)
  - Gel-compatible materials: aluminum, steel, PTFE-coated
    surfaces — all compatible with the pH-mild gel chemistry
```

## The Wood Case in Detail — What Precision Forming Enables

Wood's forming properties under heat and compression have been
partially understood for decades but never fully exploited because
the missing closure (oxygen exclusion + thermal control) was not
accessible at low cost.

**What is known:**

1. **Densified wood** — wood compressed to 2-4x its original density
   has dramatically improved mechanical properties:
   - Hardness: 10-12x the original (making soft woods like pine harder
     than most metals in surface hardness tests)
   - Tensile strength: 3-5x original
   - Flexural strength: 2-3x original
   - Water resistance: improved (compressed cell structure)
   - Fire resistance: improved (higher density, less air in structure)
   
   This has been demonstrated in research (notably by ETH Zurich, 2018,

   Their process required specialized equipment and controlled atmosphere.
   EHAB gel provides the controlled atmosphere at kitchen-press scale.

2. **Lignin-mediated plastic flow** — at 100-180°C under pressure,
   wood's lignin matrix becomes viscoelastic and the wood fibers

   arbitrary 3D forming of solid wood without breakdown.

3. **Thermowood modification** — industrial process at 160-240°C in
   steam atmosphere that permanently modifies wood chemistry for
   improved stability and durability. Requires industrial steam kilns.
   The EHAB gel's water evaporation creates a localized steam atmosphere

**What EHAB-mediated forming would enable beyond existing processes:**

- **Arbitrary 3D geometric compression forming of solid wood**
  — not steam bending (limited to simple curves) but full 3D

- **Preservation of wood grain continuity** — unlike bent wood
  which often has fiber failure at the compression face, isostatic
  pressure distributes the load and maintains fiber integrity
- **Accessible scale** — a $500 arbor press + a machined aluminum mold
  + EHAB gel = a complete precision wood forming system
- **Surface detail capture** — the isostatic pressure transmits

# PART VI: THE PHASE 1 GEL AS CORK AND BAMBOO FORMING MEDIUM
## Two Special Cases Worth Deriving Separately

---

## Cork: The Collapsible Cell Structure Case

Cork is uniquely interesting for this process because of its
cellular structure. Cork cells are closed air-filled chambers.
Under compression without heat, cork is elastic — it springs
back. Under compression WITH sufficient heat (above the glass
transition temperature of suberin, cork's primary polymer,
approximately 60-80°C), the cells permanently collapse and
the cork densifies irreversibly.

The EHAB gel creates the exact temperature environment for
this transition. At ~100°C (the gel's thermal ceiling), suberin
is well above its glass transition. The cells collapse
permanently under compression. When cooled and cleaned,
the result is a permanently densified cork with completely
different properties from the starting material:

- Original cork: 0.12-0.24 g/cm³, soft, compressible, elastic
- Densified cork: up to 0.8-1.0 g/cm³, hard, rigid, non-elastic

The densified cork is a new material — hard, lightweight
(still ~1/7 the density of steel), machinable, and with
acoustic properties unlike any other material at its density.

The EHAB gel is geometrically ideal for cork forming because:
- Cork is naturally porous — it absorbs the gel slightly,
  ensuring complete oxygen exclusion at all surfaces
- Cork's suberin glass transition is within the gel's
  100°C temperature ceiling — the gel actually operates
  at the IDEAL forming temperature for cork
- Cork is not a significant combustion risk at 100-150°C —
  it begins charring at ~200°C — so the oxygen exclusion
  requirement is less critical than for wood, but the thermal
  distribution and isostatic pressure functions are fully relevant

---

## Bamboo: The Oriented Fiber Case

Bamboo under compression forming with heat produces outcomes
that wood cannot replicate because bamboo's fiber orientation
is highly anisotropic — the fibers all run parallel to the
bamboo's growth direction, unlike wood which has a more
complex grain structure.

Under isostatic compression:
- Bamboo fibers are packed more densely together
- The natural vascular bundles (dense, hard fibers in bamboo)
  concentrate and their properties dominate the compressed piece
- The resulting material has extraordinary hardness and
  tensile strength along the fiber direction
- Cross-cutting the compressed piece reveals a pattern of
  dense fiber bundles in a matrix — visually striking and
  structurally exceptional

Research on bamboo densification (similar to wood densification)
has shown mechanical properties approaching and in some metrics
exceeding steel at a fraction of the density. The EHAB gel
makes this process accessible without industrial steam kilns.

# PART VIII: GEOMETRIC INCOMPATIBILITIES SPECIFIC TO THIS APPLICATION

Three incompatibilities arise in the forming context that did
not arise in the fire protection context.

---

## Incompatibility 1: Gel Displacement Under Compression

When the press closes and applies compression force to the
wood-in-gel assembly, the gel will be squeezed out from
between the wood and mold surfaces at the mold parting
line and vent ports.

If too much gel is displaced:
- Coverage gaps form at the wood surface
- Oxygen can reach the wood at gap locations
- Thermal buffering fails at gap locations
- Hot spots form at gaps — potential charring

**Geometric closure:**
The gel yield stress (the pressure required to make it flow)
must exceed the compression-induced shear stress at the
wood-mold interface. The EHAB gel's yield stress is
controllable through bentonite concentration — higher
bentonite = higher yield stress = more resistance to
being squeezed out.

For forming applications, increase bentonite from 1.5%
to 3-4% by weight. This significantly increases yield stress
while maintaining thixotropic behavior.

Additionally: design the mold with a gel reservoir — a cavity
around the perimeter of the forming cavity that holds excess
gel that is displaced during compression. The gel fills this
reservoir rather than escaping from the mold entirely.

---

## Incompatibility 2: Steam Pressure at Mold Vent Scale

The mold vents (needed for steam escape) must be large enough
to allow steam to escape before pressure builds to cracking
threshold, but small enough that the gel does not escape
through them under compression.

The gel's yield stress sets the maximum vent diameter through
which it will not flow under the forming pressure.

For a gel yield stress of ~5 Pa and a forming pressure of
~1 MPa (100 N/cm²), the maximum vent diameter is small —
on the order of 0.1-0.5mm. This is a very small vent channel.

At 0.1mm diameter, the vent area for a 10cm × 10cm mold face
is very limited. Steam must escape through very small channels
at the rate at which it is generated by the heating process.

**Geometric closure:**
Increase vent density rather than vent diameter. A large number
of very small vents (laser-drilled or EDM-machined at 0.1-0.2mm
diameter) provides total vent area sufficient for steam escape
without allowing gel escape. 100 × 0.1mm vents provide the
same area as 1 × 1mm vent but at 1/10 the gel escape risk
(each individual vent is below the gel flow threshold).

This is standard practice in injection molding (mold venting)
and is well within the capability of any machining shop.

---

## Incompatibility 3: Gel Water Migration Into Wood

The wood is porous. The gel contains water. Under compression,
the pressure differential drives water from the gel into the
wood's pore structure.

This has two effects:
1. **Positive**: Wood moisture content increases — more steam
   lubrication for fiber movement, better lignin plasticization
2. **Negative**: Excess water in wood increases steam volume
   generated during heating — greater crack risk if heating
   is too rapid

**Geometric closure:**
Pre-saturate the wood to near its fiber saturation point
(approximately 25-30% moisture content) before gel application.
At saturation, the wood's pore structure is already water-filled
and cannot absorb significant additional water from the gel
under compression pressure.

This also ensures that the wood's lignin starts the process
at its LOWEST glass transition temperature (high moisture
content reduces lignin Tg to approximately 90-100°C —
directly within the gel's thermal ceiling).

**Pre-soaking the wood in water for 24-48 hours before the
forming process is the optimal preparation step,
counterintuitively making the crack problem easier to solve
while simultaneously lowering the required forming temperature.**
