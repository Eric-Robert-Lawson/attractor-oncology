## 1.1 — Evaporation from Open Water Reservoirs

```
THE BASELINE PROBLEM:
  Open water reservoirs lose enormous quantities of water to
  evaporation. This is not a marginal effect — it is one of
  the largest sources of water loss in arid and semi-arid regions.

EVAPORATION RATES — MEASURED DATA:
  Lake Mead (Colorado River, USA/Nevada/Arizona):
    Annual evaporation: approximately 800,000 acre-feet per year
    That is 986 BILLION liters lost annually to evaporation alone
    From a single reservoir serving 25 million people

  Lake Powell (Utah/Arizona):
    Annual evaporation: approximately 500,000 acre-feet per year
    = 617 billion liters per year

  Hoover Dam reservoir average evaporation rate:
    approximately 1.8-2.4 meters per year of surface depth

  GLOBAL CONTEXT:
    Globally, open reservoir evaporation losses are estimated at
    10-30% of stored water volume annually in arid climates.
    In extreme arid regions (Middle East, North Africa, American
    Southwest): losses reach 30-50% annually.

THE MATHEMATICAL STATEMENT OF THE PROBLEM:
  Let V = reservoir volume (liters)
  Let E = annual evaporation rate (fraction of surface volume)
  Let A = surface area (m²)
  Let e = evaporation depth per year (meters/year)
  
  Annual evaporation loss = A × e × 1000 liters/m³
  
  For a reservoir with 10km² surface area in an arid region:
    A = 10,000,000 m²
    e = 2.0 m/year (typical American Southwest)
    Annual loss = 10,000,000 × 2.0 × 1000 = 20 BILLION liters/year
    
  If water costs $0.001/liter (municipal bulk rate):
    Annual evaporation cost = $20,000,000/year
    From a single mid-sized reservoir.
```

## 1.2 — Evaporation Reduction: SAP Alone vs P1S

```
EVAPORATION FROM SAP GEL SURFACE:
  As derived in previous documents, the vapor pressure above
  an SAP gel surface is approximately 80-95% of free water
  vapor pressure at the same temperature.
  
  EVAPORATION REDUCTION FROM SAP ALONE:
    5-20% reduction in evaporation rate vs. open water
    
  THIS IS MODEST. The reason:
    The SAP gel surface is still a water-rich surface.
    The polymer matrix reduces vapor pressure somewhat, but
    water molecules at the gel surface still evaporate readily.
    The gel is ~95% water by mass — it behaves almost like water.

WHAT CHANGES EVAPORATION MORE DRAMATICALLY:
  The key variable in evaporation is not just vapor pressure —
  it is whether there is a PHYSICAL BARRIER between the water
  and the atmosphere.
  
  EVAPORATION REDUCTION HIERARCHY:
  Open water:                         100% (baseline)
  SAP gel, no cover:                  80-95% (5-20% reduction)
  SAP gel + partial silica crust:     60-80% (20-40% reduction)
  P1S gel (with silica component):    65-85% (15-35% reduction)
  P1S gel + partial aerogel skin:     40-60% (40-60% reduction)
  Sand cover on water:                30-50% (50-70% reduction)
  Hexadecanol monolayer (chemical):   5-20%  (80-95% reduction)
  Shade cloth over reservoir:         40-70% (30-60% reduction)
  Rigid cover (polycarbonate panels): 2-5%   (95-98% reduction)

WHERE P1S GENUINELY DIFFERS FROM SAP ALONE:
  The FUMED SILICA component of P1S creates a SURFACE SKIN
  as the gel dehydrates. This silica skin is the same aerogel
  transition observed in fire testing.
  
  At large scale, exposed to sun and wind, P1S gel will
  develop a persistent silica-clay surface crust that:
  1. Physically separates the bulk water in the gel from
     the atmosphere
  2. Reduces convective water vapor transport from gel to
     air (the crust is a diffusion barrier)
  3. Self-heals when the crust is disturbed (re-wets and
     re-forms as the gel is mixed)
  
  SAP GEL WITHOUT SILICA: no crust formation.
  The SAP gel remains an open water surface permanently.
  
  P1S GEL: forms a silica-clay crust on the exposed surface
  that reduces evaporation beyond what SAP alone achieves.
  
  QUANTIFIED DIFFERENCE (estimated, requires field testing):
    SAP alone:  5-20% evaporation reduction
    P1S:        25-45% evaporation reduction (from crust formation)
    
  The silica crust is the mechanism that separates P1S from
  SAP in the water preservation application.
  This is the single most important physical distinction
  for the water management argument.
```

## 1.3 — The Layered Architecture for Maximum Evaporation Reduction

```
THE OPTIMAL P1S WATER PRESERVATION ARCHITECTURE:

  Rather than replacing reservoir water with P1S entirely,
  the optimal architecture applies P1S as a SURFACE LAYER
  on top of conventional stored water.
  
  LAYER ARCHITECTURE (from top to bottom):
  
  LAYER 1 — P1S GEL SURFACE LAYER: 50-100mm depth
    Applied on top of the water body.
    P1S gel floats (density slightly below or at water density
    depending on exact formulation — silica and bentonite
    slightly increase density, SAP network decreases effective
    density through entrained air pockets in the polymer matrix).
    
    The silica-clay surface crust forms on the top of this layer.
    
    This layer is the primary evaporation reduction barrier.
  
  LAYER 2 — TRANSITION ZONE: gel → water interface
    The P1S gel below the silica crust gradually transitions
    to conventional stored water. This interface is where
    the gel provides its water to the reservoir and where
    the reservoir water is continuously available.
  
  LAYER 3 — STORED WATER BODY: conventional reservoir depth
    The bulk water reservoir below the gel layer.
    Unmodified — no cost to convert bulk volume.
    Protected by the gel surface layer above.
  
  ADVANTAGE OF LAYER ARCHITECTURE:
    You only need to convert the SURFACE VOLUME to P1S —
    not the entire reservoir.
    
    For a reservoir with 10km² surface area and 20m depth:
    Total volume: 200 billion liters
    P1S layer (100mm depth): 1 billion liters — only 0.5% of total
    
    The P1S layer protects 100% of the surface from evaporation
    at a cost of only 0.5% of total reservoir volume converted to gel.
    
    This is the key economic efficiency of the layer architecture.
```

# PART VI: THE CRITICAL UNKNOWNS
## What Must Be Tested Before Governmental Adoption

```
UNKNOWN 1 — AQUATIC ECOSYSTEM IMPACT (HIGH PRIORITY):
  What happens to fish, invertebrates, and aquatic plants
  under a P1S gel layer?
  
  Light penetration: P1S gel is opaque (milky white).
  A 75mm gel layer SIGNIFICANTLY reduces light reaching
  the water below. This will impact photosynthesis in
  the water column.
  
  For DEDICATED WATER SUPPLY RESERVOIRS (no fish):
    Light reduction is not a problem.
  For MULTI-USE RESERVOIRS with fish populations:
    Significant concern. Could cause fish kills if
    phytoplankton base collapses from light deprivation.
    
  MITIGATION: Design gel layer with gaps (not 100% coverage)
  to allow light penetration zones. 70% gel coverage may
  provide nearly equivalent evaporation reduction while
  preserving ecosystem function.
  
  This must be the primary question of the pilot study.

UNKNOWN 2 — GEL STABILITY OVER TIME (MEDIUM PRIORITY):
  How does P1S gel behave after weeks and months on
  open water?
  
  Possible degradation modes:
  - UV photodegradation of SAP polymer (SAP is not UV-stable
    long-term — yellowing, chain scission)
  - Microbial growth in the gel (biofilm development)
  - SAP hydrolysis (in alkaline or acid conditions over months)
  - Silica crust fragmentation by wind/wave action
  
  These must be characterized in a 6-12 month pilot study.
  Likely mitigation: UV stabilizers added to formulation,
  periodic biocide treatment of gel surface, annual partial
  replacement of degraded gel.

UNKNOWN 3 — WILDFIRE WATER SOURCING COMPATIBILITY
  (MEDIUM PRIORITY):
  If tanker aircraft draw P1S gel from the reservoir surface,
  what happens to the pump and aircraft systems?
  
  SAP gel can clog filtration systems designed for water.
  Aircraft water scooping systems (CL-415 "Super Scooper"
  type aircraft) are designed for liquid water, not gel.
  
  MITIGATION OPTIONS:
  - Gel diluted to sprayable concentration in the aircraft tank
    by mixing 1:10 with water before drop
  - Purpose-built gel-compatible tanker systems (new design)
  - Dedicated gel draw points with mixing nozzles
  
  This is a real engineering challenge that must be solved
  before fire suppression integration is viable.

UNKNOWN 4 — WAVE AND WIND RESISTANCE OF GEL LAYER:
  On large open reservoir surfaces, wind generates waves.
  The gel layer must withstand wave action without fragmenting
  into pieces that sink and lose the evaporation reduction benefit.
  
  The thixotropic nature of P1S gel means it can deform and
  recover — but large wave action (on a 5km+ wide reservoir)
  may exceed the gel's recovery capacity.
  
  Mitigation: floating barrier rings to break wave energy at
  the gel layer boundary (like existing reservoir wave barriers).
  This adds cost but solves the wave action problem.

UNKNOWN 5 — COST REDUCTION THROUGH SCALE MANUFACTURING:
  The government procurement cost of $0.036-0.068/liter
  is estimated from industrial ingredient pricing.
  At true governmental scale (millions of tonnes of SAP),

    SAP contribution per liter of P1S: $0.005
    Total P1S cost: $0.010-0.020/liter
    
    This fundamentally changes the economics — the entire
    calculation above improves by 2-3x on cost.
    At $0.015/liter P1S, break-even in drought scenarios
    falls to under 6 months.
```
