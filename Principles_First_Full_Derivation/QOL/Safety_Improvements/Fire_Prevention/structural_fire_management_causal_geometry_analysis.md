## PART I: FRAMING — THE INVARIANT APPLIED TO FIRE

### The Triadic Invariant Restated

```
STRUCTURE × GAP × NAVIGATOR → RESOLUTION
```

The application to fire is not metaphorical. It is a direct structural mapping.

Fire is a **system process** — a thermochemical reaction that navigates an available
landscape toward its own systemic completion. It does not intend. It navigates.
The landscape it navigates is the built structure, the chemistry of materials,
the geometry of air supply, and the thermal history of the environment.

The act of fire management is **landscape authorship** — the deliberate modification
of the causal landscape through which fire navigates, in order to alter the outcome.

### The Triadic Mapping — Fire as Navigator

| Triadic Element | General Definition | Fire System Instantiation |
|---|---|---|
| **STRUCTURE (S)** | The encoded landscape. Prior geometry. Exists before the navigator arrives. | The built environment: materials, geometry, topology, thermal mass, fuel load, air pathways, compartment boundaries |
| **GAP (G)** | The underdetermined space between structural encoding and navigator output. Where the landscape is traversable. | Physical openings, penetrations, voids, cavities, thermal bridges, combustible surfaces — every space a fire can move through or across that is not structurally closed against it |
| **NAVIGATOR (N)** | The self-referential agent that traverses the landscape from gap to gap. | The fire process itself: the coupled system of combustion, convective thermal plume, radiant heat front, and smoke propagation |
| **RESOLUTION** | The systemic outcome when navigation completes or terminates. | Full structural involvement (unmanaged), compartment containment (managed), or self-extinguishment (optimally managed) |

### The Two Phases of Fire Management — Restated Geometrically

**Pre-Positioned Fire Management:**
The authored modification of the structure (S) before any fire event occurs,
such that the gaps (G) available to the fire navigator (N) are:
1. Reduced in number
2. Reduced in conductivity (how easily fire crosses them)
3. Geometrically incompatible with fire propagation beyond the designed boundary

**Active Response Fire Management:**
The real-time modification of the gap landscape during fire navigation.
Structurally constrained by whatever pre-positioned landscape was authored.
Cannot exceed the ceiling set by pre-positioned management.

**Core Geometric Consequence:**
Active response is bounded above by the pre-positioned landscape.
Every gap that was not closed before the fire began is a structural degree of freedom
that no active response can eliminate after ignition — it can only manage.
The focus of high-leverage intervention is therefore always pre-positioned.

## PART II: LITERATURE SWEEP — THE STATE OF THE FIELD

### 2.1 The Established Framework: Passive Fire Protection (PFP)

The field calls the pre-positioned structural layer **Passive Fire Protection (PFP)**.
It is contrasted with **Active Fire Protection (AFP)** — sprinklers, suppression systems,
alarms — which require a triggering event (the fire) to operate.

PFP is defined by the Firestop Contractors International Association (FCIA, 2025) as
the ensemble of measures designed to contain fire and smoke within their compartment of
origin through structural means requiring no human or mechanical activation.

**Primary PFP instruments currently in practice:**

1. **Compartmentalization** — Division of the structure into rated fire compartments
   using fire-resistant walls, floors, and ceilings. Each compartment is a bounded
   sub-landscape within which fire can navigate but beyond which it cannot cross
   without encountering a rated barrier.

2. **Fire-Rated Barriers and Assemblies** — Walls, floors, and ceilings tested and
   rated to a specific duration of fire resistance (30 min, 60 min, 90 min, 120 min,
   240 min under standard ASTM E119 / ISO 834 time-temperature curve exposure).

3. **Penetration Firestopping** — Sealing of every opening created by building services
   (pipes, conduits, cables, ducts) through rated barriers. The 2024 ICC Firestopping
   Inspection Manual codifies standard approaches. Key materials:
   - Intumescent sealants, collars, and wraps (expand when heated to close the gap)
   - Fire-rated mortar and putty systems
   - Fire-rated joint systems for movement joints and perimeter gaps

4. **Fire Doors and Dampers** — Self-closing, rated door assemblies that restore
   compartment integrity when a gap (door opening) is triggered by fire detection or
   fusible link activation.

5. **Cavity Barriers** — Horizontal and vertical barriers installed within concealed
   void spaces (behind cladding, above ceilings, below raised floors) to prevent fire
   from using these voids as highways.

6. **Surface Finish Control** — Use of non-combustible or low-spread-of-flame rated
   interior finishes to reduce the surface fuel load available to the navigator.

### 2.2 The Known Failure Modes — Where Gaps Persist

The literature identifies a consistent taxonomy of PFP failures. These are structurally
significant because they represent gaps that the field has identified but not yet
fully eliminated through current methods:

**Category A — Design Gaps (Present at Construction)**
- Unsealed or improperly rated service penetrations
- Inadequate head-of-wall firestopping at perimeter joints
- Unprotected facade cavities (post-Grenfell Tower, 2017)
- Missing or undersized cavity barriers in exterior wall assemblies
- Open-plan floor layouts creating unrestricted horizontal spread potential

**Category B — Lifecycle Gaps (Introduced After Construction)**
- Post-construction modifications that breach rated barriers
- Degradation of firestopping materials over time (especially those sensitive
  to moisture, UV, or structural movement)
- Fire door propped open, damaged, or improperly maintained
- HVAC duct penetrations not maintained to rated standard after equipment changes

**Category C — Systemic Design Gaps (Topology-Level)**
- Vertical shafts (elevator, stairwell, service) acting as chimney channels
- Stairwells not positively pressurized, enabling smoke spread to all floors
- Atrium designs creating multi-floor void spaces
- Curtain wall systems without floor-level fire barriers (spandrel protection)
- Uninterrupted facade cavities from grade to roof

**Category D — Material-System Gaps**
- Combustible insulation within rated assemblies (Grenfell: ACM panels with PE core)
- Adhesive delamination in CLT/mass timber under fire (exposed laminate falloff
  removes char protection prematurely)
- Partial encapsulation of mass timber — encapsulant falls off before char layer is
  fully established, reducing self-extinguishing potential (2024 CLT research,
  Springer)

### 2.3 The Thermodynamic Navigation Sequence — Fire's Path

Fire does not spread randomly. It follows deterministic thermodynamic gradients.
Understanding the sequence is the precondition for identifying where landscape
authorship is most geometrically effective:

**Phase 1 — Ignition (Gap Entry)**
An ignition source delivers energy to a fuel surface at or above its ignition
temperature. The fire navigator enters the landscape through an energy gap
(insufficient thermal resistance between ignition source and first fuel).

**Phase 2 — Growth (Gap Exploration)**
Combustion releases energy as heat (radiation + convection) and combustion products
(smoke, hot gases). The thermal plume rises. Radiant heat front expands spherically
from the flame. The fire explores available fuel surfaces, traversing gaps between
combustible elements.

**Phase 3 — Flashover (Attractor Basin Transition)**
The critical non-linear event. When upper-layer gas temperature reaches approximately
500-600°C (dependent on compartment geometry and fuel load), radiant heat flux to the
floor plane exceeds ~20 kW/m², causing near-simultaneous ignition of all combustible
surfaces in the compartment. This is the fire navigator reaching an attractor basin —
a stable, self-sustaining fully developed fire state. Post-flashover, the fire becomes
extremely difficult to suppress without structural intervention.

**Phase 4 — Full Development (Attractor Occupancy)**
The fire occupies the compartment fully, regulated primarily by ventilation (oxygen
supply). This is the maximum energy release phase. Structural compromise begins.

**Phase 5 — Spread to Adjacent Compartments (Gap Navigation Continues)**
Fire egresses the origin compartment through:
- Facade ejection (flames breaking through windows, igniting above)
- Barrier penetration (heat conduction through rated assemblies to ignition of
  adjacent combustibles)
- Concealed pathway navigation (shafts, voids, cavities)
- Barrier failure (structural collapse, door failure, firestop failure)

**Phase 6 — Resolution**
Either: (a) fuel or oxygen exhaustion, (b) active suppression, or (c) full structural
involvement. In managed landscapes, a well-authored compartment boundary terminates
the navigator at Phase 5 — it cannot find the gaps required to continue.

### 2.4 The CLT/Mass Timber Finding — A Structural Self-Sealing Mechanism

Recent research (2024, Springer; MDPI; XLAM) on cross-laminated timber reveals a
structurally important phenomenon:

When CLT ignites, it forms a **char layer** — a slow-forming but highly insulating
carbon crust that:
- Chemically inactivates the surface (no longer combustible)
- Thermally insulates the structural wood beneath (reducing heat transmission)
- Progressively recedes inward at a predictable, calculable rate (~0.65-0.7 mm/min)

This char layer is a **self-authored geometric closure** — the material modifies its
own gap signature (combustible surface) during fire navigation, reducing the fuel
available to the navigator over time.

The critical failure condition: if the adhesive layers between CLT laminates are
thermally unstable (PUR adhesives are more vulnerable than MUF), the char layer can
delaminate and fall off, re-exposing fresh wood and resetting the gap.

This represents a geometric incompatibility between the intended self-sealing mechanism
and the actual material system — the adhesive layer is the hidden gap within the
PFP-by-construction system.

## PART III: TRIADIC INVARIANT APPLICATION — CAUSAL GEOMETRY DERIVATION

### 3.1 Identifying the Full Gap Taxonomy Geometrically

Applying the causal geometry framework: the gap (G) in the fire system is not a single
category. Gaps exist at multiple structural levels simultaneously. The navigator
exploits whichever gap it can reach first, and gap availability determines trajectory.

**Gap Level 1 — Fuel Gaps**
The surface gap: any combustible material exposed to the thermal radiant front
constitutes a fuel gap. The fire navigator crosses this gap when the surface temperature
reaches ignition point. The fuel gap density of a space determines how fast the navigator
can traverse it horizontally (surface spread rate).

*Landscape authorship opportunity:*
- Reduce fuel gap density: replace combustible interior finishes with non-combustible
  equivalents at critical propagation surfaces (corridor walls, ceiling planes)
- Create fuel gap discontinuities: place non-combustible breaks in combustible surface
  continuity — interrupting the navigator's surface path without requiring full
  compartment barriers

**Gap Level 2 — Thermal Transmission Gaps**
The barrier gap: any location where heat can transmit through a rated or
non-rated barrier to ignite material on the far side. This includes:
- Uninsulated steel structural members conducting heat through the barrier
  (thermal bridging)
- Thin or low-mass barriers transmitting heat faster than their rating assumes
  at the actual fire load
- Service penetrations with inadequate firestop material for the penetrant type

*Landscape authorship opportunity:*
- Eliminate thermal bridge paths through rated assemblies
- Specify firestop systems matched to the specific penetrant geometry and fire
  exposure duration (not generic solutions for non-generic penetrations)

**Gap Level 3 — Airflow / Convective Pathway Gaps**
The pressure gap: hot gases and smoke travel through any available low-resistance
pathway. The building's HVAC network, shaft geometry, and pressure differentials
determine where the convective arm of the fire navigator can reach before the
thermal arm. Smoke toxicity and oxygen depletion operate through this gap at a faster
timeline than thermal spread.

*Landscape authorship opportunity:*
- Stairwell pressurization systems (active, but pre-positioned in design)
- HVAC compartmentalization and fire/smoke damper integration
- Topological elimination of uninterrupted vertical shafts by physical compartmentation
  at each floor penetration

**Gap Level 4 — Void / Concealed Pathway Gaps**
The hidden gap: fire navigating through spaces that are not visible, not inspectable,
and not monitored — facade cavities, ceiling plenums, raised floor voids, wall
interstitial spaces. These gaps are geometrically significant because:
- They are uncontrolled fuel and airflow channels
- They bypass rated compartment barriers at their perimeter (not through their face)
- They are the most common failure mode in catastrophic fire events (Grenfell, King's
  Cross Underground, 1987)

*Landscape authorship opportunity:*
- Cavity barriers: physical blocking of void propagation pathways at regular intervals
  and at every floor/wall intersection
- Non-combustible void linings: removing fuel from concealed pathways entirely
- Sealing the entry and exit points of all void pathways at rated barriers

**Gap Level 5 — Structural Integrity Gaps**
The collapse gap: fire weakens structural materials. Steel loses yield strength above
400°C; concrete spalls under rapid heating; timber char recedes through structural
sections. When structural elements fail, compartment geometries change — rated barriers
may collapse, creating new gaps that were not present in the original designed landscape.

*Landscape authorship opportunity:*
- Passive fire protection of structural elements (intumescent coating of steel,
  concrete encasement, timber size overdesign to allow for char depth)
- Redundant structural paths so that failure of a single element does not cascade
  to compartment boundary failure

### 3.2 The Fire Attractor Basins — Structural Fixed Points

Applying the causal geometry derivation method (find the fixed points):

Fire has structural attractors — stable states toward which its navigation tends
given the gap availability of the landscape:

**Attractor Basin A — Flashover**
The strong attractor. Once upper-layer gas temperature exceeds ~500°C,
the system undergoes irreversible phase transition to fully developed fire.
This is the point of no structural recovery without massive intervention.

Geometric property: **Flashover is the fire navigator reaching an unstable
equilibrium at high energy** — from which the only exit is exhaustion of fuel/oxygen
or structural interruption of the feedback loop.

**Key structural observation:**
Flashover requires:
1. Sufficient fuel surface area to sustain radiation feedback
2. Sufficient ventilation to supply oxygen
3. Sufficient thermal mass in the gas phase to maintain temperature above 500°C
4. Insufficient thermal sinks to remove energy from the upper layer faster than
   it accumulates

This means flashover has **four structural preconditions** — all four must be present.
Each is a potential landscape authorship target. Removing any one prevents the attractor.

**Attractor Basin B — Self-Extinguishment**
The weak attractor, rarely reached without designed assistance.
Fire self-extinguishes when fuel is exhausted or oxygen is depleted below ~15% 
(stoichiometric limit). In a tightly compartmented space with limited fuel load,
fire can self-extinguish without flashover.

*This is the landscape authorship target:*
Design the compartment such that the fire navigator finds a self-extinguishing
attractor before it finds the flashover attractor.

**Attractor Basin C — Contained Compartment Fire**
The engineered attractor. A fully rated compartment with no exploitable gaps
contains the navigator within a bounded space. The navigator reaches its completion
(fuel exhaustion or decay) within the compartment. The rated barriers hold.
Resolution: no spread to adjacent compartments.

This is the central objective of pre-positioned structural fire management.

### 3.3 Geometric Incompatibilities — Where Landscape Authorship Is Most Potent

The most important finding of the causal geometry derivation method is this:
**Geometric incompatibilities between current practice and the fire's navigational
requirements represent the highest-leverage intervention points.**

These are the locations where the current built environment inadvertently
*authors a landscape that assists fire navigation* — and where deliberate
counter-authorship is possible.

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #1: The Service Penetration Problem**

*Current practice:*
Building services (pipes, cables, conduits, ducts) penetrate rated fire barriers.
Code requires firestopping at each penetration. In practice, installation quality
is highly variable, lifecycle degradation is poorly tracked, and post-construction
modifications frequently breach these stops without remediation.

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
Every service penetration is a pre-authored gap in the compartment boundary — a
hole of known geometry that the fire navigator can cross if the firestop fails.
The firestop is the only authored closure. If it fails for any reason
(improper installation, wrong material for penetrant, degradation, modification),
the gap is live.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The compartment boundary is designed as a continuous rated surface, but service
penetration density in modern buildings means the boundary is **discontinuous by
default** — it is a collection of rated face material punctuated by hundreds of
firestop-dependent closures of variable quality and unknown current status.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- Design for **penetration consolidation**: route all services through a single
  rated shaft assembly rather than distributed penetrations through each barrier,
  reducing the gap count per barrier from dozens to one shaft boundary
- Specify **self-restoring firestop systems**: intumescent materials that do not
  require maintenance to remain effective (expand on heat, not on manual activation)
- Implement **penetration mapping as a built record**: every penetration has a
  documented firestop system, material, installation date, and inspection history
  — making gap status auditable, not assumed

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #2: The Void Pathway Problem**

*Current practice:*
Cavity barriers are required in building codes at specific intervals and locations.
However, the void spaces themselves (plenum ceilings, raised floors, facade cavities,
interstitial wall spaces) remain present as continuous uninterrupted channels between
their mandated barriers.

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
Void spaces are **topological channels** — connected pathways with low resistance
to both thermal and convective spread. Because they are concealed, fire can navigate
them without detection, bypassing rated barriers at the perimeter rather than through
the face.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The fire's convective arm (smoke, hot gases) moves through void pathways an order of
magnitude faster than the thermal arm moves through rated barriers. Cavity barriers
block this pathway at specific points, but the pathway itself is structurally present
as a continuous channel. The barrier is a point intervention in a channel system.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- **Void space fuel elimination**: line all concealed void spaces with non-combustible
  material, removing the fuel surface from the channel entirely. The fire navigator
  can traverse the channel but finds no gap to ignite — no fuel discontinuity to cross.
- **Void space topological interruption**: design building systems to minimize void
  space continuity. Voids that must exist (structural depth for services) are designed
  as discrete segments rather than continuous channels.
- **Void space pressurization**: establish slight positive pressure in ceiling plenums
  relative to occupied space — any breach in the plenum boundary exhausts air outward
  rather than allowing smoke to infiltrate inward.

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #3: The Thermal Bridge Problem**

*Current practice:*
Rated fire barriers are tested as assemblies — wall face materials, structural framing,
insulation, and finish layers — under standard fire exposure. The rating reflects the
whole assembly performance. However, structural elements that span across the barrier
plane (steel beams, columns, floor connections) create thermal bridge pathways that
conduct heat through the assembly faster than the rated face materials do.

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
Structural elements are thermally conductive. A steel column connecting a fire
compartment to an adjacent space is a continuous metallic pathway for heat transmission
— it bypasses the air gap and insulation that the barrier rating depends on.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The rated assembly assumes heat transfer occurs uniformly through the barrier plane.
The thermal bridge provides a preferential pathway that invalidates this assumption.
Combustibles on the far side of a "rated" barrier adjacent to an unprotected structural
element can reach ignition temperature before the rated period expires.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- **Structural element decoupling at barriers**: design barrier interfaces to thermally
  interrupt structural continuity using fire-rated expansion joints, ceramic fiber breaks,
  or intumescent-coated structural breaks
- **Structural protection scope extension**: intumescent coating applied to structural
  elements is typically specified to protect structural integrity. Extending the scope
  to also break thermal transmission across compartment boundaries requires a different
  specification philosophy — protecting the boundary, not just the element.

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #4: The Vertical Topology Problem**

*Current practice:*
High-rise building design inherently creates vertical channels — stairwells, elevator
shafts, service risers — connecting all floors. These are managed through rated shaft
enclosures, fire doors at each floor entry, and stairwell pressurization systems
(the latter is active, not passive).

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
Vertical shafts are **buoyancy-driven attractors for the fire navigator's convective arm**.
Hot gas rises. Any uninterrupted vertical channel, even a rated one with
theoretically intact doors, creates a thermal pressure differential that drives
convective spread from any breach point to all floors above.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The stack effect in tall buildings creates a persistent pressure gradient that
thermodynamically assists the fire navigator. The navigator does not need to find a
gap in the shaft wall — a single open or failed door at any floor is sufficient to
pressurize the stack and drive smoke through every other floor entry point.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- **Pressurization as pre-positioned design**: stairwell pressurization is currently
  classified as active because it requires a mechanical system to activate. However,
  **passive pressurization geometry** is achievable: design stairwell enclosures with
  controlled air inlet geometry such that thermal buoyancy from a fire event drives
  ambient air into the stairwell from the lower entry points, maintaining a passive
  positive pressure without mechanical activation
- **Shaft compartmentation at each floor**: all service shaft penetrations through
  each floor assembly sealed to rated status — the shaft is a connected series of
  separately rated compartments rather than a single connected volume
- **Lobby pressurization volumes**: design elevator lobby geometry to act as an
  airlock — a small positive-pressure vestibule between occupied floor and shaft entry

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #5: The Fuel Load Distribution Problem**

*Current practice:*
Building codes specify maximum fuel load densities for different occupancy types
but do not regulate the spatial distribution of fuel loads within compartments.
Furniture, contents, and interior finishes are treated as homogeneous.

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
Fire growth rate is determined by **fuel load proximity and continuity**, not just
density. A compartment with evenly distributed combustibles allows fire to spread
continuously from the ignition point. A compartment with clustered combustibles
separated by non-combustible breaks forces the fire navigator to cross thermal
radiation gaps between clusters — slowing growth and potentially preventing flashover.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The compartment boundary is designed to contain the fully developed fire, but the
interior fuel distribution is not designed to prevent the fire from reaching
full development in the first place. The flashover attractor is reached before the
compartment boundary becomes the limiting factor.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- **Interior fuel distribution as a design parameter**: specify combustible content
  zones separated by non-combustible surface breaks at intervals calculated from
  known radiant heat flux decay with distance (r² falloff from flame to adjacent surface)
- **Non-combustible floor zone specification**: corridors, means of egress, and zones
  surrounding structural elements specified as zero-fuel zones — no combustible
  materials within a calculated exclusion radius
- **Ceiling plane fuel management**: ceiling plane surface materials are the
  primary radiating surface that drives flashover feedback. Non-combustible ceiling
  planes with high surface emissivity (low IR reflectance) reduce the radiation
  feedback loop that drives flashover.

---

**INCOMPATIBILITY #6: The CLT Adhesive Gap (Material-System Geometric Incompatibility)**

*Specific to mass timber construction — derived from 2024 literature*

*Current practice:*
CLT/mass timber structures rely on the char layer as a passive fire protection mechanism.
The char layer is the designed protective geometry — it is structurally authored into
the material by virtue of wood's natural charring behavior.

*Geometric structure of the gap:*
The char layer is continuous only as long as the lamellae remain bonded. When adhesive
bonds (specifically PUR adhesives) soften under sustained heat, lamellae separate and
fall from the char surface, removing the insulating char and re-exposing fresh wood.
The re-exposed wood surface is a new fuel gap — the self-sealing mechanism has
been reset.

*Geometric incompatibility:*
The char layer is the intended closure of the fuel gap. The adhesive is the hidden
gap within the closure mechanism itself. A protection system that depends on
structural adhesive integrity under fire conditions contains its own geometric
failure mode — the closure closes the gap but depends on a second structural element
(the adhesive) that the fire attacks directly.

*Landscape authorship resolution:*
- **Adhesive specification as a fire protection parameter**: specify only thermally
  stable adhesives (MUF and similar) in all CLT fire-exposed elements, not as a
  structural performance parameter but as a fire protection parameter
- **Laminate encapsulation as a system requirement**: full encapsulation of CLT surfaces
  exposed to potential fire with non-combustible lining (gypsum board) such that the
  char mechanism is the backup, not the primary protection. The encapsulant is the
  primary closure; the char is the reserve closure.
- **Char depth overdesign as explicit parameter**: size CLT structural elements to
  provide char depth beyond the calculated fire exposure duration plus a delamination
  allowance — treating the outer laminates as sacrificial, designed to separate without
  compromising structural fire resistance

### 4.2 The Structural Invariants — What the Geometry Says Must Be True

Applying the derivation rule: derive what follows *necessarily* from the structure, not
from accumulated opinion.

**Invariant 1:**
*Every gap that exists in a compartment boundary before a fire starts is a potential
navigator entry point. The rated performance of the assembly is irrelevant to gaps
that are not within the rated assembly — it only applies to the rated surface.*

Consequence: The practice of rating barrier face materials without equally rating every
penetration, connection, and adjacent structural element produces an assembly with a
rated face and unrated gaps. The navigator finds the unrated gaps.

**Invariant 2:**
*Flashover is a threshold event with exactly four structural preconditions. All four
must be satisfied simultaneously for flashover to occur. Removing any one is sufficient
to prevent it.*

Consequence: Pre-positioned fire management does not need to eliminate all four
preconditions — it only needs to guarantee the reliable elimination of one.
The most structurally accessible precondition to eliminate architecturally is
**fuel surface continuity** (Precondition 1): interrupt the radiation feedback
between floor plane and upper layer gas by eliminating the continuous combustible
floor-ceiling surface connection.

**Invariant 3:**
*The concealed void space is topologically equivalent to a rated barrier failure
for the fire navigator's convective arm. It bypasses the barrier laterally rather
than penetrating it directly.*

Consequence: A building can have fully rated and intact compartment barriers and
still experience complete cross-barrier smoke spread via void channels at the
perimeter of those barriers. The barrier prevents the fire's thermal arm from crossing.
The void pathway allows the smoke arm (and its toxic products) to bypass it entirely.
Smoke kills before fire does. The more critical gap is the one currently receiving
less structural attention.

**Invariant 4:**
*The fire navigator does not distinguish between a gap that was designed into the
building and a gap that appeared through degradation or modification. It navigates
all available gaps with equal efficiency.*

Consequence: A gap management strategy that relies entirely on initial construction
quality without a lifecycle maintenance protocol is geometrically equivalent to
having no gap management strategy beyond a point in time. Every building that does
not have an active gap audit protocol is assumed by the fire navigator to have the
same gap profile as a building that has never been maintained.

### 4.3 The Novel Structural Observations — What Has Not Been Fully Exploited

The following are derived from the causal geometry framework applied to the
literature — observations that are supported by the underlying physics but have
not yet been fully operationalized in standard practice:

---

**Novel Observation 1: Non-Combustible Interior Surface Geometry as Flashover Prevention**

Current approach: Specify materials by flame spread index (FSI) and smoke development
rating. This is a surface chemistry approach.

Geometric approach: Specify the **spatial arrangement** of non-combustible surface
breaks such that no continuous combustible surface path exists from the ignition zone
to a radiant feedback distance from the ceiling plane.

The physics:
- Flashover is driven by the ceiling radiation feedback loop
- The ceiling radiates to the floor plane (Stefan-Boltzmann radiation)
- The floor plane combustibles ignite when irradiance exceeds ~20 kW/m²
- This irradiance is not uniform — it falls off with distance from the fire plume
  impact zone on the ceiling

A **geometric specification** of non-combustible ceiling zones radiating outward from
the most probable ignition zones (kitchen areas, electrical panels, HVAC equipment)
would interrupt the feedback path without requiring non-combustible treatment of
the entire ceiling — just the geometrically critical portions.

This has not been systematically derived or specified as a design parameter.
It is derivable from first principles with known heat transfer equations.

---

**Novel Observation 2: Void Space Positive Pressure as Passive Smoke Control**

Current approach: Stairwell pressurization is active (mechanically driven).
Void spaces are treated as gap-bearing pathways to be blocked at intervals.

Geometric approach: Design the building envelope and internal pressure geometry such
that void spaces are at **positive pressure relative to occupied zones** during normal
building operation — driven passively by HVAC design or stack effect management.

The physics:
- Void spaces become smoke pathways when they are at lower pressure than the fire zone
  (smoke moves from high to low pressure)
- If void spaces are maintained at positive pressure relative to occupied zones,
  any breach in the void boundary exhausts void air outward rather than pulling
  smoke inward
- This converts void pathways from **gap attractors** to **gap resistors**

This is achievable through passive design (building orientation, envelope openings,
thermal chimney management) in some building typologies, and through HVAC design
in others. It has not been systematically applied as a void management strategy.

**Novel Observation 3: Structural Geometry for Self-Extinguishing Compartment Design**

Current approach: Compartments are designed to contain fires — sized and rated to hold
a fully developed fire for the required duration. The assumption is that flashover
will occur.

Geometric approach: Design compartments whose geometry, fuel load distribution, and
thermal mass configuration are calculated to prevent flashover — creating conditions
where the fire navigator reaches the self-extinguishing attractor before the flashover
attractor.

The physics:
- Flashover requires upper-layer gas temperature ≥ 500-600°C
- Upper-layer temperature is a function of heat release rate (HRR) minus heat loss rate
- Heat loss rate depends on thermal mass of bounding surfaces (walls, floor, ceiling)
- High thermal mass bounding surfaces absorb heat faster, suppressing upper-layer
  temperature rise
- Compartments with high thermal mass AND bounded fuel load can be geometrically
  calculated to stay below flashover threshold

This is the **thermal mass as flashover prevention** concept. It is acknowledged in
the literature (thermal mass buildings perform better in fire) but has not been
systematically treated as a **designed prevention geometry** — specifying the
minimum bounding surface thermal mass as a function of the designed fuel load to
guarantee the compartment stays below flashover conditions.

This is a calculable structural parameter. It has not been codified as a design
standard because the field's default assumption is that flashover will occur and
the compartment should contain it. The geometric alternative — design to prevent
flashover rather than survive it — represents a fundamentally different authorship
of the fire's landscape.

**Novel Observation 4: The Adhesive as a First-Class Fire Protection Specification Item**

*Specific to mass timber — generalizable to adhesive-assembled systems*

Current approach: Adhesive specification in CLT is a structural/manufacturing concern.
Fire protection of CLT is treated as the char layer mechanism plus optional encapsulation.

Geometric approach: The adhesive is a structural component of the fire protection
mechanism because the char layer's integrity depends on lamellae adhesion under fire
conditions. Adhesive thermal stability is therefore a first-class fire protection
parameter — not a secondary structural one.

The derivation:
- CLT fire protection by char layer requires: char forms at surface → char insulates
  subsurface wood → subsurface wood retains structural capacity
- This requires: surface lamellae remain bonded through the char formation period
- This requires: adhesive thermal stability > char formation time × heating rate
- PUR adhesive softening point is below the critical temperature
- Therefore: PUR adhesive in CLT produces a geometric incompatibility between
  the intended char protection mechanism and the actual material system behavior
- Resolution: specify adhesive thermal stability as a fire protection parameter

This observation is supported by the 2024 full-scale CLT research (Springer, 2024)
but has not been translated into a specification standard.

## PART V: SYNTHESIS — LANDSCAPE AUTHORSHIP PRIORITY MAP

### The Pre-Positioned Structural Fire Management Hierarchy

Based on the geometric derivation, ranked by:
1. Leverage (how much navigational freedom does closing this gap remove?)
2. Reliability (how durable is the closure against degradation and modification?)
3. Current gap (how underserved is this by current practice?)

---

**TIER 1 — HIGHEST LEVERAGE / MOST UNDERSERVED**

**1A. Void Space Fuel Elimination and Topological Interruption**

Close the concealed pathway network at the fuel level, not just at point barriers.
Non-combustible void linings eliminate the fuel surface from the channel.
Topological interruption at floor levels eliminates channel continuity.

*Why it ranks first:*
- Void pathways are the primary mechanism for smoke spread (the primary killer)
- Current practice relies on point barriers in a continuous channel
- No lifecycle degradation issue once non-combustible lining is installed
- Directly addresses the Grenfell-class failure mode

**1B. Interior Surface Geometry for Flashover Prevention**

Specify non-combustible surface breaks in ceiling and wall planes using geometric
calculation from known radiation heat transfer — not uniform material substitution.

*Why it ranks high:*
- Prevents flashover rather than surviving it
- Applicable to retrofit as well as new construction
- Currently not a standard design parameter — represents genuine gap
- Calculable from first principles with standard heat transfer equations

---

**TIER 2 — HIGH LEVERAGE / MODERATELY ADDRESSED**

**2A. Penetration Consolidation and Self-Restoring Firestop Systems**

Reduce gap count by routing services through consolidated shaft assemblies.
Specify intumescent systems for all penetrations to ensure lifecycle reliability.
Implement penetration records as built documents.

**2B. Thermal Bridge Interruption at Compartment Boundaries**

Specify structural decoupling at rated barrier interfaces to eliminate thermal
bridge transmission pathways. Treat as a fire protection parameter, not a
structural-only parameter.

---

**TIER 3 — MEDIUM LEVERAGE / SPECIALIZED APPLICATION**

**3A. Passive Stairwell Pressure Geometry**

Design vertical shaft enclosures to exploit stack effect as a positive pressure
mechanism, reducing dependency on active pressurization for basic smoke control.

**3B. CLT Adhesive Specification as Fire Protection Parameter**

Mandate thermally stable adhesives (MUF class or equivalent) for all CLT fire-exposed
elements. Treat laminate bonding integrity through fire exposure as a fire protection
specification item.

**3C. Thermal Mass Compartment Design for Self-Extinguishment**

Calculate minimum bounding surface thermal mass as a function of designed fuel load
to guarantee compartments stay below flashover threshold. Codify as an alternative
design pathway to traditional rated-barrier-only approaches.

## PART VI: FALSIFIABLE PREDICTIONS

The causal geometry framework requires: *derive predictions before looking, not

The following structural predictions follow from the derivation above. They are
falsifiable — each specifies a testable structural claim.

**Prediction 1:**
Buildings with continuous non-combustible void linings will show statistically
significantly lower smoke casualties per fire event than identical buildings with
void spaces managed solely by point cavity barriers, independent of fire origin

*Falsification condition:* Evidence that smoke casualty rate is equivalent

**Prediction 2:**

*Falsification condition:* Evidence that adhesive type has no statistically

*Falsification condition:* Evidence that the calculated thermal mass threshold
does not correlate with flashover prevention in testing.

**Prediction 4:**
Building topologies that reduce void space channel continuity through floor-level

concentration during lower-floor fire events compared to buildings with equivalent
cavity barrier specification but continuous void channels.

*Falsification condition:* Evidence that void channel continuity does not
significantly affect cross-floor smoke propagation independent of cavity barrier
density.

## PART VII: WHAT THIS DERIVATION CANNOT DETERMINE

Applying the epistemological standard of the framework: the derivation is coherent.
Coherence is not truth. These are the limits:

1. **This derivation has not been validated against empirical fire testing data.**
   All findings are structural — derived from known physics and the gap analysis.
   Empirical validation is required before these findings move from derivation
   to engineering specification.

2. **Cost-benefit analysis is outside this derivation.**
   The geometric framework identifies high-leverage gaps. It does not determine
   whether closing those gaps is economically optimal compared to alternative
   risk management strategies.

3. **Code and regulatory context is not analyzed here.**
   Some of the identified interventions may already be required in specific
   jurisdictions. This derivation addresses the structural fire management
   landscape as a geometry — not as a regulatory compliance question.

4. **The pre-positioned / active boundary is not sharp.**
   Some interventions identified (stairwell pressurization, HVAC dampers) exist
   on the boundary between pre-positioned and active. This derivation has treated
   them as pre-positioned where they are designed into the building's permanent
   structure, but the boundary is a design decision, not a hard categorical one.
