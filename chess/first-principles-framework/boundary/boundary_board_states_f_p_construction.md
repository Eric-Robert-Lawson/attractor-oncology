It adds no primitive to the primitive catalog (`boundary_board_state_primitive_catalog.md`, admission rule §1) and does not touch the hard core (`boundary_board_states.md` §14 items 7 to 11). The methods, goals and procedures in it belong to the protective belt. The approach recorded is one chosen basis and is not claimed to exclude others (item 55). The dependency of the priors on an engine is not a definitional influence (item 64). Statements marked **the designer's** are the designer's. Statements marked **this file's reading** are readings of his words, offered for correction. Nothing in this file has been run. ## 1. The designer's statements (21 to 23 November 2026)

1. **Three anchoring points define F.** The inputs of F are the goals, the engines and the priors. They are the three architectural anchoring points that define the emergent solution for F, and they fit the structure of F and P. How the approach scales with irreversible segmented boundaries and with master priors is a consideration that exists. It is scoped to P, because it concerns the merging and the priors, and it is not a strict consideration now.
2. **d-depth and proof size are native to the priors.** The d-depth limitation depends on the limits of the proof size calculation, and it scopes the certainty of partial certification. The priors record the d-depth and proof size limitation they emerged from, for each irreversible segmented boundary, and carry it forward. It is not a hard-coded limit of the program and can be varied at the level of the F and P workflow. A change in the limitation is carried forward as certified only after the exact check within the scope of that change. The priors determine the limitation differently for each boundary. This is not a gap in the architecture of F.
3. **The engine is an evaluator, with the widest compatibility.** The engine is open to as wide a pool of choice as possible. It is the least impactful of the anchoring points, and it constrains the other two, because they must allow the modular approach to engine compatibility at the level of F.
4. **The priors are an output of P with a known shape.** The priors are an input to F and an output of P. Their shape is already specified in the program: the d-depth metric and its schema, the deliberate gaps for the cost of storage and speed, and chaining. Chaining mitigates the d-depth and proof size limitation, because once a chain reaches a moment of certainty the proof size can restart from that anchor point. The specifics of the priors are not known, and what they provide is understood. P is an integrated specification: the engineering of how to accomplish the outcome already specified for the priors.
5. **The goals are the programmable front of F.** The goals for White and for Black are what F leaves open, with deliberate variance. The goals that reach optimality cannot be defined beforehand, so the front is unbounded and should allow as much modification as possible. Goals can be programmed explicitly relative to the structure the priors provide, with variance allowed elsewhere. The engine is used as an evaluator and the priors are considered.
6. **The fronts of variance.** The d-depth and proof size limitation for study and for storage can differ, and this is a front of variance. The goals for White and Black, relative to the priors, are a front of variance. Which side sees which priors is a front of variance (Black sees the Black priors and White the White priors, or both see all available priors), and the goals decide the visibility.
7. **The effect of visibility is not determined without testing.** The designer can predict the effects of the choices of visibility, and they are estimates until tested. This is a deliberate architectural consideration: the programmable front must allow it.
8. **Examples of goals.** With the priors visible to both sides, White can aim at the move choices reached with the most certainty within the proof size and d-depth limitation, and Black at navigating as far from the certainty of the priors as the opportunities allow. Both can follow the priors for both sides, to check whether one prior is inherently stronger for one side. White can follow the priors as far as it can while Black deliberately surveys calculably more difficult positions down a specific branch of reachability, not unguided. There is an entire sandbox of potential.
9. **The priors are never forced to zero.** Any batch of games can be run through P to emerge as priors. The priors act as a database of a kind, and P is required to continue the loop and to accumulate anything at all. The case of zero priors needs no P. A database of games does.
10. **The goals need more from the priors than a move, and P may need differential testing.** This is where the designer identified the requirement to resolve P to some degree. The certainty the priors provide can adjust the proof size on that bias basis. Optimality can be understood from differential tests, relative to the optimality of the games and how the branches depend on the priors, measuring the priors that bias towards optimality against those that bias into bad positions. For P to decide what to omit and add, differential testing between priors and a measurement of optimality are important. Resolving P properly therefore likely involves differential testing, which involves F, as a compositional and recursive dependency.
11. **The approach to construction.** F is a modular framework. Any compatible engine is added modularly as an evaluator. A programmable front for the goals of Black and White is added, and a parameter sets how many games are played. The priors are a modular addition, integrated when P is resolved. A batch is output with duplicate branches avoided or not added. The framework is like an incomplete script into which engines and priors are put as modular additions, and the goals define the rest. P is resolved to create the priors in the way F needs, with the variance the goals allow, and its output is integrated into the framework.
12. **Progress is recorded and preserved.** The progressive understanding should be recorded and formalized, because it is hard to derive again. It is recorded here and not in the engine integration file, so that a failure of this path does not contaminate that work.
13. **The entry points of the loop.** With zero priors the loop starts with F. When a batch of games is to serve as priors, it starts with P, which flows into F, and F then resolves to give priors. Those priors are used in F to begin as with zero priors, with the goals set.
14. **What P decides, and what it can resolve alone.** P decides what to omit and keep as priors, balancing speed, coverage and storage. The emergent effects depend on the influence of the priors on speed, coverage and storage, and the designer is not certain how to measure them without a differential test in which F is run for a batch with a specific goal. For a batch given to P with zero priors: lines that resolved in a loss can be removed, which needs only a measurement of the metrics of advantage relative to the board state and a choice of routing relative to what is unresolved. A board state with a lot of potential that cannot be resolved within the proof size may be worth keeping and iterating on. For the winning side's priors the questions are which board states give important winning information that is not trivial to derive, and which gaps are trivial enough to omit. The terminal condition for certifying the d-depth metric depends on the exact check within the limitation. P depends on F, and some resolution can be made from the evaluator alone. The cost of certifying a board state, which comes from the exact check, is a true cost that affects the storage measurement and the cost of coverage relative to the unresolved space.

4. **Visibility.** A side that sees only its own priors can play from its own repertoire and cannot anticipate the other side. A side that sees both can model the opponent from the opponent's priors. Visibility is read as deciding whether opponent modelling is possible. Which is better is not determined (item 7).
6. **Differential testing and the recursion.** Differential testing of priors is either run by P calling F, or run as iterations of F whose goal is the comparison and whose batches P merges. Both are consistent with item 10, and which is used is not specified. Either way P depends recursively on F. The measurement of optimality may use certified results where there are any and evaluator values otherwise, which are heuristic (engine integration, reading 20).
8. **Certainty, game outcome and the unresolved.** A merged network of games is a heuristic prior. An entry is certified only when the exact check within d has resolved it. A lost game is a datum about the pair of agents and not about the position, so removing a line on the outcome of a game alone is a heuristic. A line that loses is kept or left as a gap by the keep-or-gap decision. A promising position that cannot be resolved is read as recorded as examined and unresolved, which costs a key and a status and marks the frontier of the next iterations. How such a position is recorded is open (§3).
9. **The static and dynamic parts of P.** When the exact check certifies a claim, the size of the proof tree comes out of it as a by-product, and it is the cost of deriving the entry again. The storage cost of an entry and that re-derivation cost are static, and with the evaluator's labels they are available to P without further games. The benefit of an entry, how often it is used and how much coverage it enables, needs runs of F with a goal that tests the change. So P has a static part from the check and the evaluator, and a dynamic part that depends on F.
10. **The bootstrap, the schedule of the limitation, and what can be tested alone.** The base case is the terminals and the certified results of lower boundaries, which need no priors, so the bootstrap is not circular. A low limitation can be certified from that base, and the certified entries are anchors that shorten checks at a higher limitation, since the depth certified at a root is the depth searched to the anchor plus the depth for which the anchor is certified (engine integration, reading 11). The schedule resembles iterative deepening and the layering by which tablebases are generated, a structural resemblance. The cost of a check grows with d, so an attempt at a low d costs a small part of an attempt at a high d and the initial depth is not a critical calibration: a claim can be attempted at increasing d until the window is exhausted, and the d reached is the emergent result. The re-derivation cost recorded for an entry is relative to the d at which it was checked, so raising d changes what is cheap to derive again and the keep-or-gap judgments are reconsidered. The proof sizes at the current leaves give a projection of the cost of an extension, which can inform when raising the limitation is viable. The fixed window applies to a single check, and the certified scope that accumulates through chaining grows with the stored anchors, which is how the certified proof size of item 18 can increase with storage while the window stays the same. A baseline P that merges games into a heuristic prior and F with zero priors can each be run alone, and the recursion bites for the dynamic part of P and for goals that use the priors, so "cannot be tested independently" holds for the specified, optimized forms.
12. **The check as a goal.** The designer suggested earlier a form in which the goal of the opposing side is to enumerate every reply within d from an anchoring point, while the certified side follows the moves of the priors, and the engine proposes moves only where the priors hold none. It needs three things (engine integration, reading 22): the replies of the opposing side are enumerated by the move generation of the program and not chosen by the engine; terminals are detected by the rules and exits by the query surface of the priors; the proof size is counted. The batch is then a proof tree, a set of lines and not unrelated games, so the batch format (§3) has to allow it. The goal is a case of the general goal front and not a different mechanism. Admission as certified remains at the gate of P (engine integration, readings 17 and 22). This is the plain exhaustive form. It is recorded as a baseline and does not fix how the goal is constructed, which item 40 leaves open, and it does not exclude using the evaluator inside the check.
17. **The reset at a chaining point, the allowance, and the choice of the reset point.** A check is rooted at a position and ends at the anchors it reaches, so the proof size is spent on the segment between the root and those anchors. The certificate of an anchor is not spent again because it is stored, and the depth certified at the root is the search depth to the anchor plus the anchor's certified depth (engine integration, reading 11). Two conditions are part of it: the anchor has to be a certified entry, since an anchor that is only a heuristic prior carries no certified depth, and it has to be reachable within the window, so the result depends on which priors are kept (item 21). The keep-or-gap rule is then the trade: a stored anchor buys a reset, and a gap costs a re-derivation inside the next window. If the segment before the anchor uses less than the window, an allowance remains, usable to widen (examine other moves of the certified side along the chain) or to deepen (continue to a farther anchor, or check from the anchor itself, which has its own window). The outcome of item 26 follows: an existing entry is certified at a greater d, or entries are added. A reset does not guarantee the next chain resets, since that needs a certified anchor that is reachable. The anchor at which a check ends is a choice: the nearest certified anchor spends least and leaves the largest allowance, and a farther anchor spends more and certifies deeper. How much the allowance is worth depends on how far apart the certified anchors are, which is empirical. The programme does not rank bias coverages by a separate measure (engine integration, §1, item 12).
18. **The loop that the check closes.** Two goal configurations alternate. Under the check configuration, F certifies the d reachable from an anchoring point within the window, and P admits the result with its d and proof size (reading 12). Under the coverage configuration, F runs from those priors to resolve more of the space, P merges the batch, and the check is applied again at a raised limitation. The check configuration is the constant goal of P and the coverage configuration is whatever goal the agent programs (item 41). The check recurs on every cycle, so its cost per call is the recurring cost of the program, and item 22's efficiency refers to this. This file does not specify how to make it efficient. The check enumerates every reply, so its batch is a proof tree and not a sample of games, which item 24 states is intended. What is open is how a goal expresses that kind of batch, and not whether the design allows it.
19. **How attempts are kept apart from this record.** This file holds the construction understanding and the designer's statements. Any record of what an attempt did or failed to do belongs in a separate file begun when attempts begin (item 23). If a result bears on a statement here, the designer decides whether it is revised.
21. **Certification along a chain, and what is recorded for a board state.** A proof tree certifies each position inside it only to the d that remains from the root, the d of the root less the plies to that position. A position to be certified at the full d needs a check of its own with its own window, and the proof sizes of these checks differ by position. Certification is therefore made along the chain (item 25), and the d recorded differs between positions on one chain. Each entry carries its own d and proof size, the limit at which its certification completed within the window. Item 2 has the limitation differ between boundaries. That is consistent with item 28: the boundary keys the priors and each entry within it carries its own d. If item 28 is meant to replace the per-boundary statement of item 2, that is the designer's decision, and item 2 is left as stated.
22. **What the batch supplies and what the check supplies.** In the check of reading 12 the moves of the certified side come from the priors, and those priors come from the batch through P. So the batch supplies the candidate, the lines and moves the check is asked about, and the check supplies the certification. The batch's role in defining coverage is the one item 25 gives, and the check cannot certify a line that no prior contains.
30. **The closure, and what invalidates it.** The proof tree carries the closure: every deviation within the d ends at an entry that is certified or at an anchor, so the entries reached compose the coverage. A verified win is permanent, and a result held only within d stays conditional on it (engine integration, reading 2). A result that rests on an anchor that is only conditional inherits that condition, and an anchor refuted at a greater d alters the results that depend on it. How the dependent entries are found and reopened is not specified.
31. **Where the earlier statements of the d-depth metric stand.** The earlier statements are those of the protective belt, and the hard core does not state d. They appear in the main document (the d-depth metric statement of 16 November 2026: the header [d][index][symmetry condition], with d the number of plies to the terminal condition), in the statements on d-depth scoping of 15 November 2026 (primitive catalog, start selection, Syzygy mining), and in the documents that build on them (calibration, d-depth applications, batch composition, cost model). Two differences: in the earlier form d is stated and the end of the calculation is a terminal reason, while in item 28 d is the result and the end of a check is any certified entry, with the terminals as the base case. One continuity: the chain between the anchor and the terminal that is not indexed (calibration, item 9) is the same idea as the storage gaps of item 28. The difference is deliberate (item 35), and none of the other documents has been changed.
34. **What items 31 and 32 rest on.** The expectation that proof size is smaller further down is a hypothesis that has not been established. The quantity it appeals to is the irreversible progress that remains, and proof size tracks that and not the number of plies inside one segment. It can be observed in the proof sizes the checks record along the branches of a batch. Near the initial position a check that cannot complete within the window records nothing as certified, so no separate rule against certified entries there is needed. Item 32 rests on four points. The edge is the limit of exhaustive coverage that the tables and the program share, so it depends on which tables the machine has. If the region within the tables is not recorded, a check that ends in it uses the tables as a source of certified results, so the query on the priors would have to reach such a source, and where the machine has no tables the check has to resolve that region within its own window. The edge moves toward the initial position as certified entries accumulate beyond it. And a check near the start may fail to complete within the window, which is separate from item 32's reason of the effect on openings. Item 32 is read to rest on the hypothesis above and on engines having the tables, and nothing has been run to test either.
41. **One operation or many.** Fitting the batch into one operation and running one game per operation are one structure with a different parameter, which agrees with item 44. They are not equal in cost. One operation over the sum shares the overlap between games inside a single window, and the depth reached falls as games are added. Separate operations give depth to each game but reuse overlap only through what is stored as anchors and re-derive the omitted overlap (item 42), and their results depend on the order of the merges. The choice is a case of the balance of item 37. Item 44's remark that merging two priors needs another approach than merging a batch into existing priors is not reconciled with the monotone merge of certificates elsewhere (engine integration, readings 10 and 18).
42. **Where the pruning of item 45 applies.** The certificate holds one winning child at each node where the certified side chooses, so the alternatives that lose cost search effort and not certificate size. At each node where the other side chooses, every reply has to be covered, although a reply that loses quickly is cheap to cover (reading 12). The pruning of item 45 is read to apply to the first kind of node. Item 45 does not say whether its eight options belong to the side that is choosing, so this is the reading to correct. Item 47 accepts it, subject to being understood in practice.
44. **The perimeter of a proof tree.** A proof tree ends in two kinds of state. One is a certified anchor, where the check ends and the window resets, and which carries its own certified depth (reading 17). The other is a state at which the window of the check is exhausted, from which nothing further is checked. Item 49's zero is read as the second kind, since beyond it is the unchecked region. Whether the first kind belongs to the perimeter is not stated (§3). The zero is relative to the check that produced the tree: a state on the perimeter can be given a higher d by a check of its own, and certified entries do not decrease, so zero is the floor of what is recorded and not a fixed property of the state. After item 52 the stored boundary is at d-depth 1 or higher, so the entries stored are certified at that d and the states at zero are not stored. The boundary is neither a heuristic entry (item 43) nor a certified bad choice (item 45). It is the edge between the examined and the unexamined, and the lines beyond it stay unexamined and unkept. The nearest recorded notion is a position held as examined and unresolved (reading 8), which was stated for promising positions and not for every state on the edge. The perimeter states are the places through which a result found later in the unchecked region would reach the entries that rest on them (a connection the designer has not stated, bearing on reading 30). The d-depth 1 certification of an anchoring state covers the legal plies available from it, and each further d is one more layer of legal plies from the perimeter, so the perimeter is the frontier of an expansion by layers from the anchoring points, which is the iterative deepening of reading 10. The states at zero are the legal plies of the states at d-depth 1.
46. **The perimeter and the size of the tree, with an arithmetic check.** In a tree in which every node has b children, the leaves are about (b-1)/b of all the nodes, so storing the leaves alone saves a factor of about b/(b-1): 2 for b equal to 2, 1.5 for 3, about 1.1 for 10. That factor does not grow with depth. With the pruning of item 45, in which the certified side has one child at its nodes, the leaf fraction also stayed constant in depth, between about a quarter and four fifths, depending on b and on which side moves last. Item 50's "at least a multiplicative factor" is consistent with this. These figures are for an idealized tree. Where positions repeat inside a proof tree it is smaller than its unfolded form and the ratio differs, so the relation of area to perimeter is one to measure, since the perimeter count and the proof size are both recorded by the checks (reading 9). The lean in item 50 is the designer's.
47. **How "already covered" may follow from items 48 to 51.** Item 41 gives the scope of a check as the differential of the batch relative to the existing priors. If the priors hold the perimeter of earlier trees with the d at which each entry stands, a line a check follows is one of three kinds: it reaches a certified entry whose recorded d is enough for what is sought, and is covered; it reaches a perimeter state, and is covered up to that state; or it reaches nothing stored, and is outside what is covered. This is one way the statements of items 49 and 50 could supply the test that this file lacked. The comparison of the recorded d with the d sought, and what a check does at a perimeter state, are not stated. Whether a check begins again from a perimeter state is a case of the retention of work (reading 63), and may bear on the repeated failed attempts of item 46, which the designer has not stated. Item 54 says how a later check proceeds varies with the goal, so the three kinds are a baseline and not a rule. Item 51 is read as the same boundary seen from two batches: the perimeter of one batch's tree is where the region of another can begin, and what lies between is the unknown region. Comparing the priors of such batches is differential testing (reading 6) or a measure of the optimality of a batch (§3), and which is meant is not stated.
48. **What the boundary at d-depth 1 asks.** The d at a position along a tree is the d at the root less the plies to the position, highest at the anchor and lowest at the edge, which is the map of item 52 for one path. Item 52 says minimum, and how a position reached at different plies or in different trees gets its d under that word is not tied to a rule (§3). Item 57 confirms the d belongs to the position and not to the path. The positions at d-depth 0 are the successors of those at d-depth 1. At nodes where the other side chooses, they are all the legal replies, which the move generation gives. At nodes where the certified side chooses, the tree holds one child (reading 42), so the entry would have to hold the certified move for the zero states to be derived, which sits against item 79 and is open (§3). With that condition, the expectation of item 52 that the d-depth 0 states are very calculable from the d-depth 1 states is read to hold. Moving the stored level one ply in reduces the count by about b where the other side chooses, and not at all where the certified side chooses (reading 46). Which level is stored remains the designer's lean of d-depth 1, open to the balance (item 52). The states at which a tree ends at a certified anchor carry that anchor's high certified depth, so they are read as outside the perimeter, which item 52 does not say and which is a question (§3).
53. **The senses of reachability.** Item 58 separates them, and asks that this be documented clearly. General: the moves needed to navigate from one board state to another within a boundary, which depends on the mode of play. The exact check enumerates every reply of the side that does not choose (reading 12), so the reachability it certifies is read to be that of the optimally adversarial case, which the designer has not stated. Counted as d: each ply and each iteration of the limitation is reachability in the general sense, counted from the anchoring points (item 58). As cost: the proof size to resolve between two stored board states (item 37). Within the window: something reachable as a proof tree within the window (items 33 and 38), which is the cost sense fitting inside the window of a check and does not mean an engine resolves the gap at effective speed. The distance of item 56 and the cost of item 37 are two measurements of reachability, how far and what it costs, and not a conflict.
55. **The engine: roles, what is and is not recorded, and what the priors can exceed.** The statements give the engine three roles. One is a source of moves in the self-play of F (items 5 and 11). One is a possible aid inside the exact check, for ordering and for choosing moves at gaps, with exactness kept (item 40). One is the consumer of the priors at play, navigating the gaps between stored certified points (items 38 and 43). Items 60 and 61 concern the third role, and the first where the strongest engine builds the priors. The uncertainty of item 59, how evaluations should impact the priors and the check, concerns the first two, and is left open. None of the three is part of the definition of F or P (item 64), and nothing recorded defines the check, F, P or the keep-or-gap decision relative to a particular engine. The check stands without the evaluator in the sense that its exactness does not depend on it (engine integration, reading 14), and in the baseline of reading 12 the engine only proposes moves for the certified side where the priors hold none, which could be enumerated at a greater cost. Item 59 is read as speaking of the potential to optimize and not as a change to item 3, which is put as a question (§3). That the keep-or-gap decision may depend on the engine in the slot, since the cost of crossing a gap can differ with the engine, is a possibility the program allows and not part of its definition. An entry certified by the exact check is exact within its d, or permanent if it is a win, so its content is not bounded by the heuristic strength of an engine, and what bounds it is the window of proof size and the storage. That is the reading under which priors stronger than the strongest engine are possible (item 62): the engine can serve as a bootstrap, as an aid to efficiency in the check, and as a consumer of the priors, and it is not the ceiling. Priors affect play only where play reaches the regions they cover, so a gain appears in games that enter a certified region where the engine alone would err inside it, which is the divergence of engine integration, reading 14. How often it occurs is empirical, and nothing has been run. Whether the gap criterion of item 38 makes the stored distance depend on the engine is not stated (§3).
60. **The tablebase resemblance, and what item 66 concerns.** The resemblance is structural: the layering in which each layer is resolved from the one before (reading 10), and the one-way dependency between boundaries (engine integration, reading 18). In tablebases, the table for a given material depends on the tables that captures or promotions lead to, which are irreversible changes. The dependency is one way, generation goes from the least material upward, and a probe loads the tables it needs. Read the same way, the boundaries a check can reach are those that the irreversible moves from its root lead to, so the priors one check needs are bounded by that reach and not by all the boundaries. Which priors are held in storage at a time, whether one boundary's priors are loaded when required and replaced by the next or all are held within one window, is primarily storage management and one more case of the storage limit of item 37. Whether it also changes what a check can certify depends on whether the window is applied to one boundary at a time or to all together (§3). The designer does not claim which is best.
63. **The working memory of a check.** Keying the work of a check by board state, so that a state reached by different move orders is derived once, is the mechanism by which work is kept (item 42), and agrees with item 57. It removes repeated derivation and compression can shrink what is kept. It does not reduce the number of distinct board states a check examines and refutes, which are not repeats and are why the effort of a check can exceed the size of its certificate, since only one winning child is kept where the certified side chooses (reading 42). If every examined state is kept, the retained work is the size of the distinct states examined, which can exceed the proof tree. Keeping part of it is a choice. The retained work is a store whose entries can be dropped: dropping one changes the cost, since the work is derived again if needed, and does not change the certificate, provided the certificate itself is kept. Where the certificate is a proof that one side wins, the alternatives the certified side set aside are not part of it, so dropping what is held about them costs effort only. Where a certificate is of another kind, such as that a move is best among its alternatives, they would be part of it. Which entries are worth keeping is the keep-or-gap trade applied to the working store: how often an entry is reused (item 43) against the cost of deriving it again (item 34). The evaluator can make that cost small for some entries, which is the link in item 68. What the evaluator supplies is heuristic and bears on what the check finds and its cost, not on the validity of what it finds, since a line that enters the certificate is checked against every reply. The working store belongs to the check and to the layer that owns the search (engine integration, reading 23), not to the engine, which is a black box. Whether it saves enough, and which policy decides what is dropped, is empirical. The policy for what a check retains and drops, how it is compressed, and how the evaluator makes re-derivation cheap are an optimization front of the kind item 40 leaves open, and not architecture. What the architecture needs is that the goal can be stated with the working store as a parameter. Whether the retained work counts against the window or a separate allowance is the one choice that changes the form of the stop condition (item 29), and the goal can be stated with both settings allowed. The parameter has an optional second part, the lifetime of the retained work, within one check or carried across checks. Keeping retained work beyond a check is allowed and not required, and can be dropped if it ever affects storage negatively (item 71). Stating the store as within one check only would exclude it and edges into the open item on where a record of failed attempts is held (§3), so it is left allowed (item 70).

70. **The entry, after items 80, 81 and 84 to 87.** An entry is one of three. It has resolved to a destination, and holds d and the index. Or it is not held, because it neither resolves nor lies on that bound. Whether an entry has resolved is read from whether it carries the index: an entry with a d is not therefore certified, because the entry at the outer bound has a d and has not resolved. No entry means not resolved (item 87). A certified position one ply from its condition also has a d of 1, and the index is what tells it from the boundary entry, which carries d alone. Item 9 and item 81 are compatible if what P accumulates is what resolves, so a batch in which nothing resolves can leave the priors as they were (the designer has not stated this). 72. **The three boundaries, what d denotes, and how segments are keyed.** The outer bound of a proof tree is the d-depth 1 boundary of items 52, 53 and 80. An irreversible segmented boundary is a segment of the priors (item 81). A boundary board state is the classification of a position in the main document. These are three different boundaries and are kept apart here. The segments are the irreversible segmented boundaries the program defines (`boundary_board_state_primitive_catalog.md`), and this file adds no kind of segment. A boundary is the key and its priors are the content (engine integration, reading 18), and the dependency between boundaries is one way, which gives the base case of reading 10. d is read as the number of plies from a position that the most optimal move sets require to derive the best move sets within the limit of certification, up to a condition that resolves the position: a terminal condition, or a reachable downstream dependency that already resolves it with fidelity. The condition is read as denoted explicitly in relation to the pool of reachable states that satisfy it, a possible replacement for the index into a legend file in reading 69 (a), which is not decided since the schema is open. The assignment of a position to a segment is read from start-selection §4 (Keys and segments) and the topology file, both readings: a segment is keyed by material, pawn structure and castling rights, with bishops told apart by the colour of their squares (item 83), and the irreversible moves (captures, pawn moves, loss of castling rights) are the edges between segments. The code uses that key.
75. **What the symmetry condition was, and the class of segments a transformation links.** The symmetry condition named a transformation mapping a stored state to the state it stands for, so one stored state serves several, which is compression and not a kind of certainty of its own. A transformation either keeps a position in its segment or carries it into another one. Item 86 gives the cases that carry it: a change in the positions of the pawns, and a bishop placed on a square of the other colour. Castling rights are an attribute of a segment. The transformations act on positions and also on the key of a segment: a pawn structure maps to the pawn structure of the image, and the bishop counts by colour of square map to those of the image. A segment together with the segments the transformations carry it to is a class. What is certified in one segment applies to the others of its class under the same limitation, and the priors are the same priors (item 86), so the unit of certification is read as the class of segments and not the single segment. The fold within a segment and the equivalence between segments are one mechanism. A position in either is found by putting the position and its key in a form shared by the class, or by trying the images, and an entry holds nothing about orientation. Which transformations apply depends on the key: with no pawns and no castling rights all eight board symmetries apply, with pawns only the reflection that swaps the files applies, and a castling right removes it (start-selection §3, a reading that has not been run). "Mirroring on the rank axis" in item 83 is read as that reflection, the one that preserves the direction of pawn moves, and the designer has not stated which reflection is meant. Four of the eight board symmetries change the colour of a square (the reflections that swap the files and that swap the ranks, and the two quarter turns) and four keep it, which this file derived for correction. Not stated: how the dependency of segments in start-selection §2g reads when its units are classes, which has not been checked; how the legend of a boundary stands, which the designer has put aside for now; which transformations are in force for a segment with castling rights; and how a lookup finds an entry that stands for a transformed position. ## 3. What is open

- **The query surface of the priors** (item 29): which queries the goals can make, and what each returns.
- **How a goal is expressed** (items 5 and 76): what a goal is given, how a side's goal is passed to the framework, and what the framework may not interpret.

- **The measurement of the optimality of a batch relative to a prior** (item 10): what is counted, what counts as bias into bad positions, and how it is recorded.
- **Where differential testing sits** (reading 6): inside P or as iterations of F, and how much of it the cost of re-derivation replaces (item 42).
- **Where the attempted d is set** (reading 2): in the iteration, in the goal, or elsewhere, and how the proof size is recorded with it (engine integration, §3, the measure of the space to resolve).
- **The batch format and what the batch records.** Refined by the exact check goal file: P takes a batch of board states.
- **The baseline P** (item 9): the form in which a batch becomes priors, as the simplest case.
- **How the re-derivation cost is recorded** (reading 9): the proof size, the time, or both, and at which d.
- **How the static measures of P combine into a rule for keeping or omitting** (items 14, 31, 33 and 34; readings 9, 34): including how the speed tolerance is set or left to emerge, within the balance of storage, coverage and speed (items 34 and 37).
- **How a promising position that is unresolved is recorded and prioritized** (reading 8).
- **When the limitation is raised, and by how much** (items 15 and 16; reading 10).
- **The perimeter of a proof tree** (items 49, 50, 52, 53 and 56; readings 44, 46 and 48): the designer views it as the lowest edge of a map of certification limits and leans toward storing it at d-depth 1, with the d-depth 0 states derived from it, and leaves the level open to the balance of storage, speed and coverage. Open: whether the states at which the tree ends at a certified anchor are outside it (readings 44 and 48 read them so, and item 57 does not address it); whether the certified move is held at the entries where the certified side chooses, which the derivation of the zero states needs and which sits against item 79; whether all of the edge or part of it is stored; how it relates to the interior states kept; and how a position reached at different plies or in different trees gets its d under the word minimum.
- **How "already covered" is decided for the differential scope** (items 41 and 48 to 51; reading 47): the designer states the understanding is closed and not yet fully articulated. What is stated is the perimeter of the tree and the role of batches. What is open is the rule that compares what is stored with what a check seeks, and what a check does at a perimeter state. Item 54 says the rule may be a baseline and not fixed.
- **How a goal that yields a proof tree relates to the two families of goals** (items 24, 41, 54, 65 and 72): item 41 separates the variable goals of F, which do not result in a proof tree, from the constant goal of P, which does. Item 54 says a goal could be a proof tree in itself and that F could be recursive with the properties of P. Whether that is the constant goal of P called from within F, a variable goal that yields a proof tree, or something else is not stated. It should not be excluded and can only be answered empirically (item 65). Item 72 adds that the goal of P has a general preset and can be as variable as the goals of F.
- **The check as a goal** (items 19 and 29; reading 12): unresolved and not attempted. It covers the form of a batch that is a proof tree, how the enumeration of every reply is requested in a goal, and how the proof size is counted. Item 56 states the objective as the coverage of the d-depth limit certification, which item 57 confirms is formally articulated as raising the lowest edge.
- **Whether work retained from a check counts against the window** (items 29, 42 and 67 to 71; reading 63): or against a separate working allowance, and for how long it is held. Whether sections of the working memory can be dumped, and whether the evaluator makes re-derivation cheap enough to allow it, is not known. It is an open front for optimization more than architecture (item 69).
- **How the batch is split into operations** (items 44 and 51; reading 41): one operation for the sum or one per game, how the merge takes the composition of the games into account, and how merging two sets of priors relates to merging a batch into existing priors, including the comparison of the priors of batches that resolve complementary regions.
- **The repeated failed attempts at a larger d** (item 46): how the program avoids repeating a lost cause, and where any record of a failed attempt is held, in the priors (which adds storage) or outside them as bookkeeping of the progression. To be discovered empirically and added to the protective belt. A possible link to the perimeter (reading 47) has not been stated by the designer.
- **The efficiency of the recurring check** (item 22; reading 18): how the check configuration is made inexpensive per call, since it recurs on every cycle. Not specified.
- **The stored form of the d recorded for a board state** (items 28, 52, 57 and 82; readings 21 and 70): what is recorded is given: d is a value held at a position, the certification limit of that position, counted in layers of legal plies from the anchoring points. Not specified: the layout in which it is stored, and its relation to the header of the earlier d-depth metric statement, which item 28 says does not apply. Item 82 leaves the schema of the field open, including whether the condition is denoted explicitly in place of the legend.
- **How the coverage of the priors affects the reset points** (items 25 and 26; reading 17): item 26 gives the mechanism. Open: how the shape of the coverage of the heuristic entries and of the certified entries sets how often and how far apart the anchors are, and whether existing priors make a check easier or longer.
- **The measure of the benefit at a boundary** (item 18; reading 10): the difference between the recorded proof size and the human parameter, which needs the human parameter (engine integration, §3).
- **The unit of the window of proof size** (items 20, 21 and 29): what is counted (nodes per claim, time, storage, or something else), whether it applies per claim or per iteration, and whether and how it differs from the storage window of the priors, given that item 21 describes it as a storage based limitation like Syzygy. Item 66 adds whether the window is applied to one boundary at a time, with the priors of a boundary loaded when required and replaced by those of the next, or to all the boundaries together.
- **How engine evaluations affect the priors and the exact check** (items 3, 40 and 59; reading 55): left unintegrated on purpose, because of the uncertainty about the effect and the potential to optimize it. Whether item 3 (least impactful anchor) and item 59 are about different things, as reading 55 reads them. The role of the evaluator is not exactly clear throughout (item 63).
- **Whether the optimal priors depend on the engine in the slot** (items 60, 62, 63 and 64): likely, and not a definitional influence. Open: how any dependence is held, which engine the keep-or-gap decision is made for, whether the priors are held per engine, how the depth an evaluator reaches relates to the d of a check, and whether the gap criterion of item 38 makes the stored distance depend on the engine (an integrated specification concern, expected and understood, item 65).
- **What "provably better than the native line" means** (item 61): whether it refers to the result or to the cost of resolving the gap, what proves it, given that an engine's finding is certified only once checked against every reply (calibration, reading 13), and what the native line is.
- **Whether the best result of an engine navigating a gap enters P** (item 61): how the best result within the time limit relates to a batch and to the priors, and whether the time limit is the speed tolerance of item 34.
- **Master priors and the irreversible segmented boundaries** (item 1): how the approach scales with them. Scoped to P and not a strict consideration now.
- **The entry and the index** (items 79 to 82, 85 to 89): open points are whether an entry with no d is valid, what the index is in the code, how a lookup finds an entry that stands for a transformed position, how the legend stands, how the pool across a change of scope is checked (item 89: record the pool, run a recurring check, or rely on certified markers), whether the clean up is the batch composition clean up, a function of P, or P with a goal, and whether other changes to the priors follow when the condition at an entry changes. The exact check goal file refines the index and the entry.
